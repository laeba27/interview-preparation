Part 1 — Verbal Reasoning

1. Number & Letter Series

In plain words

A series is a list of numbers or letters that follows one hidden rule. Your job is to find the rule and use it to fill the missing term, or to spot the one term that breaks the rule.

Almost every series in placement tests is built from a small set of rules: add a fixed number, multiply, add growing numbers, use squares or cubes, use primes, or run two series side by side. Letter series are the same thing in disguise: turn each letter into its position (A = 1 … Z = 26) and it becomes a number series.

Think of a staircase. If every step is the same height, it is a simple series. If the steps get taller by the same amount each time, you look at the "differences of the differences". Your job is to measure the steps, not stare at the floors.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Step 1 — always: write the differences between neighbours under the series. If they are equal, add that number. If not, look at the second differences (differences of the differences).

Growing fast? Then it is multiplication, powers, or ×a + b (for example ×2 + 1). Check the ratio of neighbours.

Jumps up and down? It is an alternating series: two rules taking turns, or two series woven together (1st, 3rd, 5th… and 2nd, 4th, 6th…).

Letters: convert with A = 1 … Z = 26. Reverse position (Z = 1 … A = 26) is 27 − position. Past Z, wrap around: subtract 26.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

EJOTY — E = 5, J = 10, O = 15, T = 20, Y = 25. From these five anchors you can reach any letter in one or two steps: R = T − 2 = 18, L = J + 2 = 12. Much faster than counting from A.

Shortcut

Opposite pairs (letters whose positions add to 27): AZ, BY, CX, DW, EV, FU, GT, HS, IR, JQ, KP, LO, MN. A memory hook: "GT road", "HS school", "IR railway", "LO ve", "MN = money".

Shortcut

Order of checks (stop at the first that works): differences → second differences → ratio → near squares/cubes → alternating → primes. Most exam series fall in the first three.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Find the next term: 3, 7, 13, 21, 31, ?

  1. Differences: 7 − 3 = 4, 13 − 7 = 6, 21 − 13 = 8, 31 − 21 = 10.
  2. The differences go up by 2 each time: 4, 6, 8, 10, so the next difference is 12.
  3. Next term = 31 + 12 = 43.

Answer: 43.

Example 2 (Easy). Find the next term: 2, 5, 10, 17, 26, ?

  1. Each term is one more than a perfect square: 1 + 1, 4 + 1, 9 + 1, 16 + 1, 25 + 1.
  2. So the rule is n² + 1. The 6th term is 36 + 1.

Answer: 37. Faster way: differences are 3, 5, 7, 9 (odd numbers), so add 11: 26 + 11 = 37. Both methods agree, which is a good check.

Example 3 (Medium). Find the next term: 5, 6, 10, 19, 35, ?

  1. Differences: 1, 4, 9, 16. These are 1², 2², 3², 4².
  2. Next difference = 5² = 25.
  3. Next term = 35 + 25 = 60.

Answer: 60.

Example 4 (Medium). Find the next term: 4, 10, 8, 14, 12, 18, ?

  1. The series goes up and down, so suspect an alternating rule.
  2. Steps: +6, −2, +6, −2, +6. The next step is −2.
  3. Next term = 18 − 2 = 16.

Answer: 16. Another view: odd places 4, 8, 12, 16 and even places 10, 14, 18 are two separate +4 series.

Example 5 (Medium). Find the next term: A, C, F, J, O, ? Also: Z, W, S, N, ?

  1. Convert: A = 1, C = 3, F = 6, J = 10, O = 15. Differences: +2, +3, +4, +5. Next is +6: 15 + 6 = 21 = U.
  2. Second series: Z = 26, W = 23, S = 19, N = 14. Differences: −3, −4, −5. Next is −6: 14 − 6 = 8 = H.

Answer: U and H. EJOTY helps here: O = 15 is an anchor, and U is one after T (20).

Example 6 (Hard). Find the wrong term: 2, 5, 11, 23, 47, 96

  1. Terms roughly double, so test "×2 + something".
  2. 2 × 2 + 1 = 5, 5 × 2 + 1 = 11, 11 × 2 + 1 = 23, 23 × 2 + 1 = 47, 47 × 2 + 1 = 95.
  3. The last term should be 95, not 96.

Answer: 96 is wrong (should be 95). Shortcut: in a ×2 + 1 series every term after the first is odd. 96 is the only even term, so it stands out at once.

Common trap

Stopping at the first rule that fits two or three terms. Always check your rule on every given term before choosing an option. Many wrong options are built to fit the first three terms only.

Common trap

Letter series that go past Z. After Z comes A again, so position 28 is B (28 − 26). Forgetting the wrap-around gives an impossible "28th letter".

2. Analogy & Odd One Out

In plain words

An analogy question gives you one pair that is linked in some way, written A : B :: C : ? (read "A is to B as C is to ?"). You find the link in the first pair and apply the same link to the second.

Odd one out (classification) gives four or five items. All but one share a property. You find the property and pick the item that does not have it.

Analogy is like copying a recipe: "tea is to kettle as rice is to cooker". You don't care about tea or rice themselves, only about the rule "what you cook it in". Odd one out is like finding the one person in a group photo not wearing the team jersey.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Say the link as a sentence. "A works in B", "A is used to B", "B is A cubed". Then read the same sentence with C. The sentence must be specific: "related to" is too loose.

Number analogy: test n², n³, n² ± 1, n³ ± 1, n × k, n + k and digit sums in that order.

Letter analogy: convert to positions. Check shift (+k), reverse order, opposite letter (27 − n).

Odd one out: the property is usually prime / square / cube / even / divisible by k for numbers, equal gaps for letter groups, and category (fruit, vegetable, tool, animal) for words.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

For number analogies, look at the size jump. 3 → 27 is a big jump, so think power (cube). 7 → 50 is roughly ×7, so think n² ± 1. 12 → 15 is a small step, so think addition.

Shortcut

For odd one out with numbers, run this quick checklist on every option: even/odd, prime, perfect square, perfect cube, divisible by 3, 7, 11. The odd one is the only option that fails (or passes) one test.

Shortcut

Divisibility by 11: the alternating sum of digits is 0 or a multiple of 11. For 572: 5 − 7 + 2 = 0, so 572 is divisible by 11. This settles many "odd number" questions in seconds.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Doctor : Hospital :: Teacher : ? (a) Book (b) Student (c) School (d) Class

  1. Make the sentence: "A doctor works in a hospital."
  2. Apply it: "A teacher works in a ___." The workplace is a school.
  3. "Student" and "Book" are linked to teachers, but not by "works in".

Answer: (c) School.

Example 2 (Easy). 3 : 27 :: 5 : ?

  1. 27 = 3 × 3 × 3 = 3³.
  2. Same rule for 5: 5³ = 125.

Answer: 125.

Example 3 (Medium). ACE : BDF :: GIK : ?

  1. A → B, C → D, E → F: each letter moves +1.
  2. G → H, I → J, K → L.

Answer: HJL.

Example 4 (Medium). 7 : 50 :: 9 : ?

  1. 50 is close to 49 = 7². So the rule is n² + 1.
  2. 9² + 1 = 81 + 1 = 82.

Answer: 82.

Example 5 (Medium). Find the odd one out: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 — and — 121, 144, 169, 196, 210

  1. First set: all are primes except 9 (= 3 × 3). Odd one: 9.
  2. Second set: 121 = 11², 144 = 12², 169 = 13², 196 = 14². 210 is not a perfect square. Odd one: 210.

Answer: 9 and 210. Notice all six numbers in the first set are odd, so "odd/even" does not separate them. The property that splits exactly one item away is "prime".

Example 6 (Hard). Find the odd one out: BDF, HJL, MOQ, RTW

  1. Convert gaps: B→D→F is +2, +2. H→J→L is +2, +2. M→O→Q is +2, +2.
  2. R (18) → T (20) → W (23) is +2, +3.

Answer: RTW.

Common trap

Reversing the direction. In A : B :: C : ? the rule goes from A to B. In "Hospital : Doctor :: School : ?" the answer is Teacher, not a building. Keep the order of the first pair.

Common trap

In odd one out, a number like 64 is both a square and a cube. Do not throw it out as "odd" just because it has an extra property. Ask which property all but one share.

EasyBird : Fly :: Fish : ? (a) Water (b) Swim (c) Gills (d) Scales

Answer: (b) Swim

The link is "how it moves". A bird flies, a fish swims. Water is where it lives, not its action.

16 = 4². So 6² = 36. (The rule "×4" would also turn 4 into 16 and give 24, but 24 is not among the options, so the intended rule is squaring. When two rules fit one pair, let the options decide.)

3. Coding–Decoding

In plain words

In coding–decoding, a word or sentence is turned into a secret code using a fixed rule. You are shown one example, you work out the rule, and then you use it to code (or decode) a new word.

The rules are almost always one of these: shift every letter by a fixed number, reverse the word, replace each letter by its opposite, turn letters into numbers, swap one name for another (substitution), or give each word of a sentence its own code word.

It is like a child's secret language with a friend. Once you catch that "every letter is moved one step forward", you can read every message they send.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Compare letter by letter. Write the word above its code and find the shift for each position. Same shift everywhere = simple shift. Shift that grows (+1, +2, +3…) = position-based shift.

Opposite letter: code = 27 − position (A↔Z, B↔Y…).

Reverse: the code is the word written backwards, sometimes with a shift on top.

Number coding: letter → its position, or a sum of positions, or position × something.

Sentence coding: the code of a word = the code common to two sentences that share only that word.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Check only the first and last letters of each option. Once you know the rule, compute just the first and last code letters and eliminate. Usually only one option survives, so you never write the whole code.

Shortcut

Same letter, same code. If a letter repeats in the word (the two O's in BOOK), a simple shift gives the same code letter twice. If the codes differ, the rule depends on position.

Shortcut

Substitution questions: first answer the question in real life ("what do we drink? water"), then look up what that word is called in the code. Answer with the code name.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). In a code, MANGO is written NBOHP. How is APPLE written?

  1. M→N, A→B, N→O, G→H, O→P. Every letter moves +1.
  2. APPLE: A→B, P→Q, P→Q, L→M, E→F.

Answer: BQQMF.

Example 2 (Easy). If TABLE is coded ELBAT, how is CHAIR coded?

  1. ELBAT is TABLE written backwards.
  2. CHAIR backwards is RIAHC.

Answer: RIAHC.

Example 3 (Medium). If GOLD is written TLOW, how is SILVER written?

  1. G (7) → T (20): 7 + 20 = 27. O (15) → L (12): 27. So each letter becomes its opposite.
  2. S (19) → 8 = H, I (9) → 18 = R, L (12) → 15 = O, V (22) → 5 = E, E (5) → 22 = V, R (18) → 9 = I.

Answer: HROEVI. Shortcut: use the opposite-pair list (HS, IR, LO, EV) instead of computing.

Example 4 (Medium). If BAD is written 214, how is FACE written?

  1. B = 2, A = 1, D = 4, written side by side.
  2. F = 6, A = 1, C = 3, E = 5.

Answer: 6135.

Example 5 (Medium). If sky is called sea, sea is called water, water is called air and air is called cloud, what do we drink?

  1. In real life we drink water.
  2. In this code, water is called "air".

Answer: air.

Example 6 (Hard). In a code language, "pit na sa" means "you are good", "na ho pit" means "they are good", and "ka sa" means "you go". What is the code for "they"?

  1. Sentences 1 and 2 share "are good" and the codes "pit na". So {are, good} = {pit, na}.
  2. Sentence 2 is "they are good" = "na ho pit". Remove pit and na: they = ho.
  3. (Also: sentences 1 and 3 share "you" and "sa", so you = sa, and go = ka.)

Answer: ho. Note that we cannot tell which of pit / na is "are" and which is "good" — exam options avoid asking that.

Example 7 (Hard). If ABC is written BDF, how is CODE written?

  1. A→B is +1, B→D is +2, C→F is +3. The shift equals the letter's place in the word.
  2. C +1 = D, O +2 = Q, D +3 = G, E +4 = I.

Answer: DQGI.

Common trap

In substitution questions, answering the real-life word. "What do we drink?" — the answer is the code name for water, not "water".

Common trap

In sentence coding, the codes are usually jumbled. Never assume the first code belongs to the first word.

4. Blood Relations

In plain words

Blood relation questions describe a family through short clues ("A is the brother of B", "C is the mother of A") and ask how two people are related. The skill is to turn the words into a small family-tree drawing, then read the answer off the drawing.

Three styles appear in tests: direct clues, "pointing to a photograph" puzzles where the speaker describes someone through a chain of relations, and coded relations where symbols like + or × stand for "brother of", "father of" and so on.

It is like following directions in a new city. "Go to my mother's only son's house" means "go to my brother's house" (or to me, if I am the only son). Break the chain from the end nearest the speaker, one link at a time.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Drawing notation: square = male, circle = female, dashed box = gender unknown, double line = married couple, horizontal bar over people = siblings, vertical line down = next generation (child).

Generations: keep grandparents on the top row, parents in the middle, children at the bottom. Two people on the same row are siblings, cousins or spouses.

Gender: never assume it. "B is the child of A" does not say whether B is a son or daughter. If an answer needs the gender and it is not given, the answer is "cannot be determined".

Pointing puzzles: start from "my" and decode the phrase from the speaker outwards. "My father's only son" = the speaker (if male) or the speaker's brother (if the speaker is female).

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Only son / only daughter: "the only son of my father" said by a man is himself. "The only daughter of my mother" said by a woman is herself. Watch the speaker's gender: a woman saying "my father's only son" means her brother.

Shortcut

Coded relations: first write a one-line key (+ = brother, − = sister, × = father, ÷ = mother), then translate the expression left to right into short clues and draw. Each symbol also tells you a gender: "A × B" means A is male.

Shortcut

Count generations. Mark each person with a level (+1 for each step up to a parent, −1 for each step down to a child). Two levels up = grandparent, one level up but not a parent = uncle/aunt, same level = sibling/cousin.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). A is B's brother. C is A's mother. D is C's father. How is D related to B?

  1. A and B are siblings, so C is the mother of both A and B.
  2. D is C's father, so D is the father of B's mother.
  3. Mother's father = grandfather.

Answer: D is B's grandfather (maternal).

Example 2 (Medium). Pointing to a photograph, a man said, "I have no brother or sister, but that man's father is my father's son." Who is in the photo?

  1. "My father's son" — the speaker has no brother, so his father's only son is the speaker himself.
  2. So "that man's father is me".
  3. The man in the photo is the speaker's son.

Answer: his son. Many students answer "himself" — but the speaker is the man's father, not the man.

Example 3 (Medium). Pointing to a woman, Meena said, "She is the daughter of my mother's only son." How is the woman related to Meena?

  1. Meena is female, so "my mother's only son" is Meena's brother.
  2. The woman is the daughter of Meena's brother.
  3. Brother's daughter = niece.

Answer: niece.

Example 4 (Medium). If A + B means A is the brother of B, A − B means A is the sister of B, A × B means A is the father of B, and A ÷ B means A is the mother of B, what does P × Q + R − S tell us about P and S?

  1. P × Q: P is the father of Q.
  2. Q + R: Q is the brother of R. So P is also R's father.
  3. R − S: R is the sister of S. So S is another child of P.

Answer: P is the father of S. We do not know whether S is male or female, and we do not need to.

  3. W is one row below Z. Y is W's father's sister, that is W's aunt.
  4. The husband of an aunt is an uncle.

  2. So "his wife is me".

Answer: she is his wife.

Common trap

Assuming gender from a name or from the question. "A is the child of B" does not make A a son. If a relation needs the gender and it is not given, look for an option like "cannot be determined".

Common trap

Decoding a long phrase from the wrong end. In "the son of the only son of my grandfather", start with "my grandfather", then "his only son" (my father), then "his son" (me or my brother).

5. Direction Sense

In plain words

Direction sense questions describe someone walking: "go 3 km north, turn right, walk 4 km". You track where they end up and answer "how far from the start?" or "in which direction?". A second type uses shadows: the sun's position in the morning or evening tells you which way someone is facing.

It is like guiding a friend on the phone: "keep walking, now turn left at the temple". If you draw each leg on paper as you hear it, you never get lost. If you try to hold it all in your head, you will.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Compass: North up, South down, East right, West left. Between them: NE, SE, SW, NW.

Turns: a right turn = 90° clockwise, a left turn = 90° anticlockwise. From North, right = East, left = West. From South, right = West, left = East.

Shortest distance (straight line from start to end): √(east–west gap² + north–south gap²) — Pythagoras.

Shadows: the sun rises in the East and sets in the West. Morning shadow falls to the West, evening shadow falls to the East. At noon (in India) the shadow is almost under you, so noon is never asked.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Net out the legs. Add all North moves and subtract South moves; add all East moves and subtract West moves. You are left with one vertical and one horizontal gap. Then use Pythagoras on just those two numbers.

Shortcut

Common Pythagorean triples (exam favourites): 3-4-5, 6-8-10, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25, 9-12-15, 12-16-20. If the two gaps match a triple, you have the answer without a square root.

Shortcut

Angle turns: take clockwise as + and anticlockwise as −, add all turns, and apply the total once. For example +90 then −135 is a net −45: from North that is North-West.

Shortcut

Shadow trick: in the morning, the shadow points West. If the shadow is on your right, your right is West, so you face South. If on your left, your left is West, so you face North. In the evening swap West for East.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Ram walks 3 km North, turns right and walks 4 km. How far is he from the start, and in which direction?

  1. Facing North, a right turn means East. So he is 3 km North and 4 km East of the start.
  2. Distance = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5 km.
  3. He is North and East of the start: North-East.

Answer: 5 km, North-East.

Example 2 (Easy). A girl walks 10 m East, turns left and walks 5 m, then turns left again and walks 10 m. Where is she now?

  1. East 10 m. Left from East = North, 5 m. Left from North = West, 10 m.
  2. East–West: +10 − 10 = 0. North–South: +5.

Answer: 5 m North of the start.

Example 3 (Medium). One morning after sunrise, Rahul and Sita stand facing each other. Rahul's shadow falls exactly to his right. Which direction is Rahul facing?

  1. Morning: the sun is in the East, so shadows fall to the West.
  2. The shadow is on Rahul's right, so his right hand points West.
  3. If your right hand points West, you are facing South.

Answer: South (and Sita, facing him, faces North).

Example 4 (Medium). A man walks 6 km South, turns left and walks 8 km, then turns left and walks 12 km. How far and in which direction is he from the start?

  1. South 6. Left from South = East, 8 km. Left from East = North, 12 km.
  2. North–South: −6 + 12 = 6 km North. East–West: 8 km East.
  3. Distance = √(6² + 8²) = √100 = 10 km (triple 6-8-10).

Answer: 10 km, North-East.

Example 5 (Medium). A person faces North. He turns 90° clockwise, then 135° anticlockwise. Which direction does he face now?

  1. Net turn = +90 − 135 = −45°, that is 45° anticlockwise.
  2. 45° anticlockwise from North is North-West.

Answer: North-West.

Example 6 (Hard). Priya walks 10 m North, turns right and walks 8 m, turns right again and walks 25 m. How far is she from the start, and in which direction?

  1. North 10. Right from North = East, 8 m. Right from East = South, 25 m.
  2. North–South: +10 − 25 = −15, so 15 m South. East–West: 8 m East.
  3. Distance = √(15² + 8²) = √(225 + 64) = √289 = 17 m (triple 8-15-17).

Answer: 17 m, South-East.

Common trap

Turning "right" as if you were still facing North. A right turn depends on the direction you are facing now. Facing South, right is West, not East.

Common trap

Adding all the legs to get the distance. "Distance from the start" means the straight line, not the total walked. In Example 6 she walked 43 m but is only 17 m away.

6. Ranking & Order

In plain words

Ranking questions give someone's position counted from one end of a row (or from the top of a class) and ask for the position from the other end, the total number of people, or how many stand between two people. Order questions give comparisons ("A is taller than B") and ask who is first, last or in the middle.

Picture a queue at a ticket counter. If you are 7th from the front and 12th from the back, then counting the people ahead of you (6), behind you (11) and you yourself (1) gives 18 people. That "don't count yourself twice" idea solves most ranking questions.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Total = left position + right position − 1 (the person is counted in both, so subtract 1).

Position from right = Total − position from left + 1 (and the same the other way).

People between A and B (both measured from the same end): |pos A − pos B| − 1. If one is given from the left and the other from the right, first convert both to "from left".

Interchange: when A and B swap places, A takes B's old position. So A's new position from left + B's position from right − 1 = Total.

Rank in class: "top" works like "left" and "bottom" like "right". Same formulas.

Total = 40, Left = 15Right40 − 15 + 1 = 26

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Convert everything to one end. Most mistakes come from mixing "from left" and "from right". Turn all positions into "from left" first, then subtract.

Shortcut

Overlap check: if A's position from the left plus B's position from the right is more than the total, their counts overlap, so B is to the left of A. Convert and subtract as usual; do not add.

Shortcut

Comparison chains: write ">" chains as you read ("C > A > B") and slot each new person into the chain. With five people, the middle one is 3rd from both ends.

Solved examples

  1. Left + right counts Ravi twice.
  2. Total = 7 + 12 − 1 = 18.

Answer: 18.

  1. Right = Total − Left + 1 = 40 − 15 + 1.

Answer: 26th. Check: 15 + 26 − 1 = 40.

  1. Convert B to "from the top": 35 − 20 + 1 = 16th.
  2. A is 10th, B is 16th. Between = 16 − 10 − 1 = 5.

Answer: 5.

Example 4 (Medium). In a row of 25 children, A is 15th from the left and B is 18th from the right. How many children are between A and B?

  1. 15 + 18 = 33 is more than 25, so the counts overlap: B is to the left of A.
  2. B from the left = 25 − 18 + 1 = 8.
  3. Between = 15 − 8 − 1 = 6.

Answer: 6.

Example 5 (Hard). In a row, A is 12th from the left and B is 18th from the right. They swap places, and now A is 20th from the left. How many people are in the row?

  1. After the swap, A stands where B stood.
  2. That spot is 20th from the left (A's new position) and 18th from the right (B's old position).
  3. Total = 20 + 18 − 1 = 37.

Answer: 37. Note that A's old position (12th) is not needed. It is there to confuse you, or for a follow-up question (B is now 12th from the left).

Example 6 (Hard). Among five friends, A is taller than B but shorter than C. D is taller than C. E is shorter than B. Who is in the middle by height?

  1. A > B and C > A gives C > A > B.
  2. D > C gives D > C > A > B.
  3. E < B gives D > C > A > B > E.
  4. The 3rd of five is A.

Answer: A.

Common trap

Forgetting the "− 1". Left + right is always one more than the total because the person is counted in both.

Common trap

"How many are between" with an unknown overlap. If the question does not give the total, there can be two answers (one with overlap, one without). Read whether it asks for the minimum, the maximum, or gives the total.

7. Alphabet & Word Tests

In plain words

Alphabet tests check how well you can move around the A–Z line: which letter is 7th to the right of the 14th letter, which word comes first in a dictionary, how many letter pairs in a word are spaced the same as in the alphabet, and which words can be made from the letters of a given word.

It is like knowing the house numbers on your street by heart. Once you know that "O" is house 15, finding "four houses to the left of O" takes a second instead of counting from the corner.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

k-th letter from the right = (27 − k)-th letter from the left.

m-th to the right of the n-th from the left = letter number n + m. To the left: n − m.

Dictionary order: compare the first letter where the words differ. If one word is the start of another (PLAN, PLANE), the shorter word comes first.

Letter pairs: a pair counts when the number of letters between them in the word equals the number between them in the alphabet. In practice: gap in word = |difference of positions|. Check both directions (forward like D→E and backward like W…R).

Forming words: each letter can be used only as many times as it appears in the given word.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Use EJOTY (5, 10, 15, 20, 25) and 27 − n for reverse positions. "10th from the right" = 27 − 10 = 17th = Q. No counting on fingers.

Shortcut

Letter pairs in one pass: write positions under the word, then for each letter look only at the letters after it. For neighbours check a difference of 1, for letters two apart a difference of 2, and so on. Stop once the gap in the word is larger than any difference possible.

Shortcut

Forming words: count the letters of the given word first (for example EXAMINATION: A2, I2, N2, E1, X1, M1, T1, O1). Then check each option only for letters it uses more often than allowed, or letters that are missing.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Arrange in dictionary order: Plan, Planet, Plane, Plain, Place. Which word comes third?

  1. All start "Pla". Next letters: n, n, n, i, c. So Place (c) comes first, then Plain (i).
  2. Plan, Plane, Planet: Plan is the start of the other two, so it comes first; Plane is the start of Planet.
  3. Order: Place, Plain, Plan, Plane, Planet.

  4. Gap 5: W–R, 23 − 18 = 5, yes.

Answer: 3 pairs (ON, DE, WR).

Example 5 (Medium). Which word cannot be formed from the letters of EXAMINATION? (a) NATION (b) ANIMATE (c) MOTION (d) TAXI

  2. MOTION needs two O's, but EXAMINATION has only one O.
  3. NATION (N2, A, T, I, O), ANIMATE (A2, N, I, M, T, E) and TAXI all fit.

8. Linear Seating Arrangement

In plain words

A group of people sit in a straight line. You get a list of clues such as "A sits second to the right of B" or "C sits at one end". Your job is to place everyone in the right seat, then answer 4–6 questions about the final row.

The only real difficulty is left and right. Left and right always belong to the person, not to you. When a person faces you, their right is on your left.

Think of a cinema row. If you sit in the row and look at the screen, your left is the same as the person next to you. But the usher standing at the screen and looking back at the row sees everything flipped. Seating questions are always solved "from the chair", never "from the screen".

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Number the seats 1, 2, 3 … n from the page left. Draw everyone as if you look at the row from behind (from the south).
  • Facing north: right = next seat number up (+1), left = next seat number down (−1).
  • Facing south: right = −1, left = +1 (the directions flip on paper).
  • "X is k-th to the right of Y" means X is k seats away from Y, on Y's right side. The direction always belongs to the person named after "of".
  • "Only k people sit between X and Y" means their seat numbers differ by k + 1.
  • "Immediate right / left" means next seat (k = 1). "Second to the right" means one person is skipped.
  • Middle seat in a row of n (n odd) = seat (n + 1) / 2. For 7 people it is seat 4.
  • "X is at an extreme end" means seat 1 or seat n. "X is not at an end" rules out both.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Order of attack: (1) definite clues, (2) clues that link to a person already placed, (3) gap clues with 2 cases, (4) negative clues to delete cases. Never start with a "not" clue.

Shortcut

Draw cases side by side. When a clue gives two positions, draw two short rows (Case 1, Case 2). Most cases die within one or two clues, and you avoid rubbing out.

Shortcut

For south-facing rows, write an arrow under each seat before you start. Then read every clue with "+1 or −1?" instead of thinking about left and right again.

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy). Five friends P, Q, R, S and T sit in a row facing north. R sits in the middle. P sits to the immediate left of R. T sits at the right end. Q is not a neighbour of T. Who sits at the left end?

  1. Middle of 5 seats = seat 3, so R = 3.
  2. Immediate left of R (facing north) = seat 2, so P = 2.
  3. Right end = seat 5, so T = 5. Seats 1 and 4 are left for Q and S.
  4. Seat 4 is next to T. Q is not next to T, so Q = 1 and S = 4.

Row: Q P R S T. Answer: Q.

Example 2 (full set). Seven people A, B, C, D, E, F and G sit in a row facing north.

  • D sits exactly in the middle of the row.
  • Only two people sit between D and E.
  • C sits to the immediate left of E.
  • A sits third to the left of C.
  • B does not sit next to A.
  • F sits to the immediate right of B.

Questions: (i) Who sits exactly between A and G? (ii) How many people sit between B and C? (iii) Who sits second to the right of F? (iv) Who sits at the right end?

  1. Definite clue first. Middle of 7 = seat 4, so D = 4.
  2. Gap clue. Two people between D and E means the seats differ by 3. E = 4 − 3 = 1 or E = 4 + 3 = 7. Two cases.
  3. Kill a case. C must be on E's immediate left (seat E − 1). If E = 1, there is no seat 0, so Case 1 dies. E = 7 and C = 6.
  4. Relative clue. A is third to the left of C: 6 − 3 = 3, so A = 3.
  5. Negative clue. Free seats are 1, 2 and 5. B is not next to A (seats 2 and 4), so B = 1 or 5. F must be on B's immediate right. If B = 5, F = 6, but C is there. So B = 1, F = 2, and G takes the last seat, 5.

Final row: B F A D G C E. Answers: (i) seats 3 and 5 have seat 4 between them: D. (ii) B = 1, C = 6, so 6 − 1 − 1 = 4 people. (iii) F = 2, two seats to the right = seat 4: D. (iv) E.

Faster way: the moment you read "C sits to the immediate left of E" together with "E is 3 seats from the middle", you know E cannot be at the left end. Look for such "no room" eliminations first.

Example 3 (facing south). Five people J, K, L, M and N sit in a row facing south. M sits in the middle. K sits second to the left of M. N sits to the immediate right of K. J sits at the extreme right end of the row. Who sits to the immediate left of L?

  1. Facing south: left = +1 on paper, right = −1.
  2. M = 3. K is second to the left of M: 3 + 2 = 5, so K = 5.
  3. N is immediately right of K: 5 − 1 = 4, so N = 4.
  4. The extreme right end of south-facing people is seat 1 (the page left). So J = 1, and L = 2.
  5. Immediate left of L = seat 2 + 1 = 3 = M.

Row on paper: J L M N K. Answer: M.

Example 4 (some face north, some face south). Six people U, V, W, X, Y and Z sit in a row. Some face north and some face south.

  • X sits at an extreme end and faces south.
  • W sits second to the right of X, and W faces the same direction as X.
  • Z sits to the immediate right of X.
  • U sits at an extreme end.
  • Y sits to the immediate right of W. W sits to the immediate left of Y.
  • The immediate neighbours of Y face opposite directions.
  • U faces the same direction as V. Z faces the direction opposite to X.

Question: Who sits second to the left of Z, and how many people face north?

  1. X faces south, so X's right is −1. "W is second to the right of X" needs two seats on the −1 side, so X cannot be seat 1. X = 6, W = 4, Z = 5.
  2. W faces south (same as X). W's right is −1, so Y = 3.
  3. "W is to the immediate left of Y": W is at 4 = Y + 1, so Y's left is +1. That means Y faces south.
  4. U is at an end; seat 6 is taken, so U = 1 and V = 2.
  5. Y's neighbours are V (seat 2) and W (south). They face opposite ways, so V faces north. U faces like V: north. Z faces opposite to X: north.

Z faces north and sits in seat 5, so its left is −1: second to the left = seat 3 = Y. North-facing people: U, V, Z = 3.

Example 5 (two parallel rows). P, Q, R sit in Row 1 facing south. A, B, C sit in Row 2 facing north, so each person faces someone in the other row. Q sits in the middle of Row 1. A faces the person to the immediate right of Q. R sits to the immediate left of Q. C does not face R. Who faces P, and who sits to the immediate right of C?

  1. Row 1 faces south, so right = −1 on paper. Q = 2, immediate right of Q = seat 1. Immediate left of Q = seat 3 = R, so P = 1.
  2. A faces seat 1, so A = 1 in Row 2.
  3. C does not face R (seat 3), so C = 2 and B = 3.
  4. A faces P. C faces north, so C's right is +1: seat 3 = B.

Answer: A faces P; B sits to the immediate right of C.

Common trap

"A is to the left of B" does not mean "immediately left". It only says A is somewhere on B's left side. Only "immediate left" fixes the next seat.

Common trap

In "X is second to the right of Y", the direction belongs to Y. If Y faces south, move −2 on paper even if X faces north.

Common trap

"Only two people sit between" means a difference of 3 seats, not 2. Count the gap people, then add 1.

9. Circular Seating Arrangement

In plain words

Now the people sit around a round table. There are no ends, so you cannot start from "the left end". You start by putting any one fixed person at the top of your circle and build around them.

People can face the centre (the usual case), face outside, or a mix of both. Left and right again belong to the person, and they flip when a person faces outside.

Picture friends around a campfire, all looking at the fire. If each one passes a marshmallow to their left, the marshmallow travels clockwise when seen from above. If they all turn around to look at the stars, "pass left" now sends it anticlockwise.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Draw the circle as seen from above. Number seats clockwise from the top: 0, 1, 2 … n − 1.
  • Facing the centre: LEFT = clockwise, RIGHT = anticlockwise.
  • Facing outside: LEFT = anticlockwise, RIGHT = clockwise.
  • Opposite seat (n even) = seat + n/2. With 8 people, seat 0 faces seat 4.
  • k-th to the left and (n − k)-th to the right point to the same seat. With 8 people, 3rd to the left = 5th to the right.
  • Between two people there are two gaps (one each way round). "Only k people between A and B" can be counted either way unless the clue says clockwise/left/right.
  • If A is x-th to the left and also y-th to the right of B, then n = x + y.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Fix the most-mentioned person at the top. In a circle only the relative order matters, so putting that person at seat 0 costs nothing and removes all rotations.

Shortcut

Opposite pairs first. "A sits opposite B" is gold: it fixes two seats at once. With 8 seats, an opposite clue plus one "second to the left" clue usually fixes three people.

Shortcut

Mixed facing: draw a small arrow on each seat (in or out) as soon as you learn it. Then "left" is just: arrow in = clockwise, arrow out = anticlockwise.

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy). Six friends A, B, C, D, E and F sit around a round table facing the centre. A sits opposite D. B sits to the immediate left of A. C sits second to the right of B. E is not an immediate neighbour of C. Who sits opposite E?

  1. Put A at seat 0. Opposite = 0 + 3 = seat 3, so D = 3.
  2. Facing the centre, left = clockwise (+1). B = seat 1.
  3. Right = anticlockwise (−1). C = 1 − 2 = −1, which is seat 5.
  4. Seats 2 and 4 are left. Seat 4 is next to C (seat 5), so E = 2 and F = 4.
  5. Opposite E (seat 2) = seat 5 = C.

Clockwise from A: A B E D F C. Answer: C.

Example 2 (full set). Eight people P, Q, R, S, T, U, V and W sit around a circular table facing the centre.

  • P and T sit opposite each other.
  • Q sits second to the left of P.
  • R is an immediate neighbour of T, but R is not an immediate neighbour of Q.
  • V sits to the immediate right of P.
  • U sits between Q and T.
  • S is not an immediate neighbour of P.

Questions: (i) Who sits opposite Q? (ii) Who sits third to the left of V? (iii) Counting clockwise from W, how many people sit between W and R? (iv) Who sits between S and P?

  1. Anchor. P = seat 0, T = seat 4 (opposite).
  2. Left = clockwise. Q = 0 + 2 = seat 2.
  3. Right = anticlockwise. V = 0 − 1 = seat 7.
  4. Two cases for R. Neighbours of T are seats 3 and 5. Seat 3 is next to Q (seat 2), so R = 5.
  5. U sits between Q (2) and T (4): U = 3.
  6. Seats 1 and 6 are left for S and W. Seat 1 is next to P, so S = 6 and W = 1.

Answers: (i) Q = 2, opposite = 6: S. (ii) V = 7, left = clockwise, 7 + 3 = 10 → seat 2: Q. (iii) W = 1, R = 5, clockwise seats 2, 3, 4 lie between: 3 people. (iv) S = 6 and P = 0 (8): the seat between is 7: V.

Example 3 (facing outside). G, H, I, J, K and L sit around a circle facing outside. H sits second to the left of G. J sits opposite H. K sits to the immediate right of J. L is not an immediate neighbour of G. Who sits to the immediate left of I?

  1. Facing outside: left = anticlockwise (−1), right = clockwise (+1).
  2. G = 0. H = 0 − 2 = seat 4. J opposite H = seat 1.
  3. K = J + 1 = seat 2.
  4. Seats 3 and 5 are left. Seat 5 is next to G, so L = 3 and I = 5.
  5. Immediate left of I = 5 − 1 = seat 4 = H.

  1. U = 0, faces in. X = 3 (opposite), faces out.
  2. V is on U's left; U faces in, so left = clockwise: V = 1. V faces out.
  3. W is second to V's left; V faces out, so left = anticlockwise: W = 1 − 2 = seat 5. W faces out (same as V).
  4. Y is on X's left; X faces out: Y = 3 − 1 = seat 2. Z takes seat 4.
  5. Facing the centre so far: U, Z. Facing out: V, W, X. Three must face in, so Y faces the centre.
  6. Z faces in, so right = anticlockwise: 4 − 1 = seat 3 = X.

Answer: X sits to the immediate right of Z; Y faces the centre.

Example 5 (how many people?). In a circle of people facing the centre, M is 4th to the left of N and also 7th to the right of N. How many people sit in the circle?

Going left 4 seats and going right 7 seats reach the same person, so together they cover the full circle once: n = 4 + 7 = 11.

Common trap

Using linear habits in a circle. In a row, "left" is always the page left. In a circle facing the centre, "left" is clockwise, which at the bottom of your drawing points to the page left but at the top points to the page right. Always think clockwise/anticlockwise, not page left/right.

Common trap

"Only two people sit between A and B" in an 8-seat circle: the seats differ by 3 one way round, which is 5 the other way round. Do not count both sides.

10. Puzzles: Floors, Boxes & Scheduling

In plain words

A puzzle gives you a set of slots (floors of a building, boxes in a stack, days of a week, months of a year) and a set of people or things to put in them. Sometimes each person also has a second detail, like a fruit, a colour or a city. The clues link them. You place everyone, then answer questions.

These are the same skill as seating, just drawn vertically (floors, boxes) or as a timeline (days, months). The tool that makes them easy is a table.

It is like filling a hotel register. Rooms are fixed (floor 1 to 6). Guests arrive with notes like "put me just above my friend" or "not on the top floor". You pencil in the certain ones first, try the "maybe" ones in two columns, and cross out whatever breaks a rule.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Floors: floor 1 is the bottom (ground floor if stated), the top floor is n. "Above" means a higher number; "immediately above" means +1.
  • Boxes: box 1 is at the bottom of the stack. "Kept on" or "immediately above" = +1.
  • "k people / boxes between X and Y" means their positions differ by k + 1.
  • Days: Monday = 1 … Sunday = 7 (or the week given). "Before" = smaller number, "after" = larger.
  • Months: write Jan–Dec as 1–12 and note the days: 30 days = Apr, Jun, Sep, Nov; 28/29 = Feb; all others 31.
  • "X lives on an odd floor" / "an even-numbered floor" cuts the options in half.
  • Table-grid method: one row per slot (or per person), one column per detail. Tick what is certain, cross what is impossible.

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy floor). Five people A, B, C, D and E live on floors 1 to 5 of a building (1 = bottom). B lives on the lowest floor. Only one person lives between B and C. D lives immediately above A. Who lives on the floor just below C?

  1. B = 1. One person between B and C means C = 3.
  2. D = A + 1. Free floors are 2, 4, 5. The only pair of consecutive free floors is 4 and 5, so A = 4, D = 5.
  3. E takes floor 2, which is just below C.

Answer: E.

Example 2 (floors + a second detail, with cases). Six people A–F live on floors 1 to 6, one on each floor. Each likes a different fruit: Apple, Banana, Cherry, Grape, Mango, Orange.

  • Only two people live between the Apple-lover and B.
  • C lives immediately above E. F lives immediately above C.
  • E likes Orange. C likes Banana.
  • D lives on the top floor and does not like Grape.
  • The Grape-lover lives immediately below D.
  • B does not like Mango.

Questions: (i) Who likes Grape? (ii) Who likes Apple? (iii) What does B like?

  1. Definite: D = 6. Grape = floor 5.
  2. Glue block: F-C-E sit on three consecutive floors (E lowest) among floors 1–5. Cases: E-C-F on 1-2-3, 2-3-4, or 3-4-5.
  3. Case 1 (E=1, C=2, F=3): A and B are on 4 and 5. Apple must be at floor 1 (E, Orange) or 2 (C, Banana) or beyond the building. Fails.
  4. Case 3 (E=3, C=4, F=5): A and B are on 1 and 2. The Apple-lover is 3 floors from B, so at floor 4 or 5. Floor 4 is C (Banana), floor 5 is Grape. Fails.
  5. Case 2 (E=2, C=3, F=4): A and B are on 1 and 5. If B = 5, Apple would be at floor 2 (E, Orange): fails. So B = 1, A = 5, and Apple is at floor 4 = F.
  6. A is on floor 5, so A likes Grape. Cherry and Mango remain for B and D; B does not like Mango, so B = Cherry, D = Mango.

11. Syllogisms

In plain words

You get two or three statements ("All dogs are animals", "Some animals are pets") and two conclusions. You must say which conclusions are definitely true if the statements are true, even when the statements sound silly in real life.

The safest method is to draw Venn diagrams: one circle per group. A conclusion follows only if it is true in every possible drawing.

It is like a lawyer's argument. The judge only accepts what the evidence forces. "Some students are athletes" does not prove "Some athletes are not students", however likely it sounds. If you can draw even one picture where a conclusion is false, the conclusion does not follow.

Key formulas & rules

StatementWhat it forcesWhat follows by itselfWhat does NOT follow
All A are BA circle inside BSome A are B; Some B are AAll B are A
Some A are BA and B overlapSome B are AAll A are B; Some A are not B
No A is BA and B apartNo B is A; Some A are not B; Some B are not A—

Key formulas

Standard conclusion table (two statements, the middle term is B):

  • All + All = All (All A are B, All B are C → All A are C)
  • All + No = No (All A are B, No B is C → No A is C)
  • Some + All = Some (Some A are B, All B are C → Some A are C)
  • Some + No = Some not (Some A are B, No B is C → Some A are not C)
  • No + All = Some not, reversed (No A is B, All B are C → Some C are not A)
  • All + Some, Some + Some, No + No = no definite conclusion between A and C

A possibility conclusion ("All A being C is a possibility") follows if you can draw at least one picture where it is true without breaking any statement.

Either–or: when neither conclusion follows but they are about the same two groups and one is always true (pairs Some / No, or All / Some not), the answer is "Either I or II follows".

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Try to break it. For each conclusion ask: "Can I redraw the circles so the statements stay true but this conclusion is false?" If yes, it does not follow. This single test replaces most rules.

Shortcut

Either–or check in 5 seconds: both conclusions failed, same two terms, and the pair is one of: Some + No, All + Some not. Then answer "Either". Otherwise "Neither".

Shortcut

Possibility questions: a possibility fails only if some statement directly forbids it. "No A is B" forbids every possibility of A and B meeting. "Some A are B" forbids "No A is B".

Solved examples

Example 1. Statements: All dogs are animals. All animals are living things. Conclusions: I. All dogs are living things. II. Some living things are dogs.

  1. Dogs sit inside animals, which sit inside living things.
  2. I: every dog is inside the big circle. True in every picture.
  3. II: dogs exist and are inside living things, so some living things are dogs.

Answer: Both I and II follow.

Example 2. Statements: Some pens are books. All books are bags. Conclusions: I. Some pens are bags. II. All bags are pens.

  1. Some + All = Some: the pens that are books are also bags. I follows.
  2. II: draw bags much bigger than pens. Nothing forbids it. II fails.

Answer: Only I follows.

Example 3. Statements: No cat is a dog. All dogs are pets. Conclusions: I. No pet is a cat. II. Some pets are not cats.

  1. Dogs are inside pets. Cats are away from dogs.
  2. I: the cats circle can still overlap pets outside the dogs region. Then some pets are cats, so I fails.
  3. II: the dogs region of pets never contains a cat, so some pets (the dogs) are not cats. II follows.

Answer: Only II follows. Shortcut: No + All = "Some C are not A" (Some pets are not cats).

Example 4 (either–or). Statements: Some A are B. Some B are C. Conclusions: I. Some A are C. II. No A is C.

  1. Some + Some gives no definite link between A and C, so I alone fails and II alone fails.
  2. Same two terms (A, C), and the pair is Some / No: one of them must always be true.

Answer: Either I or II follows.

Example 5 (possibility). Statements: All rings are gold. No gold is silver. Conclusions: I. No ring is silver. II. All gold being rings is a possibility.

  1. All + No = No: rings are inside gold, gold is away from silver, so no ring is silver. I follows.
  2. II: can the gold circle be exactly the same as the rings circle? Yes, nothing forbids it. II follows as a possibility.

Answer: Both I and II follow.

Example 6 (three statements). Statements: Some A are B. All B are C. No C is D. Conclusions: I. Some A are not D. II. Some C are A.

  1. Some A are B + All B are C → Some A are C (Some + All = Some).
  2. Those A that are C cannot be D (No C is D). So Some A are not D. I follows.
  3. Some A are C reversed = Some C are A. II follows.

Answer: Both I and II follow.

Common trap

Using real-world knowledge. "All cats are birds. All birds fly. → All cats fly" follows in a syllogism. Judge only by the statements.

Common trap

"All A are B" does not mean "All B are A". It only lets you say "Some B are A".

Common trap

Saying "Either" when the two conclusions are about different pairs (for example "Some A are C" and "No B is C"). Either–or needs the same two terms.

12. Statements: Conclusions & Assumptions

In plain words

You read a short statement (a notice, an advert, a piece of news, some advice) and then judge two sentences.

  • A conclusion is something that must follow from the statement. You may not add outside facts or guesses.
  • An assumption is something the speaker quietly believed before speaking. It is not written, but without it the statement would make no sense.

If a friend says "Carry an umbrella today", the unspoken belief (assumption) is "it may rain" and "an umbrella will keep you dry". A conclusion from the same sentence would be "My friend thinks I might get wet". The assumption sits behind the words; the conclusion comes out of them.

Key formulas & rules

ConclusionAssumption
Question it answersWhat must be true after reading this?What did the speaker take for granted?
TestIs it forced by the words alone?Negation test: deny it; if the statement becomes pointless, it is an assumption
Valid whenIt restates, sums up or directly followsIt is needed for the speaker's aim, advice or plan to make sense
Invalid whenIt adds new facts, predicts too far, or uses extreme words not in the statementIt is too strong ("only", "all", "best"), or about something the statement does not need
Key formulas
  • Extreme words such as only, all, always, never, best, definitely, must usually make a conclusion or assumption invalid, unless the statement itself uses them.
  • Adverts assume: people read/see ads, ads influence buyers, the product has the quality claimed.
  • Advice / appeal / order assumes: people will (at least partly) follow it, and following it helps.
  • A plan or decision assumes: it is possible to carry out and will give the desired result.
  • Either–or applies to conclusions when the statement itself offers two exclusive choices and the two conclusions are those choices.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

For conclusions, stay inside the box. Underline the facts in the statement. A conclusion that needs even one extra fact ("because the teachers were bad", "prices will fall next year") is out.

Shortcut

For assumptions, ask "why would anyone say this?" The answer is usually the assumption. Then do the negation test to confirm.

Shortcut

Comparisons are traps. "X is good" never assumes "Y is bad". Adverts for one brand do not assume other brands fail.

Solved examples

Example 1 (conclusion). Statement: Of all the students who took the test, only 40% passed. The rest must repeat the course. Conclusions: I. 60% of the students will repeat the course. II. The test was very hard.

  1. I: 100% − 40% = 60% failed, and "the rest must repeat". Directly follows.
  2. II: a low pass rate could be due to poor preparation, strict marking, anything. The statement does not say why. Does not follow.

Answer: Only I follows.

Example 2 (conclusion). Statement: Morning walks are good for health. Conclusions: I. All healthy people take morning walks. II. Evening walks are harmful.

  1. I: "good for health" does not mean it is the only way to be healthy. Extreme word "all". Fails.
  2. II: nothing is said about evening walks. Comparison trap. Fails.

Answer: Neither follows.

Example 3 (assumption). Statement: "Use Brand X toothpaste for whiter teeth" — an advertisement. Assumptions: I. People want whiter teeth. II. Other toothpastes do not whiten teeth.

  1. I: negate it: "people do not care about white teeth". Then the advert is pointless. So I is assumed.
  2. II: the advert praises X; it does not need other brands to fail. Comparison trap.

Answer: Only I is implicit.

Example 4 (assumption). Statement: The school has asked parents to send their children in warm clothes from next week. Assumptions: I. The weather is expected to turn cold next week. II. Most parents will follow the school's request.

  1. I: why ask for warm clothes? Because cold is expected. Negate it and the request makes no sense. Implicit.
  2. II: any request assumes it will be followed; otherwise the school would not bother. Implicit.

Answer: Both are implicit.

Example 5 (either–or conclusion). Statement: The committee will either approve the new bridge this month or drop the project completely. Conclusions: I. The committee will approve the new bridge this month. II. The committee will drop the bridge project.

  1. Neither is certain alone.
  2. The statement gives exactly these two outcomes, and one of them must happen.

Answer: Either I or II follows.

Common trap

Choosing a conclusion because it is true in real life. "Smoking is injurious to health" is a fact, but it is not a valid conclusion unless the statement leads to it.

Common trap

Treating a possible reason as an assumption. "Sales fell, so the shop is cutting prices" assumes lower prices can raise sales. It does not assume the competitor's prices are lower.

13. Cause & Effect, Course of Action

In plain words

This section covers three close cousins that test practical judgement:

  • Cause & effect: two events are given. Decide whether one caused the other, or whether both came from something else.
  • Course of action: a problem is given with suggested actions. Decide which actions are sensible, practical and actually fix (or reduce) the problem.
  • Strong / weak arguments: a yes/no question with arguments. Decide which arguments are serious and relevant.

A doctor does all three every day. "Fever and a cough" may share one cause (an infection). The course of action is to give medicine, not to "ban winter". And a patient's argument "I should skip the medicine because my neighbour did" is weak, however confident it sounds.

Key formulas & rules

OptionMeaningClue in the wording
(a) I is the cause, II is the effectI happened first and led to IIII is a reaction to I
(b) II is the cause, I is the effectII happened first and led to II is a reaction to II
(c) Both are independent causesEach causes something else; no linkRare; two unrelated reasons
(d) Both are effects of independent causesTwo unrelated resultsDifferent subjects, no shared trigger
(e) Both are effects of some common causeOne hidden event caused bothSame place and time, neither explains the other
Key formulas
  • Course of action follows if it is practical, legal, within the authority's power, and reduces or solves the problem. It may be a long-term or short-term step.
  • It does not follow if it is extreme (ban, shut down, punish everyone), impossible, or does not touch the actual problem.
  • A strong argument is relevant to the question, based on a real and important fact, and gives a clear reason.
  • A weak argument is an opinion without reason, an unproved comparison ("other countries do it"), a matter of taste, or too extreme / too trivial.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Cause & effect in 3 questions: (1) Which happened first? (2) Can the first one explain the second in one simple step? (3) If neither explains the other but they sit in the same place and time, look for a common cause (a storm, a strike, a policy).

Shortcut

Course of action: read it as the officer in charge. "Would I actually do this tomorrow, and would it help?" If you would be laughed at or sued, it does not follow.

Shortcut

Strong arguments often mention safety, health, money, jobs, education, law or large numbers of people. Weak ones often start with "all", "only", "everybody knows", or compare with another country.

Solved examples

Example 1 (cause & effect). I. The prices of vegetables in the city have risen sharply this month. II. Heavy rain has destroyed crops in the nearby farming region.

  1. Crops destroyed comes first (it reduces supply).
  2. Less supply leads straight to higher prices.

Answer: (b) II is the cause and I is the effect.

Example 2 (cause & effect). I. Schools in the district were closed for the day. II. Several trains to the district were cancelled.

  1. Closing schools does not cancel trains, and cancelled trains do not close schools.
  2. Both fit a single event such as a flood or a heavy snowfall in the district.

Answer: (e) Both are effects of some common cause.

Example 3 (cause & effect). I. The price of petrol has gone up this week. II. The sale of umbrellas has gone up this week.

Two results with no believable shared trigger (fuel prices and rain are unrelated). Answer: (d) Both are effects of independent causes.

Example 4 (course of action). Statement: Many students of a college failed their final exams, and most of them had very poor attendance. Courses of action: I. The college should set a minimum attendance rule for sitting the exam. II. The college should be shut down.

  1. I is practical, within the college's power, and addresses the link between attendance and failure. Follows.
  2. II is extreme and punishes everyone, including students who passed. Does not follow.

Answer: Only I follows.

Example 5 (strong / weak argument). Should mobile phones be banned inside classrooms? I. Yes, phones distract students during lessons and lower attention. II. No, students in many rich countries carry phones to class.

  1. I gives a real, relevant reason tied to learning. Strong.
  2. II copies another country's practice without showing it helps learning. Weak.

Answer: Only argument I is strong.

Common trap

"Happened after" is not "caused by". Two events on the same day are not linked unless one can explain the other in one clear step.

Common trap

Rejecting a course of action because it does not solve the problem completely. A step that reduces the problem or helps the victims still follows.

14. Data Sufficiency

In plain words

A question is followed by two statements, I and II. You do not have to find the answer. You only decide whether the statements give enough information to find one single answer.

"Enough" means exactly one answer. For yes/no questions, a definite "yes" or a definite "no" both count as enough.

A friend asks "Can you reach my house?" Statement I: "It is on MG Road." Statement II: "It is house number 42." Alone, neither is enough (MG Road is long; many streets have a number 42). Together, you can walk straight to the door. You never had to actually walk there to know that.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

The standard five options (wording varies slightly between tests):

  • (a) Statement I alone is sufficient, but II alone is not.
  • (b) Statement II alone is sufficient, but I alone is not.
  • (c) Each statement alone is sufficient.
  • (d) Both statements together are needed; neither alone is enough.
  • (e) Both statements together are still not sufficient.

Always test I alone, then II alone, and only then combine. Never use information from I while judging II alone.

Stop solving early. The moment you see that a statement pins down one answer, mark it sufficient and move on. Actually computing the value wastes time.

Shortcut

15. Inequalities

In plain words

You get a chain of comparisons like A > B ≥ C = D < E and must decide which conclusions (such as "A > D") are definitely true. Sometimes the signs are hidden behind codes like "@" or "#", and you first decode them.

The whole topic rests on one idea: you can walk from one letter to another only if every sign on the way points the same direction.

Think of heights of people standing on steps. If Asha is taller than Bina, and Bina is at least as tall as Charu, Asha is surely taller than Charu. But if Asha is taller than Bina and Charu is also taller than Bina, you cannot say who is taller between Asha and Charu: both just "beat Bina".

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Same direction only: between two letters, if the signs are all "> / ≥ / =" (or all "< / ≤ / ="), a relation exists. If both ">" and "<" types appear, there is no relation.
  • Strongest sign wins: if any sign on the path is strict (> or <), the result is strict. If all are "≥" or "=", the result is "≥". If all are "=", the result is "=".
  • A > B ≥ C gives A > C. A ≥ B ≥ C gives A ≥ C. A ≥ B = C gives A ≥ C. A = B = C gives A = C.
  • Either–or rule: if neither conclusion follows, both are about the same two letters, and together they cover every case without overlap (for example > with ≤, or < with ≥; or > with = when the chain already gives ≥), then "Either I or II follows".
  • Reading backwards is fine: A ≥ B means B ≤ A.

Shortcut

Combine statements into one line by joining at the common letter: "J ≥ K, K > L, L = M" becomes J ≥ K > L = M. Then every question is one walk along the line.

Shortcut

Shortcut

Solved examples

Example 1. Statement: A > B ≥ C = D < E. Conclusions: I. A > D. II. E > B.

  1. I: A > B ≥ C = D, all signs point one way, one is strict. A > D is true.
  2. II: from B to E the path is B ≥ C = D < E. The "<" points the other way. No relation between E and B.

Answer: Only I follows.

Example 2 (either–or). Statement: P ≥ Q = R > S ≤ T. Conclusions: I. P > R. II. P = R.

  1. P ≥ Q = R gives P ≥ R. So P > R is not certain, and P = R is not certain.
  2. Same letters, and ">" plus "=" together make exactly "≥", which is true.

Answer: Either I or II follows.

Example 3 (combining statements). Statements: J ≥ K, K > L, L = M, M ≤ N. Conclusions: I. J > M. II. N ≥ L.

  1. Join: J ≥ K > L = M ≤ N.
  2. I: J ≥ K > L = M, same direction, one strict: J > M. True.
  3. II: L = M ≤ N, read backwards: N ≥ M = L, so N ≥ L. True.

Answer: Both follow.

Example 4 (coded). "A @ B" means A ≥ B, "A # B" means A > B, "A $ B" means A = B, "A % B" means A < B, "A & B" means A ≤ B. Statements: F # G, G $ H, H & K. Conclusions: I. F # H. II. K % G.

  1. Decode: F > G, G = H, H ≤ K. Join: F > G = H ≤ K.
  2. I decodes to F > H: F > G = H. True.
  3. II decodes to K < G: but K ≥ H = G, so K < G is false.

Answer: Only I follows.

Example 5 (no relation). Statement: A < B ≤ C > D. Conclusions: I. A < D. II. A > D.

  1. A < B ≤ C and then C > D: the path from A to D changes direction at C. No relation.
  2. Same letters, but I and II leave out "A = D", so they do not cover every case. Not either–or.

Answer: Neither follows. If II had been "A ≥ D", the pair would cover all cases and the answer would be "Either I or II".

Common trap

Jumping over an opposite sign. In A > B < C, both A and C are bigger than B, but nothing links A and C.

Common trap

Marking "A ≥ C" as true when the chain gives A > C. It is true: if A > C then A ≥ C also holds. But "A = C" would be false.

16. Clocks

In plain words

A clock is a circular race between two runners on a 360° track. The minute hand is the fast runner, the hour hand is the slow one. Almost every clock question asks one of three things: how far apart are they (the angle), when will they be a given distance apart (together, opposite, at right angles), or how wrong is a faulty clock (gaining or losing).

Once you know the speed of each hand, every question becomes a simple relative-speed problem, exactly like two people running on a circular track.

Think of two friends jogging round a park. One laps it every hour, the other every 12 hours. The fast one catches the slow one again and again, but because the slow one has also moved on a little, each catch-up takes slightly more than an hour: 65 5/11 minutes.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Minute hand speed = 6° per minute (360° in 60 min). Hour hand speed = 0.5° per minute (30° in 60 min).

Relative speed = 6 − 0.5 = 5.5° per minute (the minute hand gains 11/2 degrees on the hour hand every minute).

Angle between the hands at H hours M minutes: θ = |30H − 5.5M|. If the result is more than 180°, the smaller angle is 360° − θ.

Time after H o'clock when the hands make angle θ: M = (2/11)(30H ± θ) minutes. Take the value that lies between 0 and 60.

Hands coincide every 65 5/11 minutes (= 720/11) of correct time, not every 60.

Shortcut

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy). Find the angle between the hands at 3:40 and at 7:20.

  1. 3:40 → θ = |30 × 3 − 5.5 × 40| = |90 − 220| = 130°. It is under 180°, so that is the answer.
  2. 7:20 → θ = |30 × 7 − 5.5 × 20| = |210 − 110| = 100°.

Answer: 130° and 100°. Why it works: from 12, the hour hand at 3:40 has moved 3 × 30 + 40 × 0.5 = 110°, and the minute hand 40 × 6 = 240°. The gap is 130°, the same as the formula.

Example 2 (easy). At what time between 4 and 5 o'clock are the hands together?

  1. At 4:00 the hour hand is 120° ahead of the minute hand (4 × 30).
  2. The minute hand gains 5.5° every minute, so it needs 120 ÷ 5.5 = 240/11 minutes to catch up.
  3. 240/11 = 21 9/11 minutes.

Answer: 21 9/11 minutes past 4. Shortcut: 60H/11 = 60 × 4 / 11 = 21 9/11.

Example 3 (medium). At what time between 2 and 3 are the hands at right angles?

  1. Use M = (2/11)(30H ± 90) with H = 2, so 30H = 60.
  2. With the plus sign: (2/11)(150) = 300/11 = 27 3/11 minutes. This lies between 0 and 60, so it is valid.
  3. With the minus sign: (2/11)(−30) is negative, so it is not valid. The next right angle is exactly at 3:00, which belongs to the next hour.

Answer: 27 3/11 minutes past 2 (only once in that hour). This is exactly why there are 44 right angles a day and not 48.

Example 4 (medium). At what time between 7 and 8 are the hands in opposite directions?

  1. At 7:00 the hour hand is 210° ahead. For them to be opposite, the minute hand must be 180° behind, so it has to close a gap of 210 − 180 = 30°.
  2. Time = 30 ÷ 5.5 = 60/11 = 5 5/11 minutes.

Answer: 5 5/11 minutes past 7. Formula check: (2/11)(210 − 180) = 60/11.

Example 5 (medium). The mirror image of a clock shows 3:40. What is the actual time?

  1. Mirror and real time always add up to 11:60 (that is, 12:00).
  2. Actual = 11:60 − 3:40 = 8:20.

Answer: 8:20.

Example 6 (hard). A clock is set right at 8 a.m. It gains 10 minutes in 24 hours. What is the true time when it shows 1 p.m. the next day?

  1. From 8 a.m. to 1 p.m. the next day the clock shows 29 hours have passed.
  2. When 24 true hours pass, the clock shows 24 h 10 min = 24 1/6 h = 145/6 h.
  3. So true time = 29 × 24 ÷ (145/6) = 29 × 144 / 145 = 28.8 hours = 28 h 48 min.
  4. 8 a.m. + 28 h 48 min = 12:48 p.m. the next day.

Answer: 12:48 p.m. A gaining clock is ahead, so the true time must be earlier than what it shows: a quick sense check.

Example 7 (hard). The hands of a faulty clock coincide every 64 minutes of true time. How much does it gain or lose per day?

  1. In a correct clock the hands meet every 720/11 = 65 5/11 minutes of clock time.
  2. This clock completes that same clock-time in only 64 true minutes, so it runs fast: it gains 65 5/11 − 64 = 16/11 min every 64 min.
  3. In a day (1440 min): 16/11 × 1440 / 64 = 360/11 = 32 8/11 minutes.

Answer: it gains 32 8/11 minutes per day.

Common trap

17. Calendars

In plain words

Weekdays repeat every 7 days, so for day-of-the-week questions only the remainder after dividing by 7 matters. That remainder is called the number of odd days. 365 days = 52 weeks + 1 day, so an ordinary year pushes every date forward by 1 weekday; a leap year pushes it by 2.

To find the day for any date you add up the odd days from a fixed starting point and read the weekday off a 0–6 ring.

It is like a 7-seat merry-go-round. Whole turns (full weeks) change nothing. Only the leftover seats you move decide where you stop.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Leap year rule: divisible by 4, but century years only if divisible by 400. 2024 and 2000 are leap; 1900 and 2100 are not.

Ordinary year = 1 odd day. Leap year = 2 odd days.

100 years = 5 odd days, 200 years = 3, 300 years = 1, 400 years = 0. (100 years hold 24 leap years + 76 ordinary: 48 + 76 = 124 = 17 weeks + 5.)

Odd-day number → day: 0 Sun, 1 Mon, 2 Tue, 3 Wed, 4 Thu, 5 Fri, 6 Sat.

Date d / month m / year y | 1. Complete years = y − 1. Shortcuts

Shortcut

Reference-date jump. If you know the day for one date, count the days between the two dates, take mod 7 and move forward (or backward) that many places. Year to year: +1 for an ordinary year, +2 if a 29 February lies between the two dates.

Shortcut

Remember these anchors: 1 January 2001 was Monday (the 2000 years before it add up to 0 odd days, so 1 Jan of year 1 is also Monday). The last day of a century can only be Monday, Wednesday, Friday or Sunday.

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy). 1 January 2023 was a Sunday. What day was 1 January 2024?

  1. 2023 is an ordinary year, so it has 1 odd day.
  2. Sunday + 1 = Monday.

Answer: Monday.

Example 2 (easy). 10 March 2020 was a Tuesday. What day was 10 March 2021?

  1. Between 10 March 2020 and 10 March 2021 the February is February 2021, which has 28 days. The leap day, 29 February 2020, is before the start date.
  2. So the gap is 365 days = 1 odd day. Tuesday + 1 = Wednesday.

Answer: Wednesday. Most students add 2 because "2020 is a leap year". That leap day was already behind us.

Example 3 (medium). What day of the week was 15 August 1947?

  1. Complete years = 1946 = 1600 + 300 + 46.
  2. 1600 years → 0. 300 years → 1.
  3. 46 years: 11 leap years (1904 … 1944) and 35 ordinary. Odd days = 22 + 35 = 57 → 57 mod 7 = 1.
  4. Jan–Jul 1947 (not leap): 3 + 0 + 3 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 3 = 16 → 2.
  5. Date: 15 → 1.
  6. Total = 0 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 1 = 5 → Friday.

Answer: Friday.

Example 4 (medium). What day was 26 January 1950?

  1. Complete years = 1949 = 1600 + 300 + 49.
  2. 0 + 1 so far. 49 years: 12 leap (1904 … 1948) and 37 ordinary → 24 + 37 = 61 → 5.
  3. No complete month. Date 26 → 26 mod 7 = 5.
  4. Total = 0 + 1 + 5 + 5 = 11 → 4 → Thursday.

Answer: Thursday.

Example 5 (medium). The calendar of 2019 will be repeated in which year?

  1. We need the running total of odd days to hit a multiple of 7, and the new year must be ordinary like 2019.
  2. Odd days: 2019 (1), 2020 (2), 2021 (1), 2022 (1), 2023 (1), 2024 (2), 2025 (1), 2026 (1), 2027 (1), 2028 (2), 2029 (1).
  3. Running total: 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14. It reaches 14 at the end of 2029.
  4. So 1 January 2030 falls on the same weekday as 1 January 2019, and 2030 is ordinary.

Answer: 2030. Shortcut: 2019 is leap + 3, so add 11.

Example 6 (hard). What day will 1 January 2100 be?

  1. Complete years = 2099 = 2000 + 99.
  2. 2000 years → 0 (five 400s).
  3. 99 years: 24 leap years (2004 … 2096) and 75 ordinary → 48 + 75 = 123 → 123 mod 7 = 4.
  4. Date 1 → 1. Total = 5 → Friday.

Answer: Friday. Note 2100 itself is not a leap year, so its calendar is not the same as 2072's even though that is 28 years earlier.

Common trap

Treating every year divisible by 4 as leap. 1900, 2100, 2200 are not leap years. Only century years divisible by 400 (1600, 2000, 2400) are.

Common trap

Adding 2 odd days for a leap year when the dates do not cross 29 February. From 1 March 2024 to 1 March 2025 is 365 days, only 1 odd day.

Common trap

Counting the current year as complete. For 15 August 1947 you count 1946 complete years, then the months of 1947 up to July, then the 15 days.

18. Cubes & Dice

In plain words

Two kinds of questions live here. Painted-cube questions: a big cube is painted and then cut into n × n × n small cubes; you count how many small cubes have 3, 2, 1 or 0 painted faces. Dice questions: you see a die from two or three angles, or you see its flat unfolded net, and you must say which number sits opposite which.

A cube has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 corners. Every answer comes from those three numbers.

Picture a Rubik's cube dipped in paint. The 8 corner pieces show 3 colours, the edge pieces show 2, the centre of each face shows 1, and the hidden core piece shows none.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas (cube painted on all faces, cut into n³ equal cubes)

3 faces painted = 8 (the corners, for any n ≥ 2).

2 faces painted = 12(n − 2) (12 edges, each with n − 2 middle cubes).

1 face painted = 6(n − 2)² (6 faces, each with an (n − 2) × (n − 2) centre block).

0 faces painted = (n − 2)³ (the inner core).

Check: 8 + 12(n − 2) + 6(n − 2)² + (n − 2)³ = n³. Minimum cuts for n³ pieces = 3(n − 1).

  3. Check: 8 + 12 + 6 + 1 = 27.

Answer: 1, 6, 12, 8.

  1. n = 4, total 64. Unpainted core = (4 − 2)³ = 8.
  2. At least one face = 64 − 8 = 56.

  1. 6 is on top in both views, so only the four side faces moved.
  2. In View 1, 2 is at front-left. In View 2, 2 has moved to front-right. So the die turned one step, and the new front-left face (4) came from the hidden back-left position of View 1.
  3. In View 1, the back-left face is exactly opposite the front-right face, which shows 3.

Answer: 4. What we cannot say: whether 2 is opposite 1 or 5 (both fit). A good exam question only asks what the views decide.

Example 5 (hard). Three views of one die are shown. Find all three opposite pairs.

  1. Views 1 and 2 share two numbers (1 and 2). By the two-common-numbers rule, the third numbers, 3 and 6, are opposite.
  2. Neighbours of 2 seen so far: 1, 3 (View 1), 6 (View 2), 4 (View 3). That is 4 neighbours, so the leftover number, 5, is opposite 2.
  3. The last two numbers, 1 and 4, must be opposite each other.

Answer: 1–4, 2–5, 3–6. (A brute-force check over all 720 ways to number a cube confirms only this pairing fits all three views.)

Example 6 (medium). Which faces are opposite in these two nets?

  1. Cross net: in the row B C D E, B and D are alternate, so opposite; C and E are alternate, so opposite. In the column A C F, A and F are the ends, so opposite.
  2. Staircase net: fold C as the floor. B folds up as the back wall, D as the right wall, A hangs off B to become the left wall, E hangs off D to become the front wall, and F then becomes the lid.
  3. So A (left) faces D (right), B (back) faces E (front), C (floor) faces F (lid).

Answer: cross A–F, B–D, C–E; staircase A–D, B–E, C–F.

Using n = total cubes instead of the cubes along one edge. "Cut into 64 cubes" means n = 4, not 64. Always take the cube root first.

Common trap

Assuming a die is standard (opposite faces add to 7) when the question shows views. If the views show 1 next to 6, it is not a standard die. Use the views, not memory.

Common trap

In a net, calling two touching squares opposite. Squares that share an edge in the net are always neighbours on the cube.

19. Mirror, Water Images & Patterns

In plain words

A mirror image (mirror standing upright to the side) swaps left and right: top stays top. A water image (reflection in a lake below the object) swaps top and bottom: left stays left. Pattern or figure series questions show a row of pictures that change by a fixed rule (turning, adding a line, moving a dot) and ask for the next picture.

In both, you are not "seeing" the answer; you are applying one rule carefully, element by element.

Raise your right hand in front of a bathroom mirror: the person in the mirror raises the hand on your right side, which is their left. Now look at a tree in a pond: the trunk is still on the same side, but the leaves are at the bottom.

20. Venn Diagrams

In plain words

A Venn diagram draws each group as a circle. If one group lies completely inside another, its circle sits inside. If two groups share some members, the circles overlap. If they share nothing, the circles are separate. Two question types appear: choose the diagram that shows how three words are related, and count people in overlapping groups.

For counting, the whole idea is: when you add two groups, the people in both were counted twice, so subtract them once.

A class photo where students in both the cricket team and the music club appear in two team photos. To count heads, you add both team lists and then cross out the names that appear twice.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Two sets: n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B).

Neither = Total − n(A ∪ B).

Three sets: n(A ∪ B ∪ C) = A + B + C − (AB + BC + CA) + ABC, where AB means "in both A and B" (including those in all three).

Exactly two = (AB + BC + CA) − 3·ABC. Exactly one = Union − Exactly two − ABC.

Only A = A − AB − AC + ABC.

Minimum overlap of two groups = A + B − Total (if positive). Maximum overlap = the smaller of A and B.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

For relation questions, test pairs. For each pair of words ask: "all X are Y?", "some X are Y?", "no X is Y?". Three pair-answers fix the diagram. Mothers–Women: all mothers are women (inside). Doctors–Women: some (overlap). Doctors–Mothers: some (overlap).

Shortcut

For three-set counting, fill from the centre out. Write ABC in the middle first, then each "both" region minus ABC, then each "only" region. Every number you need is then just reading off regions.

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy). Which diagram shows Doctors, Women and Mothers? And India, Asia, Earth?

  1. All mothers are women, so the Mothers circle goes inside the Women circle.
  2. Some doctors are women and some are not (male doctors), so the Doctors circle overlaps Women and also sticks out.
  3. Some mothers are doctors and some are not, so Doctors also overlaps Mothers.
  4. India lies inside Asia, which lies inside Earth: three nested circles.

Example 2 (easy). In a class of 60, 35 like tea, 30 like coffee and 10 like both. How many like neither?

  1. Tea or coffee = 35 + 30 − 10 = 55.
  2. Neither = 60 − 55 = 5.

Answer: 5.

Example 3 (medium). In a survey, 70% like Maths and 80% like Physics. At least what percentage like both?

  1. If the overlap were smaller, the two groups together would need more than 100% of people.
  2. Minimum overlap = 70 + 80 − 100 = 50%.

Answer: 50%. (Maximum overlap would be 70%, when all Maths lovers also like Physics.)

Example 4 (hard). Out of 100 students, 50 take Maths (M), 40 Physics (P), 45 Chemistry (C). 15 take M and P, 12 take P and C, 18 take M and C, and 7 take all three. Find (i) how many take none, (ii) exactly two, (iii) exactly one, (iv) only Maths.

  1. Centre: all three = 7.
  2. Only-two regions: M and P only = 15 − 7 = 8; P and C only = 12 − 7 = 5; M and C only = 18 − 7 = 11. Exactly two = 8 + 5 + 11 = 24.
  3. Only regions: only M = 50 − 8 − 11 − 7 = 24; only P = 40 − 8 − 5 − 7 = 20; only C = 45 − 11 − 5 − 7 = 22. Exactly one = 66.
  4. Union = 66 + 24 + 7 = 97 (formula check: 135 − 45 + 7 = 97). None = 100 − 97 = 3.

Answer: none 3, exactly two 24, exactly one 66, only Maths 24.

Example 5 (hard). Of 50 people, 30 like apples and 25 like bananas. What are the maximum and minimum numbers who like neither?

  1. Neither is largest when the union is smallest, which happens when the overlap is as big as possible: all 25 banana lovers also like apples. Union = 30, neither = 20.
  2. Neither is smallest when the union is largest: overlap as small as possible = 30 + 25 − 50 = 5. Union = 50, neither = 0.

Answer: maximum 20, minimum 0.

Reading "15 take Maths and Physics" as "only Maths and Physics". Unless the question says only, that 15 includes the 7 who take all three.

In three-set union, forgetting to add ABC back. Subtracting the three pairs removes the centre three times after adding it three times, so it must be added once more.

Time-saving tips

Shortcut

Options are data. In series, calendars and clocks, plug options back in when the forward method is slow. For a day-of-week question, the gap between two options' days tells you which odd-day count to check.

Shortcut

Draw, do not imagine. Blood relations, directions, seating, Venn and dice all take 10 seconds to sketch and save a minute of re-reading.

Common trap

Outside knowledge in statement and syllogism questions. If the premises say "All cats are dogs", you accept it. Answer from the text only.

Test Strategy & Learning Path

In plain words

The reasoning section rewards method more than brilliance. Most questions are one of about 20 fixed types, and each type has a routine that takes 30 to 60 seconds once practised. Your job is to learn the routines, then learn to choose: which questions to do first, and which to leave.

Like a cricket batter in a limited-overs match: rotate the strike with easy singles (quick questions), hit the loose balls (formula questions), and do not throw your wicket away chasing a hard delivery (a long puzzle) early in the innings.

How to approach the section

  1. First 60 seconds: scan. Flip through the section and note which question types appear. Mark any puzzle or seating set (4–5 questions on one arrangement).
  2. Round 1: quick singles. Series, analogy, odd one out, coding, alphabet, direction, ranking, clocks, calendars, painted cubes, mirror images, numeric Venn. Target 30–45 seconds each.
  3. Round 2: logic singles. Syllogisms, inequalities, blood relations, data sufficiency, statements. Target 45–60 seconds each.
  4. Round 3: sets. Seating and puzzles. Choose the set with the most definite clues first. Budget 5–7 minutes for a 4–5 question set; if the arrangement is not fixed after 3 minutes, move on.
  5. Last 2 minutes: return to marked questions. Guess only if there is no negative marking, or if you have eliminated at least two options.

The error log

Keep one page per topic. For every wrong or slow question, write a single line: what type it was, why you missed it (rule forgotten, misread, calculation slip, too slow) and the rule that fixes it. Read the whole log every Sunday. By week 6 most mistakes repeat; the log shows you exactly which three or four rules you keep forgetting.

Before the test: sleep well, read the cheat sheet once, and plan your round order. In the hall, write EJOTY, the clock formula and the odd-day table on your rough sheet in the first minute, so they are ready when you need them.
