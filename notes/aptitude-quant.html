Part 1 — Number Foundations

1. Number System

In plain words

The number system is the "grammar" of arithmetic. It tells you what kinds of numbers exist (natural, whole, integers, primes...), how to tell quickly whether one number divides another, what is left over after a division (the remainder), and how to find the last digit of a huge power without calculating it.

Almost every other quant chapter (HCF, percentages, time and work) quietly uses these ideas. Test setters love this chapter because a student who knows the rules answers in 20 seconds, while one who does not gets stuck calculating.

Think of numbers as people in a city. Types of numbers are the neighbourhoods they live in. Divisibility rules are the quick ID checks at the gate. Remainders are the change you get back after paying. Unit-digit cycles are like the days of the week: they repeat, so you only need to know where you are in the cycle.

Key formulas

Dividend = Divisor × Quotient + Remainder, and 0 ≤ remainder < divisor.

Remainder of a product: rem(a × b) = rem(rem(a) × rem(b)). The same works for sums and powers.

Remainder theorem (polynomials): when f(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder is f(a).

Number of factors: if N = ap × bq × cr (a, b, c primes), then factors = (p+1)(q+1)(r+1).

Sum of factors: (1 + a + ... + ap)(1 + b + ... + bq)(1 + c + ... + cr).

Trailing zeros in n!: ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + ... (count the 5s; 2s are always more). For the power of any prime p in n!, divide by p, p², p³... the same way.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Unit digit of an: divide the power n by 4 and use the remainder as the new power. If the remainder is 0, use power 4. So 795: 95 ÷ 4 leaves 3, so the unit digit is the same as 73 = 343, that is 3. Only the last digit of the base matters: 1,23795 also ends in 3.

Shortcut

Make the remainder 1 (or −1). For 2100 ÷ 7, notice 23 = 8 leaves 1. Then any power of 8 also leaves 1, so only the "extra" bit matters. If a power leaves −1 (like 24 = 16 = 17 − 1 when dividing by 17), an even power of it leaves 1 and an odd power leaves −1.

Shortcut

Divisor and a factor of it: if a number leaves remainder r when divided by D, and d is a factor of D, the remainder when divided by d is simply the remainder of r divided by d. No need to find the number.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Is 918,082 divisible by 11?

  1. Number the digits from the right: 2 (1st), 8 (2nd), 0 (3rd), 8 (4th), 1 (5th), 9 (6th).
  2. Odd places: 2 + 0 + 1 = 3. Even places: 8 + 8 + 9 = 25.
  3. Difference = 3 − 25 = −22, which is a multiple of 11.

Answer: Yes, 918,082 is divisible by 11 (918,082 = 11 × 83,462).

Example 2 (Easy). Find the unit digit of 795 × 358.

  1. 795: 95 ÷ 4 leaves 3, so it ends like 73, which is 3.
  2. 358: 58 ÷ 4 leaves 2, so it ends like 32, which is 9.
  3. Multiply the unit digits: 3 × 9 = 27, so the unit digit is 7.

Answer: 7.

Example 3 (Medium). Find the remainder when 2100 is divided by 7.

  1. Look for a small power of 2 that leaves remainder 1: 23 = 8 = 7 + 1. Yes.
  2. Write 100 = 3 × 33 + 1, so 2100 = (23)33 × 2.
  3. (23)33 leaves 133 = 1. Then 1 × 2 = 2.

Answer: 2.

Example 4 (Medium). How many factors does 360 have, and what is their sum?

  1. Prime factorise: 360 = 23 × 32 × 51.
  2. Number of factors = (3+1)(2+1)(1+1) = 4 × 3 × 2 = 24. Why add 1? Each prime can appear 0, 1, ... p times, which is p + 1 choices.
  3. Sum = (1+2+4+8)(1+3+9)(1+5) = 15 × 13 × 6 = 1,170.

Answer: 24 factors, sum 1,170.

Example 5 (Medium). How many zeros are at the end of 125!?

  1. Each zero needs a pair 2 × 5. There are many more 2s than 5s, so count the 5s.
  2. ⌊125/5⌋ = 25, ⌊125/25⌋ = 5, ⌊125/125⌋ = 1.
  3. Total = 25 + 5 + 1 = 31.

Answer: 31. Faster: keep dividing by 5 and adding the quotients: 125 → 25 → 5 → 1, sum 31.

Example 6 (Hard). A number divided by 357 leaves remainder 39. What is the remainder when the same number is divided by 17?

  1. Number = 357k + 39. Since 357 = 17 × 21, the part 357k is a multiple of 17.
  2. So only 39 matters: 39 = 2 × 17 + 5.

Answer: 5. This works only because 17 divides 357. If the new divisor is not a factor of the old one, the answer cannot be fixed.

Common trap

When the power leaves remainder 0 on division by 4, students use power 0 and say the unit digit is 1. Wrong: use power 4. For 240, the unit digit is 6 (like 24 = 16), not 1.

Common trap

For divisibility by 12, "divisible by 2 and 6" is not enough (6 and 2 are not co-prime: 18 passes but is not divisible by 12). Always split into co-prime parts: 12 = 3 × 4, 18 = 2 × 9, 24 = 3 × 8.

2. HCF & LCM

In plain words

HCF (Highest Common Factor, also called GCD) is the biggest number that divides all the given numbers exactly. LCM (Least Common Multiple) is the smallest number that all the given numbers divide into exactly.

Rule of thumb for word problems: if the question is about cutting, sharing or splitting into the largest equal pieces, it is HCF. If it is about things happening together again (bells, lights, runners, buses), it is LCM.

You have ribbons of 36 cm and 84 cm and want to cut both into equal pieces as long as possible with nothing wasted: the piece length is the HCF (12 cm). Two buses leave a stop every 36 and 84 minutes; the next time they leave together is the LCM (252 minutes).

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Prime factorisation method: HCF = product of the lowest powers of the common primes. LCM = product of the highest powers of all primes that appear.

Division (Euclid) method for HCF: divide the bigger by the smaller, then divide the divisor by the remainder, and repeat until the remainder is 0. The last divisor is the HCF.

For two numbers only: HCF × LCM = first number × second number.

Fractions: HCF = HCF of numerators ÷ LCM of denominators; LCM = LCM of numerators ÷ HCF of denominators. Reduce each fraction to lowest terms first.

HCF always divides the LCM. The HCF of co-prime numbers is 1 and their LCM is their product.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Check the options. The HCF must divide every given number, and the LCM must be divisible by every given number. In MCQs you can often eliminate three options in seconds this way.

Shortcut

Other number from HCF and LCM: other = HCF × LCM ÷ given number. If the HCF is H, the two numbers are H·a and H·b with a, b co-prime, and LCM = H·a·b.

Shortcut

Counting "times together": in a time T, things with LCM L meet ⌊T/L⌋ times after the start. Add 1 if the question counts the starting moment too.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Find the HCF and LCM of 36, 84 and 120.

  1. 36 = 22 × 32, 84 = 22 × 3 × 7, 120 = 23 × 3 × 5.
  2. HCF: common primes are 2 and 3; lowest powers 22 and 31. HCF = 4 × 3 = 12.
  3. LCM: all primes at highest powers: 23 × 32 × 5 × 7 = 8 × 9 × 35 = 2,520.

Answer: HCF 12, LCM 2,520.

Example 2 (Easy). Find the HCF of 1071 and 462 by the division method.

1071 = 2 × 462 + 147
 462 = 3 × 147 + 21
 147 = 7 × 21  + 0    -> last divisor is 21

Answer: 21. Why it works: any number dividing both 1071 and 462 also divides 1071 − 2 × 462 = 147, and so on down, so the HCF never changes as the numbers shrink.

Example 3 (Medium). The HCF of two numbers is 12 and their LCM is 336. If one number is 48, find the other.

  1. Use HCF × LCM = product: 12 × 336 = 48 × other.
  2. Other = 4,032 ÷ 48 = 84.

Answer: 84. Check: HCF(48, 84) = 12 and LCM(48, 84) = 336.

Example 4 (Medium). Find the HCF and LCM of 2/3, 8/9 and 16/81.

  1. Numerators 2, 8, 16: HCF 2, LCM 16. Denominators 3, 9, 81: HCF 3, LCM 81.
  2. HCF = 2/81. LCM = 16/3.

Answer: HCF 2/81, LCM 16/3.

Example 5 (Medium). Four bells toll at intervals of 6, 8, 12 and 15 minutes. They toll together at 9:00 AM. When will they next toll together, and how many times will they toll together in 6 hours (counting 9:00 AM)?

  1. LCM(6, 8, 12, 15) = 23 × 3 × 5 = 120 minutes = 2 hours.
  2. Next time together: 9:00 AM + 2 hours = 11:00 AM.
  3. In 6 hours = 360 minutes: 360 ÷ 120 = 3 times after the start, plus the start = 4.

Answer: 11:00 AM; 4 times.

Example 6 (Hard). Find the greatest number that divides 43, 91 and 183 leaving the same remainder each time.

  1. If all three leave the same remainder r, then the differences have no remainder at all.
  2. Differences: 91 − 43 = 48, 183 − 91 = 92, 183 − 43 = 140.
  3. HCF(48, 92, 140) = 4.

Answer: 4 (each number leaves remainder 3).

Example 7 (Hard). Find the smallest number which, when divided by 12, 15, 20 and 54, leaves remainder 8 in each case.

  1. LCM(12, 15, 20, 54) = 22 × 33 × 5 = 540.
  2. Add the common remainder: 540 + 8 = 548.

Answer: 548.

Common trap

HCF × LCM = product works for two numbers only. For three numbers, 36 × 84 × 120 is not equal to 12 × 2,520.

Common trap

"How many times do they toll together in 1 hour?" Read whether the starting toll counts. Test setters put both answers (with and without the +1) in the options.

3. Simplification & Approximation

In plain words

Simplification questions give you a long expression with brackets, fractions and decimals and ask for its value. The whole game is doing the operations in the right order and keeping the numbers small along the way.

Approximation questions (very common in bank-style and TCS/Infosys tests) use ugly numbers like 39.97% of 649.8. You are expected to round to friendly numbers, calculate in your head, and pick the closest option.

It is like getting dressed: socks before shoes, shirt before jacket. Do them in the wrong order and the result looks silly. Brackets are the "socks": they always go first, starting from the innermost one.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

VBODMAS order: Vinculum (bar) → Brackets ( ), { }, [ ] from the inside out → Of → Division and Multiplication (left to right) → Addition and Subtraction (left to right).

"Of" means multiply, but it is done before ÷ and ×. So 48 ÷ 1/4 of 16 = 48 ÷ 4 = 12.

Recurring decimals: 0.ababab... = ab/99 (e.g. 0.3636... = 36/99 = 4/11); 0.4777... = (47 − 4)/90 = 43/90 (all digits minus non-repeating part, over one 9 per repeating digit and one 0 per non-repeating digit).

Identities: (a + b)2 = a2 + 2ab + b2; a2 − b2 = (a + b)(a − b); a3 − b3 = (a − b)(a2 + ab + b2); a3 + b3 = (a + b)(a2 − ab + b2).

Telescoping: 1/(n(n+1)) = 1/n − 1/(n+1).

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Approximation: round each number to the nearest "nice" value (49.98 → 50, 16.02 → 16, 39.97% → 40%). When one number is rounded up and the other down in a product, the errors partly cancel. Then choose the option closest to your estimate; options are usually far apart.

Shortcut

Decimals: count decimal places. 0.25 × 0.4: 25 × 4 = 100 with 3 decimal places → 0.100. For division, shift both decimals together: 1.2 ÷ 0.3 = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.

Shortcut

Spot an identity. If you see a3 − b3 over a2 + ab + b2, the answer is just a − b. Test setters use these to make the "long way" painful.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Simplify 18 ÷ 3 × 2 + 4 − 6.

  1. Division and multiplication go left to right: 18 ÷ 3 = 6, then 6 × 2 = 12.
  2. Then addition and subtraction left to right: 12 + 4 − 6 = 10.

Answer: 10. (Doing 3 × 2 first would give 18 ÷ 6 = 3, and the wrong answer 1.)

Example 2 (Medium). Simplify 36 − [18 − {14 − (15 − 4 ÷ 2 × 2)}].

  1. Innermost ( ): 4 ÷ 2 × 2 = 2 × 2 = 4, so 15 − 4 = 11.
  2. { }: 14 − 11 = 3.
  3. [ ]: 18 − 3 = 15.
  4. Finally 36 − 15 = 21.

Answer: 21.

Example 3 (Easy). Simplify 48 ÷ 1/4 of 16.

  1. "Of" comes before division: 1/4 of 16 = 4.
  2. 48 ÷ 4 = 12.

Answer: 12. (If you divided first you would get 48 × 4 × 16, a huge wrong number.)

Example 4 (Easy). Find 0.25 × 0.4 + 1.2 ÷ 0.3.

  1. 0.25 × 0.4 = 0.1.
  2. 1.2 ÷ 0.3 = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.
  3. 0.1 + 4 = 4.1.

Answer: 4.1.

Example 5 (Medium). Find the approximate value of 39.97% of 649.8 + 25.02 × 11.99.

  1. Round: 40% of 650 = 260.
  2. Round: 25 × 12 = 300.
  3. Estimate = 260 + 300 = 560.

Answer: about 560 (exact value 559.71).

Example 6 (Hard). Simplify 1 + 1 / (1 + 1 / (1 + 1/2)).

  1. Start at the bottom: 1 + 1/2 = 3/2.
  2. 1 ÷ 3/2 = 2/3, so the middle is 1 + 2/3 = 5/3.
  3. 1 ÷ 5/3 = 3/5, so the whole thing is 1 + 3/5 = 8/5.

Answer: 8/5. For continued fractions, always start at the bottom and climb up.

Common trap

Division and multiplication have equal rank; so do addition and subtraction. They are done left to right, not "D before M". 18 ÷ 3 × 2 is 12, not 3.

Common trap

A minus sign before a bracket flips every sign inside: 10 − (7 − 5 − 3) = 10 − (−1) = 11, not 10 − 7 − 5 − 3.

4. Squares, Cubes, Roots & Indices

In plain words

A power (index) is repeated multiplication: 25 = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2. A root undoes a power: √49 = 7 because 72 = 49. A surd is a root that does not come out as a whole number, like √2 or √5.

In tests, powers and roots appear inside almost every chapter (compound interest, areas, simplification). Knowing squares up to 30 and cubes up to 15 by heart turns many 1-minute questions into 10-second ones.

A power is like folding a sheet of paper: each fold doubles the layers, so 5 folds give 25 = 32 layers. A root asks the reverse question: "32 layers, how many folds?"

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Laws of indices: am × an = am+n; am ÷ an = am−n; (am)n = amn; (ab)n = anbn; a0 = 1 (a ≠ 0); a−n = 1/an; am/n = (ⁿ√a)m.

Equal bases: if ax = ay (a ≠ 0, 1, −1) then x = y.

Surds: √a × √b = √(ab); √a ÷ √b = √(a/b); √a + √b is not √(a + b).

Rationalising: 1/(√a − √b) = (√a + √b)/(a − b) — multiply top and bottom by the conjugate.

Digital root (repeated digit sum): digital root of a product = digital root of the product of digital roots. Use it to check answers.

6. Percentages

In plain words

A percentage is just a fraction with 100 at the bottom. "25%" means "25 out of every 100", which is the same as 1/4. Almost every arithmetic chapter (profit, interest, growth, data interpretation) is percentages in disguise, so this chapter pays back more than any other.

The one idea to hold on to: a percentage is always of something. Always ask "percent of what?" — the base. Most mistakes come from using the wrong base.

A phone battery at 40% means 40 out of 100 bars. If a bigger battery is also "at 40%", it holds more charge, because the base is bigger. Same percent, different base, different amount.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • x% of N = x × N / 100
  • What % is A of B? = A / B × 100
  • Percentage change = (New − Old) / Old × 100 (always divide by the OLD value)
  • Increase by r%: multiply by (1 + r/100). Decrease by r%: multiply by (1 − r/100).
  • Successive changes a% and b%: net change = a + b + ab/100 % (use a minus sign for a decrease)
  • Growth for n years: P(1 + r/100)n. Depreciation: P(1 − r/100)n.
  • A is r% more than B → B is r / (100 + r) × 100 % less than A.
  • A is r% less than B → B is r / (100 − r) × 100 % more than A.
  • Price up r%, keep spending same → cut consumption by r / (100 + r) × 100 %.

7. Profit, Loss & Discount

In plain words

Cost price (CP) is what the seller paid. Selling price (SP) is what the buyer pays. If SP > CP there is a profit; if SP < CP there is a loss. The marked price (MP) is the price on the tag, and a discount is how much is taken off the tag.

The golden rule: profit and loss % are on CP; discount % is on MP. Keep those two bases apart and most questions become simple.

A shopkeeper buys a bag for ₹100 (CP), puts a tag of ₹140 (MP), and during a sale gives 15% off the tag, so you pay ₹119 (SP). She still makes ₹19 profit, which is 19% of her cost.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Profit = SP − CP; Loss = CP − SP
  • Profit % = Profit / CP × 100; Loss % = Loss / CP × 100
  • SP = CP × (100 + P%) / 100; SP = CP × (100 − L%) / 100
  • CP = SP × 100 / (100 + P%) or SP × 100 / (100 − L%)
  • Discount = MP − SP; Discount % = Discount / MP × 100
  • Successive discounts d1% and d2%: single equivalent = d1 + d2 − d1·d2/100
  • Mark-up m% and discount d%: profit % = m − d − m·d/100
  • MP needed for profit P% after discount D%: MP / CP = (100 + P) / (100 − D)
  • False weight (sells at CP): gain % = error / (true weight − error) × 100
  • Buy x, get y free: effective discount = y / (x + y) × 100
  • Two items sold at the SAME SP, one at x% gain and one at x% loss: always a loss of x²/100 %
  • CP of n items = SP of m items: profit % = (n − m) / m × 100

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Multiplier thinking. 20% profit = ×1.2, 10% loss = ×0.9, 15% discount = ×0.85. A 40% mark-up with 15% off is 1.4 × 0.85 = 1.19, so 19% profit. Finding CP from SP is a division: CP = SP / 1.2.

Shortcut

Fraction trick for "had he sold for ₹X more". If the change moves the result from L% loss to G% gain, then (L + G)% of CP = ₹X. So CP = X / (L + G) × 100.

Shortcut

Same SP, x% gain and x% loss. Do not calculate anything: the answer is always a loss of x²/100 %. 10% each way → 1% loss.

8. Simple & Compound Interest

In plain words

Interest is the rent you pay for using someone's money. Simple interest (SI) is paid only on the original amount (the principal), so it is the same every year. Compound interest (CI) is paid on the principal plus the interest already earned, so it grows faster each year — "interest on interest".

For 1 year SI and CI are equal (with yearly compounding). From year 2 onwards CI pulls ahead.

SI is like a fixed salary of ₹1,000 a year. CI is like a salary that gets a 10% raise every year on whatever you earned last year: the raises themselves start earning raises.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • SI = P × R × T / 100; Amount A = P + SI
  • CI amount A = P(1 + R/100)T; CI = A − P
  • Half-yearly: use rate R/2 and time 2T. Quarterly: rate R/4 and time 4T.
  • CI − SI for 2 years = P(R/100)2
  • CI − SI for 3 years = P(R/100)2(3 + R/100)
  • SI: sum doubles in T years → R = 100/T. Becomes n times → R = 100(n − 1)/T.
  • CI: sum becomes x times in T years → becomes xk times in kT years.
  • Rule of 72 (CI, rough): doubling time ≈ 72 / R years.
  • Two consecutive CI amounts An and An+1: rate = (An+1 − An) / An × 100

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Use a + b + ab/100 for CI. 2 years at 10%: 10 + 10 + 1 = 21%. 3 years at 10%: 21 + 10 + 2.1 = 33.1%. You never need to raise anything to a power.

Shortcut

Year-by-year for small T. ₹10,000 at 10%: year 1 interest 1,000; year 2 interest 1,000 + 100 (interest on last year's 1,000); year 3 = 1,000 + 200 + 10. CI for 3 years = 3,310.

Shortcut

Powers in CI "times" questions. If money triples in 4 years, it becomes 3 × 3 = 9 times in 8 years and 27 times in 12 years. Never multiply the time by the factor.

9. Ratio & Proportion

In plain words

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind by division. "Boys : girls = 3 : 5" means for every 3 boys there are 5 girls. It tells you the shares, not the actual numbers: the class could have 3 and 5, or 30 and 50.

A proportion says two ratios are equal, like 3 : 5 = 9 : 15. Most ratio questions come down to one move: turn the ratio into "parts", find what one part is worth, then multiply.

A lemonade recipe says 1 cup lemon juice to 4 cups water. Whether you make a glass or a bucket, the taste stays the same because the ratio stays the same. Scaling a recipe is proportion.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Divide amount S in ratio a : b → first = a/(a+b) × S, second = b/(a+b) × S
  • Combine a : b and c : d into A : B : C → multiply to make the common term equal: A : B : C = ac : bc : bd
  • In a : b = c : d, a and d are extremes, b and c are means, and a × d = b × c
  • Mean proportional of a and b = √(ab)
  • Third proportional to a and b = b² / a (a : b = b : x)
  • Fourth proportional to a, b, c = bc / a (a : b = c : x)
  • Duplicate ratio of a : b = a² : b²; sub-duplicate = √a : √b; triplicate = a³ : b³
  • Ratio of fractions 1/a : 1/b : 1/c → multiply every term by the LCM of a, b, c
  • Adding the same number x to both terms: solve (a + x)/(b + x) = new ratio by cross-multiplying

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Think in parts. In 3 : 5 there are 8 parts. If the total is ₹1,200, one part is ₹150. Every ratio question with a total, a difference or one known share is solved by finding the value of one part.

Shortcut

Combining ratios with the "N" method. Write A : B = 2 : 3 and B : C = 4 : 5 one below the other. A = 2 × 4, B = 3 × 4, C = 3 × 5 → 8 : 12 : 15. (Multiply along a zig-zag.)

Shortcut

Check options. If the numbers are in 3 : 5, both must be multiples of 3 and 5 respectively. Plugging options into the condition is often faster than algebra.

Solved examples

  1. Total parts = 3 + 5 = 8.
  2. One part = 1,200 / 8 = ₹150.

  3. So A : B : C = 8 : 12 : 15.

Answer: 8 : 12 : 15.

10. Partnership

In plain words

When people start a business together, the profit is shared in the ratio of how much each put in and for how long. Money invested for 12 months deserves more than the same money invested for 6 months. So the share depends on capital × time.

A working partner may take a salary or a fixed cut first; only what is left is split by capital × time. A sleeping partner only invests money.

Renting out a car: lending it for 10 days earns more than lending it for 2 days. ₹1,000 working for 12 months counts as "12,000 rupee-months", the same as ₹2,000 working for 6 months.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Same time for all: profit ratio = capital ratio
  • Different times: profit ratio = C1 × T1 : C2 × T2 : C3 × T3
  • Capital changes during the year: add up each piece, e.g. 10,000 × 6 + 8,000 × 6
  • Given profit ratio and time ratio: capital ratio = (P1/T1) : (P2/T2)
  • Given profit ratio and capital ratio: time ratio = (P1/C1) : (P2/C2)
  • Working partner: first take out the salary / commission, then split the rest by capital × time

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Cut the zeros early. 60,000 × 8 : 50,000 × 12 → 6 × 8 : 5 × 12 → 48 : 60 → 4 : 5. Work with small numbers.

Shortcut

"Joins after k months". A partner who joins after k months in a 12-month year invests for (12 − k) months. Write the time next to each name before doing anything else.

11. Averages

In plain words

The average (mean) is the value every item would have if the total were shared out equally. Average = total ÷ count. Turn it around and you get the most useful fact in this chapter: total = average × count.

Nearly every average question is solved by working with totals: find the old total, find the new total, and the difference is the person or score that joined, left or was replaced.

Five friends pool money for a pizza: ₹100, ₹150, ₹200, ₹250, ₹300. The total is ₹1,000, so the "fair" share is ₹200 each. That fair share is the average.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Average = Sum / Number; Sum = Average × Number
  • Weighted average of groups: (n1·a1 + n2·a2) / (n1 + n2)
  • New member joins and average rises by d: new member = old average + (new count) × d
  • Replacement (count stays n, average rises by d): new person = old person + n × d
  • Average speed for equal distances at x and y: 2xy / (x + y) (not (x + y)/2)
  • Average speed in general = total distance / total time
  • Average of consecutive numbers (or any AP) = (first + last) / 2 = the middle term
  • Average of first n natural numbers = (n + 1)/2
  • If every value increases by k, the average increases by k; if every value is multiplied by k, so is the average

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Deviation method. To average 48, 52, 55, 45, 50, assume 50. Deviations: −2, +2, +5, −5, 0 → sum 0. Average = 50 + 0/5 = 50. No big additions.

Shortcut

Replacement in one line. "Average of 8 rises by 2.5 when a person of 65 kg is replaced." The group's total rose by 8 × 2.5 = 20, so the new person weighs 65 + 20 = 85 kg.

Shortcut

Weighted average sits closer to the bigger group. 30 students at 60 and 20 at 70: the answer must be nearer 60. The gaps (4 and 6) are in the inverse ratio of the group sizes (30 : 20 = 3 : 2).

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Find the average of 12, 15, 18, 21 and 24.

  1. They are equally spaced (step 3), so the average is the middle number.
  2. Middle = 18. (Check: sum = 90, 90 / 5 = 18.)

Answer: 18.

  1. Total A = 30 × 60 = 1,800. Total B = 20 × 70 = 1,400.
  2. Combined = 3,200 / 50 = 64.

Answer: 64. Not 65 — the bigger class pulls the average toward 60.

  1. The count stays 8. The total rose by 8 × 2.5 = 20 kg.
  2. That extra 20 kg came only from the swap, so the new person is 20 kg heavier than 65.
  3. New person = 85 kg.

  2. Teacher = 231 − 200 = 31.

Answer: 31 years. Shortcut: new average + (old count × rise) = 21 + 10 × 1 = 31.

  3. Check with distance 120 km: time 2 h + 3 h = 5 h, total 240 km / 5 h = 48.

Common trap

Averaging the speeds. (60 + 40)/2 = 50 is wrong because you spend more time at the slower speed. Use total distance / total time.

Common trap

Averaging averages. You can only take a plain average of group averages if the groups are the same size. Otherwise weight them.

12. Mixtures & Alligation

In plain words

When you mix two things with different prices (or strengths), the mixture's price lands somewhere in between. Alligation is a quick rule that tells you in what ratio to mix them to hit a target price. It is just the weighted average from the Averages chapter, run backwards.

The second idea is repeated replacement: taking some liquid out of a vessel and topping it up with water, again and again. Each round keeps the same fraction of the pure liquid.

Mixing hot water (80°C) and cold water (20°C) to get a 40°C bath: you need more cold than hot, because 40 is closer to 20. Alligation tells you exactly how much more: 2 parts cold to 1 part hot.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Alligation rule: Cheaper qty : Dearer qty = (Dearer − Mean) : (Mean − Cheaper)
  • Mean price of a mixture = (q1·p1 + q2·p2) / (q1 + q2)
  • Water costs ₹0 — treat it as the "cheaper" item with price 0 (or strength 0%)
  • Repeated removal: vessel of V litres, x litres taken out and replaced by water, n times: pure liquid left = V × (1 − x/V)n
  • Mixing two mixtures in equal amounts: average the fractions of each component
  • Works for anything that averages: prices, % strength, profit %, speeds for equal time, marks

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Draw the cross. Cheaper at top-left, dearer at top-right, mean in the middle. Subtract along the diagonals and write the differences at the bottom. Bottom-left : bottom-right is the answer. The diagram below shows it.

Shortcut

Replacement fraction. Removing 1/10 of the liquid each time keeps 9/10. After 3 times, pure = (9/10)³ = 729/1000 of the start. Think "keep fraction" to the power n.

Shortcut

Alligation beyond prices. "Sold part at 8% profit and the rest at 18%, overall 14%" — alligate the percentages directly: (18 − 14) : (14 − 8) = 2 : 3.

Solved examples

  1. Cheaper = 40, dearer = 55, mean = 45.
  2. Cheaper : dearer = (55 − 45) : (45 − 40) = 10 : 5 = 2 : 1.

Answer: 2 : 1. Check: (2 × 40 + 1 × 55) / 3 = 135 / 3 = 45.

  2. Water : milk = (20 − 16) : (16 − 0) = 4 : 16 = 1 : 4.
  3. Milk is 60 L = 4 parts, so 1 part = 15 L of water.

Answer: 15 litres.

Example 3 (Medium). A 40% acid solution and a 70% acid solution are mixed to make 30 litres of 50% solution. How much of each is used?

  2. 30 L in 2 : 1 → 20 L of the 40% solution and 10 L of the 70% solution.

Answer: 20 L of 40% and 10 L of 70%. Check: 8 + 7 = 15 L acid in 30 L = 50%.

Example 4 (Medium). A vessel holds 40 litres of milk. 4 litres are taken out and replaced with water. This is done 3 times in all. How much milk is left?

  1. Each round keeps (1 − 4/40) = 9/10 of the milk.
  2. After 3 rounds: 40 × (9/10)³ = 40 × 0.729 = 29.16 L.

Answer: 29.16 litres. (Not 40 − 12 = 28: later rounds remove a mix, not pure milk.)

Example 5 (Hard). Two vessels have milk and water in the ratios 3 : 1 and 5 : 3. Equal amounts are poured from both into a third vessel. Find the milk : water ratio there.

  2. Equal amounts, so average the fractions: (3/4 + 5/8)/2 = (6/8 + 5/8)/2 = 11/16.
  3. Water fraction = 5/16. Ratio = 11 : 5.

Answer: 11 : 5.

Example 6 (Hard). A trader has 60 kg of sugar. He sells part at 8% profit and the rest at 18% profit, making 14% overall. How much did he sell at 8%?

  1. Alligate the profit %: 8% part : 18% part = (18 − 14) : (14 − 8) = 4 : 6 = 2 : 3.
  2. 60 kg in 2 : 3 → 24 kg at 8% and 36 kg at 18%.

Answer: 24 kg.

Common trap

Swapping the sides. The difference written under "cheaper" comes from the DEARER price (dearer − mean). If your answer gives more of the item that is farther from the mean, you swapped them.

Common trap

Subtracting replacement linearly. Removing 4 L three times does not remove 12 L of milk. Use V(1 − x/V)n.

13. Problems on Ages

In plain words

Age problems give you facts about people's ages at different times — now, some years ago, some years later — and ask you to find the present ages. They are just linear equations with a story.

The key fact: everyone ages at the same speed. After 5 years, everyone is 5 years older, so the difference between two people's ages never changes. Ratios change, differences do not.

Two runners on a track moving at exactly the same speed: the gap between them stays fixed forever, even though both keep moving. Age gaps work the same way.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Present age x → t years ago: x − t; t years later: x + t
  • Age difference is constant at all times
  • Present ages in ratio a : b → take ages ax and bx
  • "A is n times as old as B" → A = n·B (at that same moment)
  • Sum of ages of k people increases by k × t after t years
  • Children born d years apart, youngest y: ages y, y + d, y + 2d, …
  • Age at birth of child = parent's age − child's age (constant)

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Try the options. Age answers are small whole numbers. Plug each option into the conditions — often faster than setting up equations. Ages cannot be negative or fractional.

Shortcut

Ratio-difference trick. Now 4 : 5, after 5 years 5 : 6. Both ratios have a difference of 1 part, and the real difference is fixed, so 1 part is the same in both. Moving from 4 to 5 parts took 5 years, so 1 part = 5. Ages: 20 and 25.

Shortcut

Draw a timeline for "when A was as old as B is now" questions. Put the two moments in columns, fill in the ages, and use the constant gap.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). A father is 3 times as old as his son. After 12 years he will be twice as old. Find their present ages.

  1. Son = x, father = 3x.
  2. After 12 years: 3x + 12 = 2(x + 12) = 2x + 24.
  3. x = 12. Son 12, father 36.

Answer: son 12, father 36. Check: 48 = 2 × 24.

Example 2 (Medium). The ages of A and B are in the ratio 4 : 5. After 5 years the ratio will be 5 : 6. Find their present ages.

  1. Ages 4x and 5x. (4x + 5)/(5x + 5) = 5/6.
  2. 6(4x + 5) = 5(5x + 5) → 24x + 30 = 25x + 25 → x = 5.
  3. A = 20, B = 25.

Answer: 20 and 25. Shortcut: ratio-difference trick above.

Example 3 (Medium). The sum of the ages of 5 children born 3 years apart is 50 years. Find the youngest child's age.

  1. Ages: y, y + 3, y + 6, y + 9, y + 12.
  2. Sum = 5y + 30 = 50 → y = 4.

Answer: 4 years. Shortcut: the middle child is the average, 50/5 = 10, so the youngest is 10 − 6 = 4.

Example 4 (Medium). Ten years ago, A was half as old as B. The ratio of their present ages is 3 : 4. Find the sum of their present ages.

  1. Present ages 3x and 4x. Ten years ago: 3x − 10 and 4x − 10.
  2. 3x − 10 = (4x − 10)/2 → 6x − 20 = 4x − 10 → x = 5.
  3. Ages 15 and 20. Sum = 35.

Answer: 35 years. Check: ten years ago 5 and 10.

Example 5 (Hard). A is twice as old as B was when A was as old as B is now. The sum of their present ages is 63. Find A's age.

  1. Let the age gap be d = A − B. "When A was as old as B is now" was d years ago.
  2. At that time B was B − d. The sentence says A (now) = 2(B − d) = 2(B − (A − B)) = 4B − 2A.
  3. So 3A = 4B, i.e. A : B = 4 : 3. With A + B = 63: A = 36, B = 27.
  4. Check: 9 years ago A was 27 (= B now) and B was 18. A now = 36 = 2 × 18.

Common trap

Applying the time shift to only one person. "5 years ago" affects everyone in the question. Subtract 5 from every age at that moment.

Common trap

Answering the wrong person or time. You solve for x, but the question may want the father's age, the sum, or the age after 5 years. Re-read the last line before marking.

Part 3 — Time, Work & Motion

14. Time & Work

In plain words

Time and work questions ask: if a person finishes a job in some days, how much of the job do they do each day, and how long do several people take together? The trick is to stop thinking in fractions like 1/12 and 1/18, and instead give the whole job a nice number of units that every person's time divides into.

The one idea behind everything: work done = rate × time. People add their rates when they work together, never their days.

Painting a fence with 36 planks. If A paints the whole fence in 12 days, A paints 3 planks a day. If B takes 18 days, B paints 2 planks a day. Together they paint 5 planks a day, so 36 planks take 36 ÷ 5 = 7.2 days. Counting planks is far easier than adding 1/12 + 1/18.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • If A finishes a job in n days, A's one-day work = 1/n of the job.
  • Total work = LCM of all the "days alone" numbers. Each person's units per day = total ÷ their days.
  • Together: add units per day. Time together = total work ÷ (sum of units per day).
  • Two people: time together = ab / (a + b) days.
  • Efficiency is just the rate. If A is twice as efficient as B, A does 2 units while B does 1, so A takes half the time. Efficiency and time are inversely proportional.
  • Men-days-hours rule: M1 × D1 × H1 / W1 = M2 × D2 × H2 / W2 (M = men, D = days, H = hours per day, W = amount of work).
  • Wages are split in the ratio of work done, not days worked. For people who work the same number of days, that is the ratio of their efficiencies.

Shortcuts

Shortcut: the LCM (total work) method

Write a small table: person, days alone, units per day. Pick total work = LCM of the days. Now every step is whole-number addition and division. This method works for 90% of time and work questions and also for pipes and cisterns.

Shortcut: "together" and "one alone" known

If A + B together take x days and A alone takes a days, then B's rate = 1/x − 1/a. With LCM: total = LCM(x, a), B's units = (total/x) − (total/a).

Shortcut: pairs A+B, B+C, C+A

Add all three pair rates. You get 2(A + B + C). Halve it to get all three together.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). A can do a job in 12 days and B in 18 days. How long will they take working together?

  1. Total work = LCM(12, 18) = 36 units.
  2. A does 36 ÷ 12 = 3 units a day. B does 36 ÷ 18 = 2 units a day.
  3. Together: 3 + 2 = 5 units a day.
  4. Time = 36 ÷ 5 = 7.2 days.

Answer: 7 1/5 days. Faster: ab/(a + b) = 12 × 18 / 30 = 216/30 = 7.2.

Example 2 (Easy). A and B together finish a job in 10 days. A alone takes 15 days. How long does B alone take?

  1. Total = LCM(10, 15) = 30 units.
  2. A + B do 30 ÷ 10 = 3 units a day. A alone does 30 ÷ 15 = 2 units a day.
  3. So B does 3 − 2 = 1 unit a day.
  4. B alone needs 30 ÷ 1 = 30 days.

Answer: 30 days.

Example 3 (Medium). A is twice as efficient as B. Together they finish a job in 14 days. How long would A take alone?

  1. Efficiency means rate. Let A do 2 units a day and B do 1 unit a day.
  2. Together: 3 units a day for 14 days = 42 units. That is the total work.
  3. A alone: 42 ÷ 2 = 21 days.

Answer: 21 days. Notice B alone would take 42 days: twice as efficient means half the time.

Example 4 (Medium). A can do a job in 20 days and B in 30 days. They work together for 5 days, then A leaves. How many more days does B need?

  1. Total = LCM(20, 30) = 60 units. A = 3 units/day, B = 2 units/day.
  2. In 5 days together they do 5 × 5 = 25 units.
  3. Remaining = 60 − 25 = 35 units.
  4. B alone: 35 ÷ 2 = 17.5 days.

Answer: 17 1/2 days.

Example 5 (Medium). 12 men working 8 hours a day build a 240 m wall in 10 days. In how many days will 16 men working 6 hours a day build a 480 m wall?

  1. Use M1 × D1 × H1 / W1 = M2 × D2 × H2 / W2.
  2. 12 × 10 × 8 / 240 = 16 × D2 × 6 / 480.
  3. Left side = 960/240 = 4. So 96 × D2 / 480 = 4, giving D2 = 4 × 480 / 96 = 20.

Answer: 20 days. Sense check: twice the wall needs twice the man-hours (1920), and 16 × 6 = 96 man-hours a day, so 1920 ÷ 96 = 20.

Example 6 (Hard). A can do a job in 10 days and B in 15 days. With C's help they finish it in 4 days and are paid ₹1500 in total. Find each person's share.

  1. Total = LCM(10, 15, 4) = 60 units. A = 6/day, B = 4/day.
  2. All three together do 60 ÷ 4 = 15 units a day, so C = 15 − 6 − 4 = 5 units a day.
  3. They all work the same 4 days, so wages split in the ratio of daily work: 6 : 4 : 5 (sum 15).
  4. A = 1500 × 6/15 = ₹600, B = 1500 × 4/15 = ₹400, C = 1500 × 5/15 = ₹500.

Answer: A ₹600, B ₹400, C ₹500.

Common trap

Never add days. If A takes 12 days and B takes 18, together is NOT 30 days or 15 days. Together must be less than the faster person alone. Add rates (units per day), then divide.

Common trap

Wages go by work done. If someone worked fewer days, their share is (their rate × their days) out of the total work, not an equal split.

15. Pipes & Cisterns

In plain words

Pipes and cisterns is time and work in disguise. A pipe that fills a tank is a worker doing positive work. A pipe or a leak that empties a tank is a worker doing negative work. The "job" is filling (or emptying) the tank.

A bathtub with the tap on and the plug slightly open. The tap pours water in, the drain takes some out. What matters is the net flow: tap rate minus drain rate. If the drain is faster, the tub empties even with the tap running.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • A pipe fills a tank in n hours: it fills 1/n of the tank per hour (positive).
  • An outlet or leak empties it in m hours: it removes 1/m per hour (negative).
  • Net rate = sum of inlet rates − sum of outlet rates. Time = capacity ÷ net rate.
  • Two inlets: together = ab / (a + b). One inlet (a) and one outlet (b, with b > a): time to fill = ab / (b − a).
  • Leak problem: a pipe fills in a hours, with a leak it takes b hours. Leak alone empties the full tank in ab / (b − a) hours.
  • If the net rate comes out negative, the tank is actually emptying.

Shortcuts

Shortcut: capacity = LCM

Take tank capacity = LCM of all the times. Inlets get + units per hour, outlets get − units per hour. Add with signs. Same table as time and work.

Shortcut: litres given

When a rate is given in litres per minute, write every other pipe as C/time, set up one equation in C, and solve. Convert hours to minutes first so the units match.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). Pipe A fills a tank in 6 hours and pipe B in 12 hours. How long do both take together?

  1. Capacity = LCM(6, 12) = 12 units. A = 2/h, B = 1/h.
  2. Together 3 units/h, so 12 ÷ 3 = 4 hours.

Answer: 4 hours.

Example 2 (Easy). Pipe A fills a tank in 10 hours; pipe B empties it in 15 hours. If both are opened on an empty tank, when is it full?

  1. Capacity = LCM(10, 15) = 30. A = +3/h, B = −2/h.
  2. Net = +1 unit/h. Time = 30 ÷ 1 = 30 hours.

Answer: 30 hours. Faster: ab/(b − a) = 150/5 = 30.

Example 3 (Medium). A pipe fills a tank in 8 hours. Because of a leak at the bottom, it takes 10 hours. How long would the leak take to empty a full tank?

  1. Capacity = LCM(8, 10) = 40. Pipe alone = 5/h. Pipe with leak = 40 ÷ 10 = 4/h (net).
  2. So the leak removes 5 − 4 = 1 unit/h.
  3. Leak alone: 40 ÷ 1 = 40 hours.

Answer: 40 hours. Faster: ab/(b − a) = 80/2 = 40.

Example 4 (Medium). Pipes A and B fill a tank in 12 and 15 hours, and pipe C empties it in 20 hours. All three are opened together. When is the tank full?

  1. Capacity = LCM(12, 15, 20) = 60. A = +5, B = +4, C = −3 per hour (see the diagram).
  2. Net = 6 units/h. Time = 60 ÷ 6 = 10 hours.

Answer: 10 hours.

Example 5 (Medium). Pipes A and B fill a tank in 20 and 30 minutes. Both are opened, and A is closed after 6 minutes. How much longer does B take to fill the tank?

  1. Capacity = 60. A = 3/min, B = 2/min.
  2. First 6 minutes: 6 × 5 = 30 units.
  3. Left: 30 units, B alone: 30 ÷ 2 = 15 minutes.

Answer: 15 more minutes.

Example 6 (Hard). A leak can empty a full tank in 6 hours. An inlet pipe filling 4 litres a minute is opened, and now the full tank empties in 8 hours. What is the capacity?

  1. Work in minutes: 6 h = 360 min, 8 h = 480 min. Let capacity = C litres.
  2. Leak rate = C/360 L/min. With the inlet, net emptying = C/480 L/min.
  3. Leak − inlet = net: C/360 − 4 = C/480.
  4. C (1/360 − 1/480) = 4. LCM(360, 480) = 1440, so C (4 − 3)/1440 = 4, C = 5760.

Answer: 5760 litres.

Common trap

Forgetting the minus sign for an outlet or leak. Also check the question: "time to fill" vs "time to empty". If the tank starts full and the outlet is stronger, you are computing emptying time.

Common trap

Mixing hours and minutes. In litre problems, convert everything to the same unit before building the equation.

16. Time, Speed & Distance

In plain words

Speed tells you how much distance you cover in one unit of time. Every question here is built on one line: distance = speed × time. If you know any two, you get the third. Harder questions just add a twist: two people moving at once (relative speed), or a trip split into parts (average speed).

Two friends walking towards each other in a corridor close the gap using both their steps, so the gap shrinks at the sum of their speeds. If one chases the other, the gap shrinks only by the difference. That one picture solves most "meeting" and "chasing" questions.

  1. The two legs have equal distance, so use 2xy/(x + y).

  2. Same direction, so relative speed = 12 − 10 = 2 km/h.
  3. Catch-up time = 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5 hours after the policeman starts.

Answer: 2 1/2 hours.

Example 5 (Medium). Walking at 4 km/h a student reaches school 10 minutes late. Walking at 5 km/h he is 5 minutes early. How far is the school?

  2. D/4 − D/5 = 1/4, so D/20 = 1/4 and D = 5.

Answer: 5 km. Formula: (4 × 5 / 1) × 1/4 = 5.

Example 6 (Hard). Walking at 3/4 of his usual speed, a man is 20 minutes late. What is his usual time?

Average speed is not (x + y)/2 when the distances are equal. You spend more time at the slower speed, so the average leans towards it. 40 and 60 give 48, not 50. Use (x + y)/2 only when the times are equal.

Common trap

Mixing units. If speed is in km/h and time in minutes, convert first. Late and early minutes must be added to get the time difference.

17. Problems on Trains

In plain words

Train problems are speed-distance problems where the train has length. To "cross" something, the train's front must travel until its back has cleared the object. So the distance covered = train length + length of whatever it crosses.

Walking your whole body through a door: your nose reaches the door first, but you have not "passed" it until your heel is through. For a long train, the heel is hundreds of metres behind the nose.

Key formulas & rules

Train crosses…DistanceSpeed used
A pole, a standing man, a signal postL (train length)train speed
A platform, bridge or tunnel of length PL + Ptrain speed
Man walking at b, same directionLa − b
Man walking at b, opposite directionLa + b

Another train, same directionL1 + L2a − b
Key formulas
  • Time = distance ÷ speed, with speed in m/s (× 5/18) because lengths are in metres.
  • Two trains start at the same time from P and Q towards each other and, after meeting, take t1 and t2 hours to finish: S1 : S2 = √t2 : √t1.

Shortcuts

Shortcut: pole time and platform time

If a train takes t1 seconds to pass a pole and t2 seconds to pass a platform of length P, the extra time t2 − t1 is spent covering just P. So speed = P / (t2 − t1), and then L = speed × t1.

Shortcut: two trains

Always add the lengths. Only the speed changes: add speeds for opposite directions, subtract for the same direction. Convert the relative speed once, not each speed separately.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). A 150 m train runs at 54 km/h. How long does it take to pass a pole?

  1. 54 km/h = 15 m/s.
  2. Distance = 150 m. Time = 150 ÷ 15 = 10 s.

Answer: 10 seconds.

  1. Speed = 20 m/s. Distance = 240 + 360 = 600 m.
  2. Time = 600 ÷ 20 = 30 s.

  1. The extra 10 seconds are spent covering the 100 m platform, so speed = 100 ÷ 10 = 10 m/s.
  2. Length = 10 × 15 = 150 m.
  3. Speed in km/h = 10 × 18/5 = 36.

Answer: 150 m, 36 km/h.

Example 4 (Medium). Trains of 120 m and 180 m run at 50 km/h and 40 km/h. How long do they take to cross each other (a) in opposite directions, (b) in the same direction?

  1. Distance in both cases = 120 + 180 = 300 m.
  2. Opposite: relative speed = 90 km/h = 25 m/s. Time = 300 ÷ 25 = 12 s.
  3. Same direction: relative speed = 10 km/h = 25/9 m/s. Time = 300 × 9/25 = 108 s.

Example 5 (Medium). A 200 m train passes a man walking at 6 km/h in the same direction in 20 s. Find the train's speed.

  1. Relative speed = 200 ÷ 20 = 10 m/s = 36 km/h.
  2. Same direction, so train speed − 6 = 36, train speed = 42 km/h.

Answer: 42 km/h.

Example 6 (Hard). Two trains start at the same time from Howrah and Patna towards each other. After meeting, they take 9 hours and 16 hours to reach their destinations. Find the ratio of their speeds.

18. Boats & Streams

In plain words

A boat has its own speed in still water (call it u). A river flows at its own speed (call it v). Going with the flow (downstream), the river pushes the boat, so speeds add. Going against the flow (upstream), the river holds it back, so speeds subtract.

A moving walkway at the airport. Walk along it and you zoom (your speed + belt speed). Walk against it and you crawl (your speed − belt speed). Your own walking speed is the average of the two, and the belt speed is half the difference.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas
  • Downstream speed D = u + v
  • Upstream speed U = u − v
  • Boat speed u = (D + U) / 2
  • Stream speed v = (D − U) / 2
  • Round trip of distance d each way: total time = d/(u + v) + d/(u − v) = 2du / (u² − v²).
  • If upstream takes k times as long as downstream for the same distance, then (u + v)/(u − v) = k, which gives u : v = (k + 1) : (k − 1).
  • "Still water speed" means the boat's own speed u. "Speed of current / stream / river" means v.

Shortcuts

Shortcut: half-sum, half-difference

Given downstream and upstream speeds, boat = half their sum, stream = half their difference. If you are given distances and times, first turn each into a speed (distance ÷ time), then apply this.

Shortcut: "k times as long upstream"

Boat : stream = (k + 1) : (k − 1). Twice as long means 3 : 1; three times as long means 4 : 2 = 2 : 1.

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). A boat moves at 10 km/h in still water and the stream flows at 2 km/h. Find its downstream and upstream speeds.

  1. Downstream = 10 + 2 = 12 km/h.
  2. Upstream = 10 − 2 = 8 km/h.

Answer: 12 km/h and 8 km/h.

Example 2 (Easy). A boat goes downstream at 16 km/h and upstream at 10 km/h. Find the boat's speed and the stream's speed.

  1. Boat = (16 + 10)/2 = 13 km/h.
  2. Stream = (16 − 10)/2 = 3 km/h.

Answer: boat 13 km/h, stream 3 km/h.

Example 3 (Medium). A boat covers 30 km downstream in 2 hours and 18 km upstream in 3 hours. Find the speeds of the boat and the stream.

  1. D = 30 ÷ 2 = 15 km/h. U = 18 ÷ 3 = 6 km/h.
  2. Boat = (15 + 6)/2 = 10.5 km/h. Stream = (15 − 6)/2 = 4.5 km/h.

Answer: 10.5 km/h and 4.5 km/h.

Example 4 (Medium). A boat's speed is 15 km/h and the stream's is 3 km/h. How long does it take to go 36 km downstream and come back?

  1. D = 18 km/h, U = 12 km/h.
  2. Time = 36/18 + 36/12 = 2 + 3 = 5 hours.

Answer: 5 hours.

Example 5 (Medium). A man takes three times as long to row a distance upstream as downstream. Find the ratio of his speed in still water to the stream's speed.

  1. Same distance, so speeds are in inverse ratio of times: (u + v) : (u − v) = 3 : 1.
  2. u + v = 3u − 3v, so 2u = 4v and u = 2v.

Answer: 2 : 1. Shortcut: (3 + 1) : (3 − 1) = 4 : 2 = 2 : 1.

  2. d/6 + d/4 = 1. LCM 12: (2d + 3d)/12 = 1, so 5d = 12.
  3. d = 2.4 km.

Common trap

Mixing up "speed of the boat" with "downstream speed". If the question says "in still water", that is u. If it gives distance and time for a downstream trip, that gives D, not u.

Common trap

For a round trip, the average speed is not u. The slow upstream leg takes longer, so the average is (u² − v²)/u, which is less than u.

19. Races & Circular Tracks

In plain words

Race questions are speed-ratio questions in sports language. "A beats B by 20 m in a 100 m race" means when A finishes 100 m, B has run only 80 m. They ran for the same time, so their speeds are in the ratio 100 : 80.

On a circular track, runners keep going round, so the question becomes: how long until the faster one gains (or together they cover) one full lap?

Two kids on a school running track. The faster kid "laps" the slower one each time she gets exactly one full track-length ahead. If they run in opposite directions, they bump into each other each time their combined running adds up to one lap.

Key formulas & rules

PhraseMeaning (race of length L)
A beats B by x mWhen A runs L, B runs L − x. Speed A : B = L : (L − x)
A beats B by t secondsB finishes t seconds after A. B's time = A's time + t
A beats B by x m or t sB covers x m in t s, so B's speed = x / t
A gives B a start of x mB starts x m ahead; B runs only L − x
Dead heatBoth reach the finish at the same moment
Key formulas: circular track of length L, speeds a > b
  • First meeting, same direction = L / (a − b)
  • First meeting, opposite directions = L / (a + b)
  • First meeting at the starting point = LCM of lap times (L/a, L/b, …). Direction does not matter.
  • Number of different meeting points: reduce a : b to lowest terms p : q. Same direction: p − q points. Opposite: p + q points.

Shortcuts

Shortcut: chain races

"A beats B by 20 m, B beats C by 25 m in 100 m": take A = 100, then B = 80, and C runs 75% of B, so C = 60. A beats C by 40 m. Multiply the fractions; do not add the margins.

Shortcut: scale the race

The winning margin scales with the race length. A beats B by 10 m in 100 m means A beats B by 100 m in 1 km (speeds are fixed, so 10%).

Solved examples

Example 1 (Easy). In a 100 m race A beats B by 10 m. By how much will A beat B in a 1 km race?

  1. Speed A : B = 100 : 90 = 10 : 9.
  2. When A runs 1000 m, B runs 900 m.

Answer: 100 m.

Example 2 (Medium). In a 200 m race A beats B by 20 m or 5 seconds. Find A's time.

  1. B runs 20 m in 5 s, so B's speed = 4 m/s.
  2. B's time for 200 m = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 s.
  3. A finishes 5 s earlier: 45 s.

Answer: 45 seconds.

Example 3 (Medium). A runs 1 km in 3 min 10 s and B in 3 min 20 s. By how many metres does A beat B?

  1. A takes 190 s, B takes 200 s, so A wins by 10 s.
  2. B's speed = 1000/200 = 5 m/s. In 10 s B covers 50 m.

Answer: 50 m.

Example 4 (Medium). In a 100 m race A beats B by 20 m and B beats C by 25 m. By how much does A beat C?

  1. When A = 100, B = 80.
  2. B : C = 100 : 75, so when B = 80, C = 80 × 75/100 = 60.
  3. A beats C by 100 − 60 = 40 m.

Answer: 40 m, not 45.

Example 5 (Medium). A's speed is 5/3 times B's. How much start should A give B in a 200 m race so that it ends in a dead heat?

  1. Speed A : B = 5 : 3. In the time A runs 200 m, B runs 200 × 3/5 = 120 m.
  2. For a dead heat, B must only need 120 m, so B starts 80 m ahead.

Answer: 80 m.

Example 6 (Hard). A and B run on a 600 m circular track at 5 m/s and 3 m/s from the same point. When do they first meet (a) running in the same direction, (b) in opposite directions, (c) at the starting point?

  1. Same direction: A must gain one full lap. 600 ÷ 2 = 300 s.
  2. Opposite: together they must cover one lap. 600 ÷ 8 = 75 s.
  3. Start point: A is back at start every 120 s, B every 200 s. Both are there together at LCM(120, 200) = 600 s.

Answer: (a) 300 s, (b) 75 s, (c) 600 s = 10 minutes.

Common trap

In chain races, do not add margins. 20 m + 25 m = 45 m is wrong; the second margin applies to B's shorter distance, giving 40 m.

Common trap

"Meet anywhere" and "meet at the start" are different questions. The first uses relative speed; the second uses the LCM of lap times. Also convert km/h to m/s before dividing a track length in metres.

20. Permutations & Combinations

In plain words

This chapter is about counting ways without writing every way down. A permutation is an arrangement, where order matters (who sits first, second, third). A combination is a selection, where order does not matter (which three people are picked for a team).

The one question to ask every time: "If I swap two chosen items, do I get a new outcome?" If yes, it is a permutation. If no, it is a combination.

A lock code 4-7-2 is different from 2-7-4, so a "combination lock" is really a permutation lock. A fruit salad of apple, mango and banana is the same salad in any order, so that is a true combination.

  3. Units place: 8 choices left.

  3. A necklace can also be flipped, so clockwise and anticlockwise are the same: 120/2 = 60.

Forgetting that 0 cannot be the first digit of a number, and forgetting to divide by the repeats in words with repeated letters (APPLE has two P's, so 5!/2! = 60, not 120).

21. Probability

In plain words

Probability is a number from 0 to 1 that says how likely something is. 0 means it can never happen, 1 means it is certain. For fair, equally likely outcomes it is simply favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.

So almost every probability question is really a counting question (chapter 20) done twice: count the outcomes you want, count all outcomes, divide.

A bag has 3 mango toffees and 7 orange toffees. Close your eyes and pick one: the chance of mango is 3 out of 10 = 0.3. If you did it 1,000 times (putting it back each time), you would get mango about 300 times.

  3. P(hit) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6.

22. Linear & Quadratic Equations

In plain words

An equation is a balance: whatever is on the left equals what is on the right. A linear equation has the unknown only to the power 1 (like 2x + 3 = 11) and has one answer. A quadratic has x² as its highest power (like x² − 5x + 6 = 0) and has two answers, called roots.

With two unknowns (x and y) you need two equations. You solve them by removing one unknown at a time.

A shopkeeper says "2 pens and 3 pencils cost ₹34, 3 pens and 2 pencils cost ₹41". Two clues, two unknown prices. Each clue alone is not enough, but together they pin down both prices.

Solved examples

Example 1. Solve x + y = 10 and x − y = 4.

  2. Put x = 7 in the first: 7 + y = 10, so y = 3.

Example 2. Solve 3x + 2y = 16 and 2x − y = 6.

  1. From the second equation, y = 2x − 6 (substitution).
  2. Put it in the first: 3x + 2(2x − 6) = 16 → 7x − 12 = 16 → x = 4.
  3. y = 2(4) − 6 = 2.

Answer: x = 4, y = 2.

Example 3. 2 pens and 3 pencils cost ₹34; 3 pens and 2 pencils cost ₹41. Find the price of one pen.

  1. Let pen = p, pencil = q. Then 2p + 3q = 34 and 3p + 2q = 41.
  2. Add: 5p + 5q = 75 → p + q = 15.
  3. Subtract (second − first): p − q = 7.
  4. Add these: 2p = 22 → p = 11, and q = 4.

Answer: pen ₹11 (pencil ₹4).

Example 4. Find the roots of x² − 5x + 6 = 0 and of 2x² + 3x − 2 = 0.

  1. x² − 5x + 6: two numbers with product 6 and sum −5 are −2 and −3. So (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0 → x = 2 or 3.
  2. 2x² + 3x − 2: use the formula. D = 3² − 4(2)(−2) = 9 + 16 = 25, √D = 5.
  3. x = (−3 ± 5)/4 → x = 2/4 = 1/2 or x = −8/4 = −2.

Answer: 2 and 3; 1/2 and −2.

Example 5. If α and β are the roots of 3x² − 12x + 7 = 0, find α² + β².

  1. Sum α + β = −b/a = 12/3 = 4. Product αβ = c/a = 7/3.
  2. α² + β² = (α + β)² − 2αβ = 16 − 14/3 = 34/3.

Answer: 34/3. Notice we never found α and β themselves.

Example 6. For what value of k does kx² + 4x + 1 = 0 have equal roots?

  1. Equal roots means D = 0.
  2. D = 4² − 4(k)(1) = 16 − 4k = 0 → k = 4.

Answer: k = 4 (the equation becomes 4x² + 4x + 1 = (2x + 1)², root −1/2 twice).

Common trap

Sign of the sum of roots. It is −b/a, not b/a. For x² − 7x + 12 the sum is +7. Also, if a ≠ 1, divide by a: for 2x² − 8x + 5 the sum is 8/2 = 4, not 8.

Common trap

Dividing both sides by x. In x² = 5x, dividing by x loses the root x = 0. Always bring everything to one side and factorise: x(x − 5) = 0 → x = 0 or 5.

23. Progressions: AP & GP

In plain words

A progression is a list of numbers that follows a fixed rule. In an Arithmetic Progression (AP) you add the same number each time (2, 5, 8, 11 … add 3). In a Geometric Progression (GP) you multiply by the same number each time (3, 6, 12, 24 … multiply by 2).

The added number is the common difference d. The multiplier is the common ratio r. The first term is always called a.

Saving ₹500 more than last month every month is an AP. A bank balance that grows by 10% every year is a GP. That is why compound interest (chapter 8) is really a GP.

24. Logarithms

In plain words

A logarithm answers one question: "what power do I need?" log₂ 8 asks "2 to what power gives 8?" The answer is 3, because 2³ = 8. So a log is just an exponent written the other way round.

When no base is written (log 100), the base is 10. log 100 = 2 because 10² = 100.

Think of folding a sheet of paper: each fold doubles the layers. "How many folds give 32 layers?" is log₂ 32 = 5. The log counts the number of doublings (or tenfoldings, for base 10).

26. 3D Mensuration

In plain words

3D mensuration measures solid objects. The volume is how much space is inside (in cm³ or m³; 1 m³ = 1000 litres). The surface area is how much material covers the outside (in cm² or m²). Curved surface area (CSA) is only the curved part; total surface area (TSA) adds the flat ends too.

The biggest idea: when a solid is melted and recast, the volume stays the same. Only the shape changes.

A tin of paint: the paint inside is the volume, the label wrapped around the tin is the curved surface area, and the label plus the lid plus the bottom is the total surface area.

Using the vertical height h instead of the slant height l in the cone's surface area. CSA = πrl; volume uses h. Find l with l² = r² + h² first.

Common trap

Forgetting the flat face of a hemisphere. A solid hemisphere's TSA is 3πr² (curved 2πr² + flat πr²). A hollow bowl has only 2πr².

27. Data Interpretation

In plain words

Data Interpretation (DI) gives you a table or a chart and asks 3–5 questions about it. The maths is only percentages, averages and ratios (chapters 6, 9 and 11). The real skill is reading the chart correctly and calculating fast.

The four common formats are a table, a bar chart, a line chart and a pie chart. Every set is one picture and several quick questions, so a careful first read pays off for all of them.

It is like reading your electricity bill: the numbers are all there, and the questions are "how much more than last month?", "what share went to the AC?", "what is the monthly average?".

Shortcut

Learn tables and squares cold: tables to 20, squares to 30, cubes to 15, fractions 1/2 to 1/12 as percents. These alone save 10–20 seconds on many questions.

Shortcut

Work backwards from the options in equations, ages, numbers and "find the value" questions. Plugging in often beats solving.

Test Strategy & Learning Path

In plain words

Knowing the maths is half the job. The other half is speed, choosing which questions to attempt, and not panicking. Most aptitude tests give about 1 minute per question, and many questions are designed to eat 3 minutes if you let them.

This section tells you how to attack a 60-question, 60-minute paper, what order to learn topics in, an 8-week plan, and the speed you should aim for in each topic.

A test is like shopping with a fixed budget of 60 minutes. You buy the cheap, sure items first (easy questions), and only spend the leftover budget on expensive ones.

How to attempt a 60-question / 60-minute test

Read the rules (negative marking? sectional timer?) → Pass 1: easy questions only → Pass 2: medium questions → Pass 3: educated guesses (only if no negative marking) → last 2 minutes: check nothing is left unmarked
  1. Pass 1 (about 30 minutes): go through every question. If you can see the method in 10 seconds and finish in under a minute, solve it. Otherwise mark it and move on. Aim for 30–35 correct answers here.
  2. Pass 2 (about 20 minutes): return to the marked questions. Pick those where you know the method but the calculation is long. Use options, approximation and elimination.
  3. Pass 3 (about 8 minutes): the hardest remaining ones. Eliminate 2 options and guess if there is no negative marking. If there is (for example −1/4 per wrong), guess only when you have removed at least 2 options.
  4. Final 2 minutes: make sure every answer you meant to mark is actually marked.
  5. The 90-second rule: if you are stuck for 90 seconds, leave it. One stubborn question can cost you three easy ones.
  6. DI sets: read the chart once carefully (30–45 seconds), then answer all its questions together. Do not jump between sets.
Common trap

Solving in question order. Tests mix easy and hard questions on purpose. Many companies (TCS NQT, AMCAT, Cognizant) use sectional timers and sometimes no going back within a section. Check this in the instructions and adjust: with no going back, decide on each question in 10 seconds: solve now or guess and move on.

Shortcut

Accuracy first, then speed. In weeks 1–4 do not use a timer. Once you get 8 out of 10 right in a topic, start timing. Speed built on a wrong method only makes you wrong faster.

Shortcut

Target score, not full score. In most placement tests, 70–75% correct with high accuracy clears the cut-off. Attempting 45 questions with 42 right beats attempting 60 with 40 right when there is negative marking.

Common trap

Only reading solutions. Watching someone solve a problem feels like learning, but in the test you must produce the method yourself. Always try first for at least 2 minutes, then read the solution, then solve it again without looking.
