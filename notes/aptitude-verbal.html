Part 1 — Grammar

1. Parts of Speech in Brief

In plain words

Every word in a sentence has a job. Some words name things (nouns), some stand in for names (pronouns), some show action (verbs), some describe (adjectives and adverbs), and some join or link (prepositions and conjunctions). These jobs are called the parts of speech.

Why this matters for a test: almost every grammar question is really asking "is the right kind of word in the right place?" For example, "He sings good" is wrong because a verb needs an adverb (well), not an adjective.

Think of a cricket team. The same player can bat or bowl, but at any moment he has one job. The word "fast" is the same: in "a fast car" it describes a noun (adjective), in "he runs fast" it describes a verb (adverb). Look at the job, not the word.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Linking verbs (be, seem, appear, look, feel, smell, taste, sound, become) are followed by an adjective, because the word describes the subject, not the action: "The soup tastes good", "She feels bad".

Pronoun case: after a preposition (between, with, for, to, of) always use the object form: between you and me, with him, to whom.

Comparison: one-syllable adjectives take -er / -est (tall, taller, tallest); long ones take more / most (more beautiful). Never both together: "more better", "most tallest" are always wrong.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

The "-ly" test. If the blank describes how someone does an action, you need an adverb (usually -ly, or well, fast, hard). If it describes the subject after a linking verb, you need an adjective. Ask: "Is it about the doer or the doing?"

Shortcut

The "drop the partner" test for pronouns. In "between you and ___" or "He invited Ravi and ___", drop the other person: "He invited me" sounds right, "He invited I" does not. So the answer is me.

Shortcut

The he/him test for who/whom. Answer the question in your head. "To ___ did you give it?" becomes "I gave it to him". Him ends in m, so pick whom. If the answer is he, pick who.

Solved examples

1. What part of speech is "fast" in "She drives fast"?

  1. Find what "fast" describes. It tells us how she drives.
  2. "Drives" is a verb. A word that describes a verb is an adverb.

Answer: adverb. (In "a fast car" the same word is an adjective, because it describes the noun "car".)

2. Correct the sentence: "He did the job very good."

  1. "Good" is describing how he did the job, that is, the verb "did".
  2. A verb needs an adverb. The adverb form of good is well.

Answer: "He did the job very well." Rule: adverb modifies a verb.

3. Choose: "The milk smells ___." (sour / sourly)

  1. "Smells" here is a linking verb: the milk is not doing any smelling; we are describing the milk.
  2. After a linking verb, use an adjective.

Answer: sour. Rule: linking verb + adjective.

4. Choose: "This secret is between you and ___." (I / me)

  1. "Between" is a preposition.
  2. A pronoun after a preposition takes the object form.

Answer: me. Shortcut: "between us" (not "between we") shows the object form is needed.

5. Find the error: "This is the most tallest building in the city."

  1. "Tallest" is already the superlative (-est).
  2. Adding "most" makes a double superlative, which is always wrong.

Answer: "This is the tallest building in the city." Rule: no double comparison.

Common trap

"Hard" vs "hardly". They are different words, not the adjective and adverb pair. Hard = with effort ("He works hard"). Hardly = almost not ("He hardly works" means he does very little work). The same goes for late (not early) and lately (recently).

Common trap

Uncountable nouns have no plural. Information, advice, furniture, luggage, equipment, news and scenery never take -s and never take "a/an". Say "a piece of advice", "some information", "the news is good".

Practice set

EasyIn "She quickly finished the work", the word "quickly" is (a) an adjective (b) an adverb (c) a verb (d) a noun

Answer: (b) an adverb

It tells us how she finished, so it describes the verb "finished". Rule: a word that modifies a verb is an adverb.

EasyIn "The book is on the table", the word "on" is (a) a preposition (b) an adverb (c) a conjunction (d) a pronoun

Answer: (a) a preposition

"On" links the noun "table" to the rest of the sentence and shows place. Rule: prepositions show place, time or direction before a noun.

EasyChoose the correct word: "This matter is between you and ___." (a) I (b) mine (c) myself (d) me

Answer: (d) me

"Between" is a preposition, and a pronoun after a preposition is in the object form. Rule: preposition + object pronoun.

MediumChoose the correct word: "He sings ___." (a) good (b) goodly (c) well (d) more good

Answer: (c) well

The word describes how he sings (the verb), so an adverb is needed; the adverb of "good" is "well". Rule: action verb + adverb.

MediumChoose the correct word: "The flowers smell ___." (a) sweetly (b) sweet (c) sweetness (d) sweeten

Answer: (b) sweet

"Smell" is used as a linking verb here; the word describes the flowers, not an action. Rule: linking verb + adjective.

MediumChoose the correct form: "Ravi is ___ than his brother." (a) smarter (b) smartest (c) more smarter (d) most smart

Answer: (a) smarter

"Than" signals a comparison of two, so we need the comparative. "More smarter" is a double comparative. Rule: comparative with "than"; never use more + -er together.

MediumIn "Please light the lamp", the word "light" is (a) a noun (b) an adjective (c) an adverb (d) a verb

Answer: (d) a verb

It is the action being asked for (an order: "light it"). Rule: the job in the sentence decides the part of speech, not the word itself.

HardIn "She works hard", the word "hard" is (a) an adjective (b) a noun (c) an adverb (d) a verb

Answer: (c) an adverb

It tells us how she works, so it describes the verb. "Hard" is both the adjective and the adverb form ("hardly" means something else). Rule: modifier of a verb is an adverb.

HardFind the part with the error: "The team played (a) / very good (b) / in the final match (c) / No error (d)"

Answer: (b)

It should be "very well": the word describes how the team played (a verb). Rule: action verb + adverb.

HardChoose the correct word: "To ___ did you give the letter?" (a) whom (b) who (c) whose (d) which

Answer: (a) whom

The pronoun comes after the preposition "to", so the object form is needed. Check: "I gave it to him" (m ending) means whom. Rule: preposition + object pronoun (whom).

2. Tenses

In plain words

A tense tells us when an action happens (past, present or future) and what shape it has: a simple fact, an action in progress, an action completed before some point, or an action that has been going on for a while.

3 times x 4 shapes = 12 tenses. Once you learn the 4 shapes for one time, the other two times follow the same pattern with a different helping verb.

Think of photos and videos. Simple is a photo (one fact). Continuous is a video playing right now. Perfect is a photo of something already done ("the plate is empty"). Perfect continuous is a long video that is still recording ("I have been cooking for two hours").

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Definite past time word (yesterday, last week, in 2019, ago) = simple past, never present perfect. "I saw him yesterday", not "I have seen him yesterday".

After "did / does / do", use V1: "He did not go", not "did not went".

Time / condition clause (if, when, unless, as soon as, before) takes present for future: "If it rains tomorrow, we will stay home."

Since + point of time, for + length of time, both with perfect tenses: since Monday, for three days.

No sooner had ... than; Hardly / Scarcely had ... when.

Stative verbs (know, believe, understand, like, love, want, own, belong, seem) are not normally used in continuous forms: "I know him", not "I am knowing him".

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Hunt for the time word first. "yesterday / last / ago / in 2015" means simple past. "since / for" means a perfect (continuous) tense. "now / look! / listen!" means present continuous. "by the time / before + past" means past perfect for the earlier action. "by + future time" means future perfect. The time word usually decides the answer in five seconds.

Shortcut

Two past actions? Put "had" on the one that happened first. The later one stays in simple past: "The patient had died before the doctor arrived."

Solved examples

1. Correct: "I have seen this movie last week."

  1. Spot the time word: "last week" is a definite past time.
  2. Definite past time needs simple past, not present perfect.

Answer: "I saw this movie last week."

2. Fill in: "She ___ in Pune since 2020." (lives / is living / has been living)

  1. "Since 2020" gives a starting point in the past; the action is still going on.
  2. Past start + still continuing = present perfect continuous.

Answer: has been living.

3. Fill in: "When I reached the hall, the lecture ___." (started / had started / has started)

  1. Two past actions: the lecture starting and my reaching.
  2. The lecture started first, so it takes past perfect.

Answer: had started.

4. Correct: "If you will study hard, you will pass."

  1. "If" starts a condition clause about the future.
  2. In such clauses we use the simple present, and "will" goes only in the main clause.

Answer: "If you study hard, you will pass."

5. Fill in: "Hardly ___ the match begun when it started raining." (has / had / did)

  1. "Hardly ... when" is a fixed pair used with the past perfect.
  2. The inverted form is "Hardly had + subject + V3".

Answer: had. Faster: memorise the pairs "No sooner had ... than" and "Hardly/Scarcely had ... when".

Common trap

"Did" + V2. "He did not came", "Did you saw it?" are wrong. The helping verb "did" already carries the past, so the main verb goes back to V1: "He did not come", "Did you see it?"

Common trap

Continuous with stative verbs. "I am understanding", "She is having a car", "This bag is belonging to me" are wrong. Use simple forms: I understand, she has a car, this bag belongs to me. (But "having lunch" is fine: there "have" is an action, meaning eat.)

Common trap

"Since" with a length of time. "since three years" is wrong; say "for three years" or "since 2023".

Practice set

Easy"She ___ to school every day." (a) go (b) going (c) goes (d) gone

Answer: (c) goes

"Every day" shows a habit, so simple present; the subject "she" is third person singular, so add -s/-es. Rule: habits take simple present.

Easy"I ___ him yesterday." (a) have met (b) met (c) meet (d) had been meeting

Answer: (b) met

"Yesterday" is a definite past time. Rule: definite past time takes simple past, never present perfect.

Easy"Look! The baby ___." (a) is sleeping (b) sleeps (c) slept (d) has slept

Answer: (a) is sleeping

"Look!" shows the action is happening at this moment. Rule: action in progress now takes present continuous.

Medium"They ___ here since 2019." (a) live (b) are living (c) lived (d) have been living

Answer: (d) have been living

"Since 2019" marks a past starting point of an action that is still going on. Rule: since/for with an ongoing action takes present perfect (continuous).

Medium"By the time we reached the station, the train ___." (a) left (b) has left (c) had left (d) leaves

Answer: (c) had left

Two past actions; the train left first. Rule: the earlier of two past actions takes past perfect.

Medium"If it ___ tomorrow, we will cancel the trip." (a) will rain (b) rains (c) rained (d) would rain

Answer: (b) rains

This is a condition about the future; "will" belongs in the main clause only. Rule: if-clause of the first conditional takes simple present.

Medium"She ___ English for five years before she moved to London." (a) had been studying (b) has studied (c) is studying (d) studies

Answer: (a) had been studying

The studying went on for a period (five years) before another past action (moved). Rule: duration before a past point takes past perfect continuous.

Hard"No sooner ___ the bell rung than the students rushed out." (a) has (b) did (c) was (d) had

Answer: (d) had

"Rung" is V3, and the fixed pattern is "No sooner had + subject + V3 ... than". Rule: No sooner had ... than.

Hard"I ___ the answer now." (a) am knowing (b) have been knowing (c) know (d) was knowing

Answer: (c) know

"Know" is a stative verb (a state of mind, not an action), so it is not used in continuous forms. Rule: stative verbs take simple tenses.

Hard"By next June, she ___ in this company for ten years." (a) will work (b) will have worked (c) works (d) has worked

Answer: (b) will have worked

"By next June" is a future deadline and "for ten years" is a duration completed by then. Rule: by + future time takes future perfect.

3. Subject–Verb Agreement

In plain words

Subject–verb agreement means a singular subject takes a singular verb (is, was, has, goes) and a plural subject takes a plural verb (are, were, have, go). The hard part is finding the real subject, because tests hide it behind phrases like "along with", "one of the" or "either ... or".

Think of a lock and key. The subject is the lock and the verb is the key. You must match the key to the lock itself, not to the keychain hanging next to it. In "The box of chocolates is on the table", the lock is "box" (one box); "chocolates" is just the keychain.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Cross out the middle. Put brackets around any phrase that starts with of, with, along with, as well as, together with, besides, including, in addition to. Read the sentence without it. "The box [of chocolates] ___" becomes "The box ___", clearly is.

Shortcut

"THE number" is one number, "A number" is many. Replace "a number of" with "many": "Many students are absent". Replace "the number of" with "the count": "The count is rising".

Shortcut

Each / every / any / no + body/one/thing is always singular. Everyone, somebody, nothing, each of, either of, neither of: all take is / has / was.

Solved examples

1. "Either the manager or his assistants ___ responsible." (is / are)

  1. "Either ... or" joins two subjects, so look at the nearer one.
  2. The nearer subject is "assistants", which is plural.

Answer: are.

2. "The quality of the mangoes ___ poor." (was / were)

  1. Bracket "of the mangoes". The sentence becomes "The quality ___ poor".
  2. "Quality" is singular.

Answer: was. Trap: "mangoes" sits right next to the verb to fool you.

3. "The Prime Minister, as well as his ministers, ___ attending the meeting." (is / are)

  1. Bracket "as well as his ministers".
  2. The real subject is "The Prime Minister", singular.

Answer: is.

4. "One of the students ___ won a scholarship." (has / have)

  1. The subject is "one", not "students"; "of the students" is a phrase that just tells us the group.
  2. "One" is singular.

Answer: has.

5. "Three-fourths of the work ___ done, but three-fourths of the workers ___ gone home." (is, have / are, has)

  1. With fractions, look at the noun after "of".
  2. "Work" is uncountable (singular), so is; "workers" is plural, so have.

Answer: is, have.

6. "He is one of those people who never ___ up." (give / gives)

  1. "Who" refers back to the nearest noun, "people", not to "one".
  2. "People" is plural, so the verb in the who-clause is plural.

Answer: give. Compare: "He is the only one of those people who never gives up", where "who" refers to "the only one".

Common trap

"With" does not make a plural. "The teacher with her students are going" is wrong; "with" phrases are extra, so it is "The teacher with her students is going". Only and joins two subjects into a plural.

Common trap

"Each" after a plural subject does not change it. "They each have a car" is correct (subject "they"), but "Each of them has a car" (subject "each"). See which word is really the subject.

Practice set

Easy"Neither the teacher nor the students ___ present." (a) was (b) has been (c) is (d) were

Answer: (d) were

With "neither ... nor" the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "students", which is plural; the sentence is in the past. Rule: neither/nor takes the verb of the nearer subject.

Easy"Each of the boys ___ a prize." (a) have got (b) have (c) has (d) are having

Answer: (c) has

The subject is "each", which is always singular. Rule: each / every / either / neither of take a singular verb.

Easy"One of my friends ___ a doctor." (a) are (b) is (c) were (d) have been

Answer: (b) is

The subject is "one", singular; "of my friends" only names the group. Rule: one of + plural noun takes a singular verb.

Medium"The number of students in the college ___ increasing." (a) is (b) have been (c) are (d) were

Answer: (a) is

"The number" is one quantity. Rule: "the number of" takes a singular verb.

Medium"A number of students ___ absent today." (a) is (b) has been (c) was (d) are

Answer: (d) are

"A number of" means "many", so the subject is plural; "today" keeps it in the present. Rule: "a number of" takes a plural verb.

Medium"The captain, along with his players, ___ arrived." (a) have (b) are (c) has (d) were

Answer: (c) has

Skip the "along with" phrase; the subject is "the captain", singular. Rule: along with / as well as / with do not change the number of the subject.

Medium"Either my brothers or my father ___ going to pick me up." (a) are (b) is (c) were (d) have

Answer: (b) is

With "either ... or" the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "my father", singular. Rule: either/or takes the verb of the nearer subject.

Hard"The committee ___ submitted its report." (a) has (b) have (c) are (d) were

Answer: (a) has

The committee acts as one body, shown by "its". Rule: a collective noun acting as a single unit takes a singular verb.

Hard"Mathematics ___ my favourite subject, and the cattle ___ grazing in the field." (a) are, is (b) is, is (c) are, are (d) is, are

Answer: (d) is, are

"Mathematics" is a subject name that looks plural but is singular; "cattle" is always plural. Rule: names of subjects in -ics are singular; cattle/police/people are plural.

Hard"Bread and butter ___ my usual breakfast, but Ravi and Meena ___ eggs." (a) are, prefer (b) are, prefers (c) is, prefer (d) is, prefers

Answer: (c) is, prefer

"Bread and butter" is one dish (one idea), so singular; "Ravi and Meena" are two people joined by "and", so plural. Rule: two nouns forming one idea take a singular verb; two separate subjects joined by "and" take a plural verb.

4. Articles & Prepositions

In plain words

Articles are the three small words a, an and the. "A/an" points to any one thing ("a pen"); "the" points to a particular thing both of you know ("the pen I gave you"). Sometimes no article is used at all.

Prepositions (in, on, at, to, of, with...) link a noun to the rest of the sentence. Many verbs and adjectives come with a fixed preposition that you simply have to remember: interested in, married to, afraid of.

"A" is like saying "give me a chair" in a room full of chairs: any chair will do. "The" is like saying "give me the chair near the window": only one chair fits. Fixed prepositions are like a person's surname: "interested" always travels with "in", the way a first name travels with its family name.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Abbreviations read letter by letter: F, H, L, M, N, R, S, X start with a vowel sound ("ef", "aitch", "el", "em", "en", "ar", "es", "ex"), so they take an: an FIR, an MLA, an SP, an X-ray, an NGO.

Shortcut

Latin "-ior" words take "to", never "than": senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior, anterior, posterior. "He is senior to me." Also "prefer ... to" and "preferable to".

Shortcut

When a noun is "known" to both people, use "the". If you can answer "which one?", use "the". If the answer is "any one", use "a/an".

Solved examples

1. Fill in: "He waited for ___ hour and then bought ___ umbrella and ___ uniform."

  1. "Hour": the h is silent, first sound is a vowel sound, so an.
  2. "Umbrella": first sound "uh", a vowel sound, so an.
  3. "Uniform": first sound "yoo", a consonant sound, so a.

Answer: an, an, a.

2. Correct: "Sun rises in east."

  1. "Sun" is unique: there is only one, so it takes the.
  2. Directions used as places ("in the east") take the.

Answer: "The sun rises in the east."

3. Correct: "She prefers coffee than tea."

  1. "Prefer" compares using to, not "than".

Answer: "She prefers coffee to tea."

4. Correct: "We reached at the station and discussed about the plan."

  1. "Reach" takes a direct object: reach the station.
  2. "Discuss" takes a direct object: discuss the plan.

Answer: "We reached the station and discussed the plan."

5. Fill in: "She has been married ___ an engineer ___ 2015 and lives ___ Mumbai."

  1. "Married" takes to.
  2. 2015 is a point of time with a perfect tense, so since.
  3. A city takes in.

Answer: to, since, in.

Common trap

"Married with" and "married to". A person is married to someone. "Married with" only appears in phrases like "married with two children" (meaning "and has two children"), so for a partner always choose to.

Common trap

"A" before "u" and "o" words. Many students write "an university" or "an one-day match". Say them aloud: "yoo-niversity", "wun-day". Both start with consonant sounds, so it is a university, a one-day match.

Practice set

Easy"She is ___ honest girl." (a) an (b) a (c) the (d) no article

Answer: (a) an

The h in "honest" is silent, so the word begins with a vowel sound. Rule: "an" before a vowel sound.

Answer: (d) a

5. Active & Passive Voice

In plain words

In the active voice the doer comes first: "Ravi wrote a letter." In the passive voice the thing that receives the action comes first: "A letter was written by Ravi." The meaning and the tense stay exactly the same; only the focus changes.

Only verbs that have an object (transitive verbs) can be made passive. "He sleeps" has no object, so it has no passive.

Imagine a photo of a cricket shot. Active: the camera is on the batsman ("Kohli hit the ball"). Passive: the camera is on the ball ("The ball was hit by Kohli"). Same shot, same moment, different focus.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Passive = object of active + correct form of "be" + V3 + by + subject (as object form)

Pronoun swap: I → me, we → us, he → him, she → her, they → them, you → you, who → whom.

The tense of the "be" verb must match the active tense.

Shortcut

Check only the verb first. In MCQs, three options usually have the wrong tense of "be". Identify the active tense, pick its "be" form (is / is being / has been / was / was being / had been / will be / will have been) and eliminate the rest. You rarely need to read the whole sentence.

Shortcut

"Being" only comes with continuous tenses; "been" only comes with perfect tenses. If the active verb has -ing, the passive must contain "being". If the active has has/have/had, the passive must contain "been".

Solved examples

1. Change to passive: "They are building a new bridge."

  1. Tense: present continuous (are + building).
  2. Passive "be" form: is/are being + V3. The new subject "a new bridge" is singular, so is being built.
  3. Old subject "they" becomes "by them".

Answer: "A new bridge is being built by them."

2. Change to passive: "The teacher has checked the copies."

  1. Tense: present perfect (has + V3).
  2. New subject "the copies" is plural, so have been checked.

Answer: "The copies have been checked by the teacher." Note how has changed to have to agree with the new subject.

3. Change to passive: "You should respect your elders."

  1. Modal "should" + V1.
  2. Passive: modal + be + V3.

Answer: "Your elders should be respected by you."

4. Change to passive: "Shut the window."

  1. This is an imperative (an order) with no subject.
  2. Pattern: Let + object + be + V3.

Answer: "Let the window be shut."

5. Change to passive: "Who stole my pen?"

  1. "Who" is the subject, so it becomes "by whom" and moves to the front.
  2. Tense: simple past, so was + V3; question order puts "was" before the new subject.

Answer: "By whom was my pen stolen?"

Common trap

Changing the tense by mistake. "She wrote a letter" becomes "A letter was written", not "has been written" or "is written". The tense of the "be" verb must match the active verb exactly.

Common trap

Forgetting to change the pronoun case. "By he" or "by they" is wrong: after "by" use the object form: by him, by them. Going the other way (passive to active), "by me" becomes the subject I.

6. Direct & Indirect Speech

In plain words

Direct speech repeats someone's exact words inside quotation marks: He said, "I am tired." Indirect (reported) speech tells what they said in our own sentence, without quotes: He said that he was tired.

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told, asked), three things usually change: the tense moves one step back, the pronouns change to fit the new speaker, and time and place words change (now becomes then, here becomes there).

It is like telling your mother what your friend said on the phone yesterday. Your friend said "I am coming tomorrow"; you tell your mother "She said she was coming the next day." You naturally shift the words because the moment and the speaker are different now.

Key formulas

Pronouns (the SON rule): 1st person (I, we) changes to match the Subject of the reporting verb; 2nd person (you) changes to match the Object of the reporting verb; 3rd person (he, she, they) No change.

No tense change when: (1) the reporting verb is in the present or future ("He says that he is tired"); (2) the direct speech is a universal truth or habitual fact ("The teacher said that the earth moves round the sun").

Statements: said → said that; said to → told + object + that.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Eliminate by the three checks, in order: (1) Is the reporting verb right (told for commands, asked for questions)? (2) Is the tense backshifted? (3) Are the time words changed (tomorrow → the next day)? Most wrong options fail one of these three.

Shortcut

Reported questions are not questions. Any option that keeps a question mark or the question word order ("asked where did I live") is wrong. Correct: "asked where I lived".

Solved examples

  1. "Said to me" becomes "told me that".
  2. "I" refers to the speaker, so "he"; "you" refers to the listener (me), so "me".
  3. "Will" becomes "would"; "tomorrow" becomes "the next day".

Answer: He told me that he would help me the next day.

  2. "You" is the listener, "me", so "I"; "are" backshifts to "was" (for I).
  3. Statement order, no question mark.

Answer: She asked me if I was ready.

  1. It is a command, so "said to" becomes "told" (or "ordered").
  2. Negative command: not to + V1.

Answer: The teacher told the students not to talk in the class.

4. Change: He said, "I met her two days ago."

  1. Simple past "met" becomes past perfect "had met".
  2. "Ago" becomes "before".

Answer: He said that he had met her two days before.

5. Change: She says, "I am busy."

  1. The reporting verb "says" is in the present tense.
  2. So the tense inside does not change.

Answer: She says that she is busy.

Common trap

Changing a universal truth. "He said, 'Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius'" stays in the present: He said that water boils at 100 degrees Celsius. Options with "boiled" are wrong.

Common trap

"Said to" + object is not allowed with "that". "He said to me that ..." is avoided in tests; use "He told me that ...". And never "He told that ..." without an object.

7. Error Spotting

In plain words

In error spotting, a sentence is cut into three or four parts, labelled (a), (b), (c), and the last option is "No error". Exactly one part contains a grammar mistake, or none does. Your job is to find which part.

The secret is to stop reading for meaning and start checking. Almost every error in these tests comes from a short list of rules: subject–verb agreement, tense, pronoun, article, preposition, parallelism, modifier, and a few fixed pairs like "No sooner ... than".

It is like a mechanic with a checklist. He does not just listen to the car and guess; he checks brakes, then tyres, then oil, one by one. You check subject–verb, then tense, then pronoun, and so on. The error almost always shows up by the fourth check.

Key formulas & rules

CheckWhat to look forWrongRight
1. Subject–verbFind the real subject; skip "of / with / along with" phrasesThe cost of vegetables have risen.The cost of vegetables has risen.
2. TenseTime words (yesterday, since, by), did + V1, sequence of past actionsHe has come yesterday.He came yesterday.
3. PronounCase after prepositions; "myself" used as a subject; one ... one'sMy sister and myself went.My sister and I went.
4. Article / nouna/an by sound; uncountable nouns; "one of" + pluralHe gave me an advice.He gave me some advice.
5. PrepositionFixed pairs; extra preposition after discuss, reach, enterShe is good in maths.She is good at maths.
6. ParallelismItems in a list or joined by and / or / not only ... but also must have the same formHe likes reading, writing and to swim.He likes reading, writing and swimming.
7. ModifierAn opening -ing / -ed phrase must describe the subject that comes right after the commaWalking home, the rain started.Walking home, I got caught in the rain.
Extra: conjunction pairsAlthough / though ... (no but); No sooner ... than; Hardly / scarcely ... when; Not only ... but also; Unless = if not (no second "not")Although he is rich, but he is unhappy.Although he is rich, he is unhappy.

Shortcut

Read part by part, not the whole sentence at once. First find the subject and main verb (check 1 and 2); that already catches the most errors. Then scan each part for the "red flag" words: of, with, along with, each, every, one of, since, for, yesterday, although, unless, hardly, no sooner, than, between, prefer, senior.

Shortcut

Do not fear "No error". It is a real answer and does come up regularly. If you have run all seven checks and nothing fails, mark it with confidence instead of forcing a doubtful answer.

Shortcut

Red-flag word, then its partner. "Although" means check for an extra "but". "No sooner" means check for "than". "Unless" means check for an extra "not". "Prefer / senior" means check for "to". "Each / every / one of" means check for a singular verb.

Solved examples

1. "The quality of these apples (a) / are not good (b) / this season (c) / No error (d)"

  2. "Quality" is singular, so the verb must be "is".

Answer: (b), should be "is not good". Rule: subject–verb agreement, ignore the "of" phrase.

2. "He has returned (a) / from Delhi (b) / yesterday (c) / No error (d)"

  2. Check 2: "yesterday" is a definite past time, which cannot go with the present perfect "has returned".

Answer: (a), should be "He returned". Rule: definite past time takes simple past.

  2. Check 3: "myself" is a reflexive pronoun; it cannot be the subject. Drop the partner: "myself went" is clearly wrong.

Answer: (a), should be "My brother and I". Rule: reflexive pronouns are not used as subjects.

  2. Check 6: the list is intelligent (adjective), hardworking (adjective), has honesty (verb phrase). Not parallel.

Answer: (c), should be "honest". Rule: items in a list take the same grammatical form.

5. "Not only he sings (a) / but also dances (b) / very well (c) / No error (d)"

  1. "Not only ... but also" must stand right before the two matching parts.
  2. Here "not only" stands before "he sings" (subject + verb), but "but also" stands before "dances" (verb only). Move "he" to the front so both are followed by a verb: "He not only sings but also dances very well."

Answer: (a), "He not only sings". Rule: correlative pairs must be placed before parallel parts.

6. "Unless you do not (a) / leave now, (b) / you will miss the bus (c) / No error (d)"

  1. "Unless" already means "if not".
  2. "Unless you do not leave" becomes a double negative: "if you do not not leave".

Answer: (a), should be "Unless you leave now". Rule: never use "not" after "unless".

8. Sentence Improvement

In plain words

In sentence improvement, one part of a sentence is bold or underlined. You get three or four replacements plus "No improvement". You pick the option that makes the sentence grammatically correct and clear, or "No improvement" if the bold part is already right.

It uses the same grammar rules as error spotting, but in reverse: you already know where the problem might be, and you must choose the right fix.

It is like choosing a spare part for a machine. The broken part is pointed out. Some spare parts do not fit, some fit but break something else, and one fits perfectly. Sometimes the old part was fine and you should leave it.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

The four-step method

  1. Read the full sentence and ask: is the bold part wrong? Use the seven checks from section 7.
  2. Name the rule that is broken (tense, agreement, preposition...). If you cannot find one, lean towards "No improvement".
  3. Eliminate options that keep the same error or add a new one.
  4. Put your choice back into the sentence and read it once. The right option fixes the error without changing the meaning.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Compare the options, not the sentence. Look at what differs between the options. If they differ only in "has / have", the question is about agreement; if they differ in "since / for / from", it is about time. The differences tell you which rule is being tested.

Shortcut

Shorter and simpler usually wins when two options are both grammatical, as long as the meaning stays the same. Tests like to reward removing redundancy ("return back", "each and every one of them all").

Solved examples

1. "He has went to the market." (a) has gone (b) had went (c) have gone (d) No improvement

  1. "Has" + V3. The V3 of "go" is "gone", not "went" (V2).
  2. (b) keeps "went", (c) has the wrong agreement with "he".

Answer: (a) has gone. Rule: perfect tenses use V3.

2. "If I would have known about the traffic, I would have left early." (a) had known (b) knew (c) have known (d) No improvement

  1. The main clause has "would have left": an imagined past (third conditional).
  2. The if-clause of the third conditional takes the past perfect.

Answer: (a) had known. Rule: If + had + V3, would have + V3.

3. "I look forward to meet you soon." (a) to meeting (b) for meeting (c) meeting to (d) No improvement

  1. In "look forward to", the word "to" is a preposition, not part of an infinitive.
  2. After a preposition, the verb takes the -ing form.

Answer: (a) to meeting. Rule: preposition + V-ing. Same for "used to (be accustomed)", "object to", "with a view to".

4. "Being a holiday, the office was closed." (a) It being a holiday (b) Having a holiday (c) Been a holiday (d) No improvement

  1. As written, the phrase says the office was a holiday: a dangling modifier.
  2. "It being a holiday" gives the phrase its own subject (an absolute phrase).

Answer: (a) It being a holiday. Rule: an opening phrase must describe the subject after the comma, or carry its own subject.

5. "He is so weak that he cannot walk." (a) too weak that he cannot (b) very weak to (c) so weak to (d) No improvement

  1. "So ... that ... cannot" is a correct pattern.
  2. (a) mixes "too" with "that"; (b) and (c) are not standard patterns.

Answer: (d) No improvement. Rule: so ... that, or too ... to; never mix them.

Common trap

Picking an option that fixes one thing and breaks another. For "The list of names are missing", the option "were missing" still disagrees with "list". Check every option fully, not just whether it looks different.

Common trap

Changing the meaning. An option can be perfectly grammatical and still be wrong because it says something else, for example changing "must" to "may" or "all" to "some". Keep the meaning of the original.

Practice set

Easy"He did not knew the answer." (a) does not knew (b) not knew (c) did not know (d) No improvement

Answer: (c) did not know

Rule: after did / does / do, use V1.

Easy"She is working here since 2018." (a) has been working here since (b) is working here from (c) worked here since (d) No improvement

Answer: (a) has been working here since

The action began in 2018 and is still going on. Rule: since + point of time with present perfect continuous.

Easy"He is too weak to walk." (a) very weak to walk (b) so weak to walk (c) too weak that he cannot walk (d) No improvement

Answer: (d) No improvement

"Too ... to" is correct and already has a negative meaning. Rule: too + adjective + to + V1.

Medium"He prefers tea than coffee." (a) prefers tea over than (b) is preferring tea than (c) prefers tea to (d) No improvement

Answer: (c) prefers tea to

Rule: prefer X to Y; "prefer" never takes "than".

Medium"Hardly had he reached the station than the train left." (a) when (b) that (c) then (d) No improvement

Answer: (a) when

Rule: Hardly / Scarcely ... when; No sooner ... than.

Medium"Unless you do not work hard, you will fail." (a) will not work (b) work (c) did not work (d) No improvement

Answer: (b) work

"Unless" already means "if not"; the condition refers to the future, so simple present. Rule: no "not" after "unless".

Medium"If I would have known about the party, I would have come." (a) knew (b) have known (c) had known (d) No improvement

Answer: (c) had known

Rule: third conditional: If + past perfect, would have + V3.

Hard"Running down the stairs, my bag fell." (a) Running down the stairs, I dropped my bag. (b) My bag fell, running down the stairs. (c) While my bag ran down the stairs, it fell. (d) No improvement

Answer: (a) Running down the stairs, I dropped my bag.

The bag was not running; the person was. Rule: an opening -ing phrase must describe the subject right after the comma (no dangling modifier).

Hard"She not only plays the guitar but also the piano." (a) plays the guitar not only but also the piano (b) plays not only the guitar but also the piano (c) not only plays the guitar but also plays piano also (d) No improvement

Answer: (b) plays not only the guitar but also the piano

"Not only" must stand before "the guitar" so that it matches "but also the piano" (noun with noun). (c) repeats "also". Rule: correlative pairs join parallel parts.

Hard"The scenery of Kashmir are very beautiful and attract many tourists." (a) is very beautiful and attract (b) are very beautiful and attracts (c) is very beautiful and attracts (d) No improvement

Answer: (c) is very beautiful and attracts

"Scenery" is uncountable and singular, so both verbs must be singular: is, attracts. Rule: subject–verb agreement applies to every verb of the subject.

9. Fill in the Blanks

In plain words

Fill in the blanks gives a sentence with one or two gaps and four options. Some blanks test grammar (the right preposition, tense or verb form). Others test vocabulary: which word fits the meaning of the sentence.

For vocabulary blanks, the sentence always contains a clue: a signal word like "although", "because" or a colon that tells you whether the missing word should agree with the rest of the sentence or go against it.

It is like a detective reading footprints. You do not guess the thief from nothing; the muddy boots and the open window point the way. In a sentence, "although" is the open window: it tells you the second half will turn in the opposite direction.

Key formulas & rules

Clue typeSignal wordsWhat the blank doesExample
Contrastbut, although, though, however, yet, despite, unlike, instead, while, far fromGoes the opposite way to the clueAlthough he was poor, he was always generous.
Cause / effectbecause, since, so, therefore, as a result, hence, so ... thatFits as the cause or the resultThe road was crowded, so the bus moved slowly.
Support / continuationand, also, moreover, in addition, similarly, in fact, not only ... but alsoGoes the same way as the clueShe is kind and helpful.
Definition / explanationcolon (:), dash, that is, in other words, which meansThe explanation defines the blankHe is frugal: he never wastes money.
Examplesuch as, for example, for instance, likeThe blank is the group the examples belong toPulses, such as lentils and chickpeas, are rich in protein.
Key formulas

Grammar blanks: look at the words right next to the blank. A preposition after it ("fond ___ reading") means a fixed pair; a verb before it may need an adverb; "the number of" needs "is". All the rules from sections 1–6 apply.

Double blanks: solve the easier blank first, eliminate every option whose word does not fit there, then check only the survivors for the second blank.

Tone: decide first whether the blank is positive or negative before looking at the options. This alone removes half the choices.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Fill it in your own words first. Cover the options, read the sentence and think of any simple word that fits ("good", "bad", "stopped"). Then pick the option closest to your word. This stops tricky options from pulling you in.

Shortcut

For double blanks, test one word per option. If the first blank clearly needs a negative word, cross out every option whose first word is positive. Often only one option survives without even reading the second word.

Shortcut

Grammar before meaning. If an option does not fit grammatically (wrong preposition, wrong part of speech, "a" before a vowel sound), eliminate it straight away even if its meaning looks right.

Solved examples

1. "Although the task was ___, she finished it in ten minutes." (a) simple (b) difficult (c) short (d) easy

  1. Signal word: "although", which means contrast.
  2. Finishing in ten minutes is a sign of ease, so the blank must go the opposite way: something that should take long.

Answer: (b) difficult. Simple, short and easy go the same way as the clue, so they cannot follow "although".

2. "The old man was so ___ that he gave all his savings to an orphanage." (a) selfish (b) generous (c) poor (d) greedy

  1. "So ... that" is cause and effect: the quality caused the action.
  2. Giving away all savings is caused by generosity.

Answer: (b) generous.

3. "The new flyover was meant to ___ traffic jams, but it actually ___ them." (a) reduce, worsened (b) increase, reduced (c) reduce, ended (d) cause, removed

  1. First blank: a flyover is "meant to" help, so a positive action like "reduce". This removes (b) and (d).
  2. "But ... actually" is a contrast: the result was the opposite of the aim. Between (a) and (c), only "worsened" goes against "reduce".

Answer: (a) reduce, worsened.

4. "Neither of the two answers ___ correct." (a) are (b) were (c) is (d) have been

  1. This is a grammar blank: the subject is "neither", which is singular.

Answer: (c) is. Rule: neither of takes a singular verb.

5. "He is very ___: he never spends money unless he really has to." (a) extravagant (b) generous (c) frugal (d) careless

  1. The colon introduces an explanation of the blank.
  2. Someone who spends only when necessary is careful with money.

Answer: (c) frugal (careful and economical with money). Extravagant and careless are the opposite.

Common trap

The "related but wrong" word. In "The medicine was so ___ that the fever went down in an hour", "bitter" and "expensive" are things medicines often are, so they feel natural. But neither explains why the fever went down. Always check the link the signal word demands, not just whether the word fits the topic.

Common trap

Missing a hidden contrast. Words like "unlike", "instead of", "far from" and "rather than" signal contrast just as strongly as "but". "Far from being happy, he was ___" needs a negative word.

Practice set

Easy"Although he was tired, he ___ working till midnight." (a) stopped (b) continued (c) refused (d) forgot

Answer: (b) continued

"Although" signals contrast: being tired would make you stop, so the blank goes the other way. Rule: contrast clue, choose the opposite direction.

Easy"The medicine was so ___ that the fever went down within an hour." (a) effective (b) useless (c) expensive (d) bitter

Answer: (a) effective

"So ... that" links a cause to its result; only an effective medicine explains the quick fall in fever. Rule: cause–effect clue.

Easy"She is fond ___ reading novels." (a) in (b) for (c) with (d) of

Answer: (d) of

Rule: fixed pair "fond of".

Medium"His speech was so ___ that many listeners fell asleep." (a) inspiring (b) short (c) dull (d) loud

Answer: (c) dull

Listeners falling asleep is the result of a boring speech. Rule: cause–effect clue ("so ... that").

Medium"Because the road was ___, the bus moved very slowly." (a) empty (b) crowded (c) wide (d) new

Answer: (b) crowded

"Because" introduces the cause of moving slowly; empty, wide and new roads would make it faster. Rule: cause–effect clue.

Medium"The results were ___: not a single student failed." (a) excellent (b) disappointing (c) average (d) mixed

Answer: (a) excellent

The colon introduces an explanation of the blank: nobody failing means the results were very good. Rule: definition/explanation clue.

Medium"Unlike his ___ brother, who talks all day, Arun is quiet and reserved." (a) shy (b) timid (c) lazy (d) talkative

Answer: (d) talkative

"Who talks all day" describes the brother directly, and "unlike" contrasts him with quiet Arun. Rule: contrast clue plus the explaining clause.

Hard"Far from being ___ by the defeat, the team was ___ to win the next match." (a) encouraged, afraid (b) pleased, unwilling (c) disheartened, determined (d) worried, reluctant

Answer: (c) disheartened, determined

"Far from" is a contrast: the first blank must be a natural reaction to defeat (negative) and the second its opposite (positive). Only (c) has negative then positive. Rule: contrast clue in a double blank.

Hard"Her ___ was well known; she gave away most of her earnings to ___." (a) greed, friends (b) generosity, charity (c) honesty, banks (d) wealth, herself

Answer: (b) generosity, charity

The semicolon joins an explanation: giving away earnings shows generosity, and such gifts go to charity. Rule: support/explanation clue; check both blanks.

Hard"The evidence was ___, so the judge had no choice but to ___ the accused." (a) conclusive, convict (b) weak, convict (c) strong, release (d) missing, punish

Answer: (a) conclusive, convict

"So ... had no choice but" is cause and effect: strong, final evidence forces a conviction. (b) and (c) pair a cause with the opposite result; missing evidence cannot force a punishment. Rule: cause–effect clue in a double blank.

10. Para Jumbles

In plain words

A para jumble gives four to six sentences of a paragraph in a mixed-up order, labelled P, Q, R, S (sometimes with the first and last sentence fixed). You must find the order that makes a logical, connected paragraph.

You do not need to understand the topic deeply. Good paragraphs are held together by links: a pronoun points back to a noun, "however" answers an earlier statement, "this" refers to something already said, a full name comes before its short form. Find the links, and the order builds itself.

It is like putting a train together. Each coach has a hook at the front and a hook at the back. The engine (opening sentence) has no hook at the front. Match each coach's front hook ("he", "this", "however") to a back hook in another coach, and the train lines up.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Mandatory pair = two sentences that must sit together, like "X" followed by "This X ..." or a question followed by its answer. Find one mandatory pair and you usually eliminate two or three options at once.

The closing sentence often concludes, sums up or suggests: therefore, thus, in short, hence, "must", "should".

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Use the options. Para jumbles are MCQs. First find the opening sentence and cross out options that start with anything else. Then find one mandatory pair (e.g. "P must come right after Q") and cross out options that break it. Usually only one option is left, and you never had to order all the sentences yourself.

Shortcut

A sentence starting with a pronoun or a connector can never be first. Scan the first word of each sentence: he, she, it, they, this, these, such, however, but, also, therefore, as a result, then, finally. Those are all "not first" sentences.

Solved examples

1. Which sentence should come first? (a) Therefore, they are called the lungs of the planet. (b) Forests absorb large amounts of carbon dioxide and release oxygen. (c) This is why cutting them down speeds up global warming. (d) They also give shelter to millions of species.

  1. (a) starts with "Therefore" and uses "they": needs something before it.
  2. (c) starts with "This" and uses "them"; (d) starts with "They" and has "also".
  3. Only (b) names the topic (forests) and stands alone.

Answer: (b).

2. First and last sentences are fixed. Arrange P, Q, R, S.
S1: Water is essential for life.
P: Yet, in many cities, a large part of it is wasted through leaking pipes.

S6: Saving water must begin at home and in our cities.

  1. "Of this small amount" (S) must follow "Only about 3%" (R): mandatory pair RS. This removes (b), (c) and (d).
  2. Check the rest: "Yet ... it is wasted" (P) contrasts with how scarce fresh water is, and "these leaks" (Q) needs the leaking pipes of P: pair PQ.

Answer: (a) RSPQ.

3. Arrange: P: After graduating, she joined a small software company in Pune. Q: Priya was born in a village near Nagpur in 1995. R: Within five years, she had become the head of its design team. S: She studied computer science at a college in her district. (a) QSPR (b) QPSR (c) SQPR (d) QSRP

  1. Q has the full name and a birth date: the opener. This removes (c).
  2. Life order: born (Q), studied (S), after graduating joined a company (P), within five years became head of "its" team (R). "Its" needs the company in P, so P comes before R. This removes (d).
  3. (b) puts "After graduating" before she studied, which breaks the time order.

Answer: (a) QSPR.

4. Arrange: P: The GST replaced many older taxes such as VAT and service tax. Q: The Goods and Services Tax (GST) was introduced in India on 1 July 2017. R: As a result, businesses now file one tax instead of several. S: However, small traders at first found the new filing system difficult. (a) QPRS (b) PQRS (c) QRPS (d) QPSR

  1. Q gives the full form with the abbreviation in brackets: the opener. This removes (b).
  2. "As a result" (R) must follow the cause, "replaced many taxes" (P): mandatory pair PR. This removes (c) and (d).
  3. S ("However, ... difficult") adds the drawback at the end.

Answer: (a) QPRS.

Common trap

Picking the "most general" sentence as the opener when it has a pronoun. "It is the largest democracy in the world" sounds like a great opening, but "it" points back to something. The opener must name its subject in full.

Common trap

Ordering all sentences yourself before looking at the options. That wastes time and invites mistakes. Two quick checks (opener + one mandatory pair) against the options usually settle the answer in under a minute.

11. Vocabulary: Synonyms, Antonyms & Roots

In plain words

A synonym is a word with the same (or nearly the same) meaning: big and large. An antonym is a word with the opposite meaning: big and small. Test questions give you one word and four options, and ask for the closest match or the clearest opposite.

You cannot memorise the whole dictionary. But most hard English words are built from a small set of Greek and Latin pieces: a prefix at the front, a root in the middle that carries the core meaning, and a suffix at the end that tells you the word type. Learn about 40 roots and 20 prefixes and you can make a smart guess at thousands of words you have never seen.

Think of LEGO. You don't need to memorise every model ever built. If you know what each brick looks like, you can look at a new model and see how it was put together. in + cred + ible is just three bricks: not + believe + able to be = "cannot be believed".

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Word = prefix + root + suffix. Read the root first (core meaning), then apply the prefix (not / again / against / before...), then the suffix (person, quality, action).

Synonym question: pick the option closest in meaning and in charge (positive or negative). Antonym question: pick the clearest opposite — never a synonym, never a word that is only "different".

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Charge test. Before looking at meanings, ask: is the word positive (+) or negative (−)? Malevolent has mal so it is negative. For a synonym keep only options with the same charge; for an antonym keep only the opposite charge. This often removes two options in five seconds.

Shortcut

Pair elimination. If two options mean the same thing (for example aggravate and worsen), neither can be the single correct answer in a synonym or antonym question. Cross both out.

Shortcut

Put it in a sentence. If you half-know a word, recall a phrase you have heard: "a candid interview", "a chronic illness", "ephemeral fame". Then plug each option into that phrase and see which one keeps the meaning.

Solved examples

Example 1 (easy). Choose the synonym of BENEVOLENT: (a) cruel (b) kind (c) greedy (d) lazy

  1. Break it: bene = good, vol = wish. A benevolent person wishes good to others.
  2. Charge: positive. (a) and (c) are negative, (d) is negative. Only (b) is positive.

Answer: (b) kind.

Example 2 (easy). Choose the antonym of EPHEMERAL: (a) transient (b) fleeting (c) permanent (d) brief

  1. Ephemeral means lasting a very short time ("ephemeral fame").
  2. (a), (b) and (d) all mean short-lived. They are synonyms, so they cannot be the antonym.
  3. The opposite of short-lived is long-lasting.

Answer: (c) permanent. Shortcut: three options are the same idea, so the odd one out is the answer.

Example 3 (medium). Using roots, what does OMNISCIENT mean? (a) all-powerful (b) present everywhere (c) all-knowing (d) eating everything

  1. omni = all, sci = know (as in science, conscious).
  2. So omniscient = knowing everything.
  3. The other options are its cousins: all-powerful is omnipotent, present everywhere is omnipresent, eating everything is omnivorous.

Answer: (c) all-knowing.

Example 4 (medium). Choose the antonym of LOQUACIOUS: (a) talkative (b) garrulous (c) taciturn (d) verbose

  1. loqu = speak. Loquacious = talking a lot.
  2. Talkative, garrulous and verbose all mean "too many words". Cross them out.
  3. Taciturn means saying very little.

Answer: (c) taciturn.

Example 5 (hard). "The manager was reticent about the reasons for the layoffs, answering every question with a vague smile." The underlined word means: (a) angry (b) reserved (c) confused (d) honest

  1. Use the context: "answering every question with a vague smile" means he did not give information.
  2. (d) honest is the opposite of what happened. (a) angry does not fit a smile. (c) confused is not supported, he was avoiding, not lost.
  3. Reticent = not revealing thoughts or feelings easily.

Answer: (b) reserved.

Example 6 (hard). Choose the word most nearly OPPOSITE in meaning to EXACERBATE: (a) aggravate (b) worsen (c) alleviate (d) intensify

  1. Exacerbate = make a problem worse ("exacerbate the pain").
  2. Aggravate, worsen and intensify are all "make worse". Pair elimination removes all three.
  3. Alleviate = make less severe (the root lev = light, as in lever, levitate: make lighter).

Answer: (c) alleviate.

Common trap

The synonym hiding in an antonym question. Test makers always put the synonym among the options of an antonym question (and the antonym among options of a synonym question). Read the instruction twice: SAME or OPPOSITE? Underline it on your rough sheet.

Common trap

Look-alike words. Eminent (famous) is not imminent (about to happen). Diffident (shy, lacking confidence) is not different. Credulous (believes too easily) is not credible (believable). Roots help, but read every letter.

Common trap

"in-" does not always mean "not". Inflammable means "easily set on fire" (same as flammable), and invaluable means "extremely valuable". When a word is common, trust the meaning you know over the prefix.

12. Idioms & Phrases

In plain words

An idiom is a group of words whose meaning is different from the meanings of the single words. "It's raining cats and dogs" has nothing to do with animals; it means "raining very heavily". You cannot work idioms out word by word, so you learn them as whole units.

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word (up, off, out, in) that together have a new meaning: give up = stop trying, call off = cancel. Tests ask you to pick the meaning of an underlined idiom, or to pick the idiom that fits a blank.

Idioms are like nicknames. Nobody guesses that "Bunty" means "Rajesh" by reading the letters. You just know it because you have heard it used. The more you read and listen, the more nicknames you know.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Meaning is fixed, words are fixed. You cannot swap words in an idiom: it is "a piece of cake", not "a slice of cake"; "the lion's share", not "the tiger's share".

Always check the sentence tone. Most idioms are clearly positive (on cloud nine) or negative (in hot water). The sentence around the blank tells you which you need.

13. One-Word Substitution

In plain words

In one-word substitution you get a long description ("a person who can speak many languages") and must pick the single word that means exactly that (polyglot). It tests whether you know precise words that pack a whole phrase into one.

14. Commonly Confused Words

In plain words

Some English words look or sound almost the same but mean different things: stationary (not moving) and stationery (paper, pens). Spell-check will not save you, because both are real words. Tests hide these in error-spotting, fill-in-the-blank and "choose the correctly used word" questions.

The fix is simple: learn each pair together, with one memory hook per pair, and always check the word type (noun, verb, adjective) the sentence needs.

Like identical twins in your class. You tell them apart by one small mark, a mole or a hairstyle. Each confused pair also has one small mark: the e in stationery is for envelopes; the a in stationary is for standing still.

15. Reading Comprehension

In plain words

Reading comprehension (RC) gives you a passage of 200–400 words and 3–6 questions about it. It checks three skills: can you find a fact that is written there, can you understand what the author is really saying, and can you draw a small, safe conclusion that is not written directly.

The golden rule: every correct answer is supported by the passage. Not by your general knowledge, not by what sounds wise, not by what you believe. If you cannot point to a line that backs your answer, it is probably wrong.

RC is like being a witness in court. The judge does not want your opinion of the case. She wants what you saw, and small conclusions you can defend from what you saw. "He was holding an umbrella, so it was probably raining" is fine. "He is a bad person" is not.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Correct answer = supported by the passage. Wrong answers are usually: too extreme, out of scope, half-right, or true but not what was asked.

This effect, called the urban heat island, happens because concrete, asphalt and brick absorb sunlight during the day and release it slowly at night. Tall buildings trap this heat, and air conditioners, vehicles and factories add more. Passage 2 (Questions 6–10).

Most students prepare for exams the same way: they read a chapter again and again the night before, then feel confident because the material looks familiar. Psychologists call this feeling the "illusion of fluency". Recognising a page is not the same as being able to recall it on your own, and the exam asks for recall.

Two techniques work far better, according to more than a century of research. The first is spacing. In the 1880s, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus memorised lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself at different intervals. He found that memory fades quickly at first and then more slowly, and that each review made the next forgetting slower. Reviews spread over several days, therefore, produce stronger memories than the same number of reviews crammed into one evening.

The second technique is retrieval practice: testing yourself instead of rereading. When you struggle to pull an answer out of memory, the effort itself strengthens the memory. In many experiments, students who spent part of their study time taking practice quizzes remembered more a week later than students who spent all of that time rereading, even though the rereaders felt better prepared. Even a quiz question that a student gets wrong helps, as long as the correct answer is checked afterwards.

Neither method feels pleasant. Spaced, effortful practice is slower and produces more mistakes during study. But that difficulty is the point. A learner who judges progress by how easy study feels will choose the wrong method almost every time.

16. Verbal Critical Reasoning

In plain words

Critical reasoning (CR) gives you a short argument of 2–4 sentences and asks you to judge it: find its conclusion, find the hidden belief it depends on (the assumption), or pick the fact that would make it stronger or weaker. Companies like Infosys, Accenture and Capgemini use it because it tests how you think, not what you have memorised.

Every argument has the same shape: premises (the reasons given) lead to a conclusion (the claim). Between them there is usually a gap that the author fills silently with an assumption. Almost every CR question is really a question about that gap.

An argument is a bridge across a river. The premises are the near bank, the conclusion is the far bank, and the assumption is the plank in the middle that nobody mentions. To strengthen, you nail the plank down. To weaken, you pull it out. To find the assumption, you just name the plank.

Key formulas & rules

Key formulas

Argument = Premises + (hidden) Assumption → Conclusion.

Negation test for assumptions: turn the option into its opposite. If the argument now falls apart, the option is a real (necessary) assumption. If the argument survives, it is not.

Conclusion indicators: therefore, so, thus, hence, clearly, it follows that, consequently. Premise indicators: because, since, as, given that, for.

Question typeTypical wordingWhat the right answer does
Conclusion / main point"Which best states the conclusion?"States the claim that the other sentences support. Ask "why should I believe this?" If another sentence answers, this is the conclusion.
Assumption"The argument assumes...", "depends on..."Fills the gap. Passes the negation test. Usually modest, not extreme.
Strengthen"Which, if true, most strengthens..."Adds new support to the gap, often by ruling out another explanation.
Weaken"Which, if true, most weakens / casts doubt..."Attacks the gap: another cause, a biased sample, an unfair comparison. It need not destroy the argument, only make it less likely.
Inference / must be true"Which can be properly inferred...", "must be true..."Follows from the statements with no outside help. Stay close to the text. Any new idea is a trap.

Common gaps (and so, common weakeners).

GapExampleHow to weaken
Correlation taken as causePeople who drink green tea are slimmer, so green tea makes you slim.Show a third factor (tea drinkers also exercise more) or reverse cause.
Alternative cause ignoredCrime fell after new street lights, so the lights cut crime.Show something else changed at the same time (more police patrols).
Unrepresentative sample90% of our app users love it, so the public will love it.Show the sample was special (only loyal users answered).
Unfair comparisonIt worked for City A, so it will work for City B.Show A and B differ in a way that matters.

Shortcuts

Shortcut

Read the question stem first. Knowing you are looking for a weakener makes you read the argument hunting for its gap. It saves a second reading.

Shortcut

Out of scope = out. Options that talk about something the argument does not (a different product, a different time, "how popular" when the argument is about "how effective") do not strengthen or weaken. Cut them first; usually two go.

Shortcut

For inference, pick the boring option. The right inference is often dull and nearly a restatement. The exciting, sweeping option ("this proves...", "all...") is a trap.

Solved examples

Example 1 (conclusion, easy). "Online courses let students replay lectures as often as needed. They also cost less than classroom courses and can be taken from anywhere. Therefore, more working adults should consider online courses for upskilling." The conclusion is: (a) Online courses cost less. (b) Lectures can be replayed. (c) More working adults should consider online courses for upskilling. (d) Classroom courses are useless.

  1. "Therefore" signals the conclusion.
  2. (a) and (b) are reasons (premises). (d) is extreme and never said.

Answer: (c).

Example 2 (assumption, medium). "Ravi scored 95% in the mock test, so he will clear the placement aptitude round." The argument assumes: (a) Ravi studied hard. (b) The mock test reflects the level and pattern of the real round. (c) Ravi has taken many mock tests. (d) Most students score below 95%.

  1. Gap: from "mock score" to "real result". The author must believe the mock predicts the real test.
  2. Negation test on (b): "The mock test does NOT reflect the real round." Then 95% tells us nothing, and the argument collapses. So (b) is needed.
  3. Negate (a): "Ravi did not study hard." He still scored 95%, so the argument survives. Negate (c) or (d): the argument also survives.

Answer: (b).

Example 3 (weaken, medium). "Since the city started a helmet-awareness campaign in January, head injuries in two-wheeler accidents have fallen by 30%. So the campaign has made riders safer." Which most weakens this? (a) The campaign was widely advertised on radio. (b) In January, the city also began heavy fines for riding without a helmet. (c) Head injuries are the most serious injuries in accidents. (d) Many riders said they liked the campaign ads.

  1. Gap: nothing else caused the fall.
  2. (b) gives another cause at the same time: fines, not the campaign, may explain the change.
  3. (a) and (d) are about how well-known or liked the campaign was, not whether it caused the fall. (c) only says why the fall matters.

Answer: (b) (alternative cause).

Example 4 (strengthen, medium). Same argument as Example 3. Which most strengthens it? (a) A nearby city with similar traffic and no campaign saw no fall in head injuries in the same period. (b) The campaign cost ₹40 lakh. (c) Two-wheeler sales rose in the year. (d) Some riders still do not wear helmets.

  1. To strengthen, rule out "it would have happened anyway".
  2. (a) is a control group: without the campaign, no fall. That supports the campaign as the cause.
  3. (b) is irrelevant. (c) does not explain a fall in injuries. (d) slightly weakens.

Answer: (a).

Example 5 (inference, hard). "All employees who completed the safety training received a badge. Meena, an employee, did not receive a badge." Which must be true? (a) Meena did not complete the safety training. (b) Meena failed the training test. (c) Meena will receive a badge next year. (d) The training was optional.

  1. Rule: completed → badge. So no badge → not completed (the contrapositive).
  2. (b) is too specific: she may never have started. (c) and (d) are not supported by anything.

Answer: (a).

Common trap

Reversing the rule. "All who completed got a badge" does NOT mean "all who got a badge completed". Someone could get a badge for another reason. Only the contrapositive (no badge → not completed) is safe.

Common trap

Picking a premise as the conclusion. A sentence can be true and important and still be a reason, not the claim. The conclusion is the one sentence that the others are there to prove.

Common trap

Using outside knowledge. In CR, every option "if true" must be accepted as true. Don't reject a weakener because you think it is unlikely in real life.

Practice set

Easy"Regular exercise improves sleep quality. It also lowers stress and helps control weight. Therefore, busy professionals should make time for exercise even on work days." The main conclusion is: (a) Exercise lowers stress. (b) Exercise improves sleep quality. (c) Busy professionals should make time for exercise even on work days. (d) Work days are stressful.

Answer: (c) Busy professionals should make time for exercise even on work days.

"Therefore" introduces it, and the first two sentences are reasons for it. (a) and (b) are premises; (d) is not stated.

Easy"The café has started offering free Wi-Fi, so it will attract more students." The argument assumes that: (a) students value free Wi-Fi when choosing a café (b) the café's coffee is cheap (c) no other café nearby has Wi-Fi (d) students do not drink coffee

Answer: (a) students value free Wi-Fi when choosing a café

Negate (a): students don't care about Wi-Fi. Then Wi-Fi won't attract them, so the argument fails. Negating (c) (other cafés also have Wi-Fi) does not destroy the argument; the café could still draw more students than before. (b) is irrelevant and (d) would hurt the café, not support the argument.

Easy"Sales of umbrellas at the store doubled in July. The manager concludes that the new window display caused the increase." Which most weakens the conclusion? (a) The display was designed by a famous artist. (b) July was the rainiest month in the city in ten years. (c) Umbrella prices were unchanged in July. (d) The store also sells raincoats.

Answer: (b) July was the rainiest month in the city in ten years.

Heavy rain is another cause that easily explains more umbrella sales. (a) is about the display's designer, not its effect. (c) removes one other cause (price cut), so it slightly strengthens. (d) is irrelevant.

Medium"Employees who work from home one day a week report higher job satisfaction. So allowing a work-from-home day raises satisfaction." Which most strengthens this? (a) Employees who chose the work-from-home day were already more satisfied before the policy began. (b) When the same employees were surveyed before and after getting the day, their satisfaction rose afterwards. (c) Some employees prefer working in the office. (d) The company has 500 employees.

Answer: (b) When the same employees were surveyed before and after getting the day, their satisfaction rose afterwards.

A before-and-after rise in the same people links the policy to the change. (a) is a weakener: the happy people chose the option, so the policy may not have caused anything. (c) and (d) are out of scope.

Medium"Every train on the Blue Line stops at Central Station. The 8:15 train does not stop at Central Station." Which must be true? (a) The 8:15 is not a Blue Line train. (b) The 8:15 is a Red Line train. (c) Central Station is closed. (d) The 8:15 is running late.

Answer: (a) The 8:15 is not a Blue Line train.

Blue Line → stops at Central. Does not stop → not Blue Line (contrapositive). (b) is too specific: it could be any other line. (c) and (d) are not supported; if Central were closed, Blue Line trains would not stop there either, which contradicts "every train on the Blue Line stops".

Medium"Plastic bags make up a large share of the city's plastic waste. So a ban on plastic bags will sharply reduce the city's plastic waste." The argument depends on which assumption? (a) Shoppers will not simply switch to other single-use plastic items in place of bags. (b) All of the city's plastic waste comes from bags. (c) Paper bags cost more than plastic bags. (d) Shop owners will welcome the ban.

Answer: (a) Shoppers will not simply switch to other single-use plastic items in place of bags.

Negate (a): shoppers switch to other single-use plastics. Then total plastic waste may not fall, and the argument breaks. (b) is too extreme: "a large share" is enough for the argument. (c) and (d) do not affect how much plastic waste there is.

Medium"Students who use the college library more often get higher grades. Therefore, using the library causes better grades." Which most weakens this? (a) Students who are more motivated both use the library more and study harder in general. (b) The library has 50,000 books. (c) Grades rose across the college last year. (d) Some students prefer to study at home.

Answer: (a) Students who are more motivated both use the library more and study harder in general.

A third factor (motivation) explains both library use and grades, so the library may not be the cause. (b) and (d) are irrelevant. (c) is about all students and does not separate library users from others.

Hard"A diet app reports that its users lost an average of 5 kg in three months. So the app is effective for weight loss." Which most weakens this? (a) The average was calculated only from users who kept using the app for all three months; those who quit were not counted. (b) The app is free to download. (c) Many users recommend the app to friends. (d) Losing 5 kg in three months is considered healthy.

Answer: (a) The average was calculated only from users who kept using the app for all three months; those who quit were not counted.

This is a biased sample: people who were not losing weight are likely the ones who quit, which inflates the average. (b) is irrelevant; (c) mildly strengthens; (d) is about whether the loss is healthy, not whether the app caused it.

Hard"Our competitor cut its prices by 10% and its sales rose 25%. If we cut our prices by 10%, our sales will also rise 25%." The argument assumes: (a) Our customers respond to price cuts in the same way as the competitor's customers. (b) The competitor is a larger company than ours. (c) Cutting prices reduces profit on each item. (d) The competitor's sales rose only because of a new advertising campaign.

Answer: (a) Our customers respond to price cuts in the same way as the competitor's customers.

This is an unfair-comparison gap. Negate (a): our customers react differently, so the 25% prediction fails. (b) and (c) do not bridge the gap. (d) would weaken the argument, and an assumption never weakens its own argument.

Hard"In a survey of 1,000 graduates, 60% had done an internship. Of these, 70% got a job within six months. Of those without an internship, 40% got a job within six months." Which must be true? (a) More than half of all surveyed graduates got a job within six months. (b) Internships cause graduates to get jobs. (c) Most graduates who got jobs had not done an internship. (d) Exactly 70% of all surveyed graduates got jobs within six months.

Answer: (a) More than half of all surveyed graduates got a job within six months.

Internship group: 600 graduates, 70% = 420 got jobs. Others: 400 graduates, 40% = 160 got jobs. Total = 580 of 1,000 = 58%, which is more than half. (c) is false: 420 of the 580 jobs went to interns. (d) is false: 58%, not 70%. (b) is a causal claim that survey data alone cannot prove (motivated students may do both).

Test Strategy & Learning Path

In plain words

The verbal section rewards two things: a steady daily habit (vocabulary and reading cannot be crammed in a week) and a smart order of attack in the exam (grab the quick marks first, leave the long passages for when you know how much time is left).

Like filling a bucket with rocks, pebbles and sand. Put the big rocks in first (the high-mark, reliable question types), then pebbles, then sand. If you start with sand, the rocks never fit.

Error log5–10 minFor every mistake, write the rule or proof line that would have got it right. This log is your revision material in Week 6.
Key formulas

Words learnt in 6 weeks at 15/day × 6 days ≈ 500 plus roots, idioms and one-word substitutes. That covers most words that repeat in placement tests.

Test yourself, don't just reread. Cover the meaning column and recall it. Recall practice builds memory far better than reading the list again.

Test-day checklist

  • Sleep well the night before; do only a 20-minute flashcard review and the Rules Cheat Sheet.
  • Note the number of questions and total time; write the "halfway" time on your rough sheet.
  • Attack in the order above: vocabulary → grammar → para jumbles → CR → RC.
  • Check if there is negative marking before guessing.
  • Keep the last 2 minutes to fill in any blanks (only if there is no negative marking).
