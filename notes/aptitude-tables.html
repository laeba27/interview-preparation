1. Multiplication Tables 1 to 30

In plain words

Tables are the base of every fast calculation: percentages, ratios, time and work and division all fall back on them. Know 1 to 20 cold, and 21 to 30 well enough to write them out in seconds.

Like knowing your friends' phone numbers by heart instead of opening the contacts every time: the lookup is what slows you down.

How to learn 11 to 30 fast

Split the number: 17 × 8 = 10 × 8 + 7 × 8 = 80 + 56 = 136. For 21 to 30, use (20 + a) × k = 20k + ak, so 27 × 6 = 120 + 42 = 162. Say each table aloud once a day for a week; then test yourself with random pairs in Calculation Practice.

2. Squares 1 to 100

In plain words

A square is a number times itself: 13² = 169. Squares 1 to 30 should be instant; 31 to 100 you can rebuild in a few seconds with the tricks below, but reading this table daily makes them instant too.

Squares are landmarks on the number line. When you see 729 or 841 you should immediately recognise 27² and 29², the same way you recognise a famous building.

Rebuild any square fast

Near 50: (50 + a)² = 2500 + 100a + a², so 53² = 2500 + 300 + 9 = 2809. Near 100: (100 − a)² = 10000 − 200a + a², so 96² = 10000 − 800 + 16 = 9216. Ending in 5: n5² = n(n+1) then 25, so 75² = 7 × 8 = 56, then 25 = 5625.

Patterns that help you check an answer

  • A perfect square can only end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9. If a number ends in 2, 3, 7 or 8 it is not a perfect square.
  • Squares ending in 0 always end in an even number of zeros (100, 400, 2500).
  • The last two digits of n² and (50 − n)² and (50 + n)² are the same: 21² = 441, 29² = 841, 71² = 5041, 79² = 6241 all end in 41.
  • Difference of consecutive squares: (n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1, so 41² = 40² + 81 = 1600 + 81 = 1681.

3. Cubes 1 to 50

In plain words

A cube is a number multiplied by itself three times: 12³ = 12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728. Cubes 1 to 20 come up constantly in mensuration (volumes) and in cube-root questions.

If a square is a floor tile pattern, a cube is the whole box of tiles stacked up: side × side × side.

Last digit of a cube

The last digit of n³ depends only on the last digit of n: 0→0, 1→1, 2→8, 3→7, 4→4, 5→5, 6→6, 7→3, 8→2, 9→9. So 2↔8 and 3↔7 swap; 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9 stay the same. This is how you find cube roots in seconds (section 6).

Useful identities

  • (a + b)³ = a³ + b³ + 3ab(a + b), so 21³ = 8000 + 1 + 3 × 20 × 1 × 21 = 9261.

  • If a + b + c = 0, then a³ + b³ + c³ = 3abc.

4. Powers of Small Numbers & Unit-Digit Cycles

In plain words

Powers show up in compound interest, growth, number system and remainder questions. You rarely need a huge power in full, but you must know the small ones and how the last digit repeats.

The last digit of a power goes round in a loop, like the hands of a clock. Once you know the loop length, you can jump straight to the 100th turn.

Unit digit of a big power

Divide the exponent by the cycle length and use the remainder (a remainder of 0 means the last item in the cycle). Example: 799: cycle 7, 9, 3, 1 (length 4); 99 ÷ 4 leaves 3, so the 3rd item: 3. Check: 3.

Common trap

For bases ending in 0, 1, 5 or 6 the last digit never changes. Do not divide by 4 for them.

5. Square Roots 1 to 50

In plain words

The square root of a number is the value that, multiplied by itself, gives the number: √196 = 14. Perfect squares have whole-number roots; the others are decimals you should know to two or three places for estimation.

Finding a square root is walking the squares table backwards: you know the answer, you are looking for the question.

Must-know roots

√2 = 1.414, √3 = 1.732, √5 = 2.236, √6 = 2.449, √7 = 2.646, √10 = 3.162. Almost every other root can be built from these: √12 = 2√3 = 3.464, √50 = 5√2 = 7.071.

Square root of a big perfect square in 10 seconds

  1. Look at the last digit to get the possible last digits of the root: 1→1 or 9, 4→2 or 8, 9→3 or 7, 6→4 or 6, 5→5, 0→0.
  2. Drop the last two digits and find the largest square below what is left. That gives the tens digit.
  3. Pick between the two candidates by comparing with (tens digit)(5)²: if the number is bigger, take the larger candidate.

Example: √5329. Ends in 9 → root ends in 3 or 7. Drop "29": 53, and 7² = 49 < 53 < 64, so the tens digit is 7. 75² = 5625 > 5329, so take the smaller: 73. Check: 73² = 5329.

6. Cube Roots

In plain words

The cube root is the number that, cubed, gives the value: ∛2744 = 14. For perfect cubes up to six digits there is a two-step trick that needs only the cubes of 1 to 9.

Like reading a car number plate: the last digit tells you one thing, the front tells you the other, and together they identify it.

Two-step cube root trick (perfect cubes)

1) The last digit of the root comes from the last digit of the cube: 1→1, 8→2, 7→3, 4→4, 5→5, 6→6, 3→7, 2→8, 9→9, 0→0. 2) Drop the last three digits; the front of the root is the largest number whose cube is at most what remains. Example: ∛103823: ends in 3 → root ends in 7. Drop 823: 103, and 4³ = 64 ≤ 103 < 125 = 5³, so front = 4. Root = 47. Check: 47³ = {comma(47**3)}.

7. Fraction ↔ Percentage ↔ Decimal (1/1 to 1/30)

In plain words

Most percentage, profit and ratio questions become one-line mental sums once you can switch between a fraction and its percentage without dividing. 12.5% is just 1/8; 16 2/3% is 1/6.

It's the same amount in three languages. A good translator doesn't look words up; they just know that 1/8 and 12.5% mean the same thing.

The big idea

x% of y = y% of x, so 16% of 25 = 25% of 16 = 4. And to take 37.5% of anything, take 3/8 of it: 37.5% of 640 = 3 × 80 = 240.

In plain words

Once 1/8 = 12.5% is automatic, 3/8, 5/8 and 7/8 are just multiples: 37.5%, 62.5%, 87.5%. The denominators below are the ones that appear most in tests.

If one slice of pizza is 12.5% of the pizza, three slices are 37.5%. You never have to cut a new pizza.

Successive percentages

Two changes of a% and b% give a net change of a + b + ab/100. +20% then −20% = 20 − 20 − 400/100 = −4%, not zero.

Common trap

The same fraction goes up and down differently. If A is 25% (1/4) more than B, then B is 1/5 = 20% less than A, because the base changed.

9. Factorials 0! to 20!

In plain words

n! (n factorial) is 1 × 2 × 3 × … × n. It counts the ways to arrange n different things in a row. 0! is defined as 1.

5 people can line up in 5! = 120 ways: 5 choices for the first spot, 4 for the next, and so on.

Common trap

Factorials grow very fast. 13! is already more than 6 billion, so in P&C questions always cancel first.

10. nCr and Pascal's Triangle (n up to 12)

In plain words

nCr is the number of ways to choose r things from n when order does not matter. Row n of Pascal's triangle lists nC0, nC1, …, nCn; each number is the sum of the two above it.

Choosing 2 friends out of 5 for a trip: the order you invite them doesn't matter, so it's 5C2 = 10 ways.

11. Prime Numbers up to 200

In plain words

A prime has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. There are 25 primes below 100 and 46 below 200. 1 is not prime, and 2 is the only even prime.

Primes are the atoms of numbers: every whole number is built by multiplying primes together in exactly one way.

Is it prime? Quick test

Check divisibility only by primes up to its square root. For 191: √191 ≈ 13.8, so test 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. None divide it, so 191 is prime.

Common trap

91 = 7 × 13, 119 = 7 × 17, 133 = 7 × 19, 143 = 11 × 13, 161 = 7 × 23, 187 = 11 × 17 and 169 = 13² look prime but are not.

In plain words

log x answers: 10 to what power gives x? Tests usually give you log 2 and log 3 and expect you to build the rest.

If log 2 and log 3 are two Lego bricks, log 6, log 8, log 12 and log 18 are all built by snapping them together.

13. Speed Tricks with Worked Examples

In plain words

These tricks replace long multiplication with two or three small mental steps. Learn one a day and practise it on ten random numbers.

Taking a shortcut through the park instead of walking around the block: same place, half the time.

Multiply by 11

64 × 11

  1. Write the two digits apart: 6 _ 4.
  2. Put their sum in the middle: 6 + 4 = 10.
  3. It's two digits, so carry the 1 to the 6: 7 0 4.

Answer: 704

87 × 11

  1. 8 _ 7, middle = 8 + 7 = 15.
  2. Carry 1: 9 5 7.

Answer: 957

Numbers near a base (100, 1000)

98 × 97

  1. Both are below 100: 98 is −2, 97 is −3.
  2. Cross-subtract: 98 − 3 = 95 (or 97 − 2). That's the left part.
  3. Multiply the differences: (−2) × (−3) = 06 (two digits for base 100).
  4. Join: 95 | 06.

14. 4-Week Memorisation Plan

In plain words

Memorising works best in short daily sessions with self-testing, not long reading sessions. Fifteen minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday.

Like going to the gym: small, regular sessions build the muscle; one huge session just makes you sore.
