Part 1 — How Behavioural Interviews Work

1. What Interviewers Are Really Assessing

In plain words

A behavioural question asks about something you actually did in the past: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate." The idea is simple: how you behaved before is the best guide to how you will behave next.

There is no single right answer. The interviewer is collecting signals (small pieces of evidence) about how you work with people, handle problems and take responsibility. Your job is to give them clear, specific evidence.

It is like a reference check you give about yourself. A good reference does not say "she is great"; it says "when our release broke on a Friday night, she stayed, found the cause and wrote down how to stop it happening again". Stories, not adjectives.

The competencies they look for

A competency is a skill or habit the company cares about. Most companies, from startups to large product and service firms, look for roughly the same set, even if they name them differently.

CompetencyJunior / fresher answer shows…Senior (3–5 yrs) answer shows…
TeamworkYou helped others, shared work fairly, asked for help early.You made the whole team work better: unblocked people, improved a process.

LeadershipYou led a college project or a small task; people followed your plan.You influenced without authority, mentored, and set direction.

CommunicationYour answer is clear and in order.You adjust your message for engineers, managers and customers.

How they score you

Most structured interviews use a rubric: a sheet listing the competencies and a scale for each. After the call, the interviewer writes notes and gives a rating per competency, then an overall "hire" or "no hire". What moves your score up:

  • Specific over general. "Last March, on the checkout page…" beats "I usually…".
  • Your own actions. They can only score what you did, so "we" answers score low.
  • Real stakes. A story with a deadline, a customer, or money involved gives stronger evidence than a trivial one.
  • A result and a lesson. What happened, ideally with a number, and what you would do again or differently.
  • Honesty and self-awareness. Admitting a gap and showing growth is a positive signal, not a negative one.
Rating (typical scale)What the notes usually say
Strong yesClear, specific story; candidate drove the outcome; measurable result; good reflection.
Leaning yesGood story, but some parts vague, or result not clear.
Leaning noMostly "we"; hypothetical ("I would…"); needed many follow-up questions.
Strong noBlamed others, showed a red flag (dishonesty, disrespect), or had no example at all.

Follow-up questions are part of the test

Good interviewers dig: "What exactly did you say to him?", "What would you do differently?", "What did your manager think?". These check that the story is real and that you understand it deeply. Pick stories you lived through, so the follow-ups feel easy.

Behavioural vs HR round

The behavioural round (often by an engineer or manager) tests competencies through past stories. The HR round checks fit and practical details: why this company, salary, notice period, relocation. The two overlap, and this page covers both: Parts 1–2 are behavioural, Part 3 is HR.

Do this

Before an interview, read the job description and the company's values page. Circle 3–4 competencies they clearly care about ("ownership", "customer first") and make sure you have a story ready for each.

Avoid

Giving opinions instead of evidence: "I am a great team player and very hard-working." Every candidate says this. Without a story behind it, the interviewer has nothing to write down.

Remember: the interviewer is on your side in one way: they need evidence to write a "hire" note. Make their note easy to write. One clear story with your actions and a result is worth more than five vague ones.

2. The STAR Method

In plain words

STAR is a simple order for telling a work story: Situation, Task, Action, Result. It stops you rambling and makes sure you cover what the interviewer needs to score you.

Situation and Task set the scene. Action is what you did, and it is where most of the marks are. Result is what changed, plus what you learned.

Like a good movie trailer: a few seconds of setting, one clear problem, most of the time on the hero's moves, and a satisfying ending. Nobody wants a trailer that is all opening credits.

Each part, with an example (story A)

PartWhat to sayExample: checkout speed (A)
SituationWhere, when, who, what was wrong. 1–2 sentences."At Brightcart, our checkout page took 4.2 seconds to load at p95, and the product team saw users dropping off."
TaskYour job or goal in it. 1 sentence."I was asked to own the performance work and get it under 2 seconds before the next quarter."
Action3–4 steps you took, with the "why" for each. Use "I"."I profiled the page and found three causes: a large JS bundle, five API calls in sequence, and uncompressed images. I split the bundle, merged the calls into one endpoint with the backend team, and moved images to a CDN with modern formats. I added a performance budget to CI so it would not slip back."
ResultWhat changed, a number if honest, and a lesson."p95 dropped to 1.6 seconds and conversion rose about 6% over the next month. I learned to measure first; my first guess about the cause was wrong."

Variants: CAR and SOAR

FormatPartsWhen to use it
STARSituation, Task, Action, ResultThe default for most "tell me about a time" questions.
CARChallenge, Action, ResultShort answers, rapid-fire rounds, or when the task is obvious from the situation. Good for freshers with small stories.
SOARSituation, Obstacle, Action, ResultWhen the story is about overcoming a blocker: a difficult person, missing information, a tight deadline. Naming the obstacle makes the difficulty clear.
STAR + LSTAR, then LearningFailure and mistake questions. The learning is the point of the answer (section 9).

Timing

  • 1.5 to 2 minutes for a full answer. Under 1 minute usually means it is too thin; over 3 minutes and the interviewer stops listening.
  • About 60% on Action. If you notice you are still describing the situation after 30 seconds, jump: "So what I did was…".
  • Leave room for follow-ups. A crisp answer invites good questions; a long one uses up the interview on one story.

The "I vs we" rule

Software is team work, so "we" feels natural and modest. But the interviewer is hiring you. Use "we" for the team's goal and context, and "I" for every action you personally took.

WeakStrong
"We fixed the webhook bug and refunded everyone.""I traced the bug to retries without idempotency, wrote the fix, and worked with finance to refund the 38 customers."
"We decided to refactor.""I proposed a two-day spike to compare both options, and after the results the team agreed to refactor."

It is fine to credit others: "Priya handled the database side while I…". That shows teamwork and makes your own part clearer.

Quantifying results honestly

  • Use numbers you actually know: time saved, errors reduced, users affected, load time, tickets closed, marks or rank for freshers.
  • Round and say "about": "about 6%", "roughly 30 hours a month". Never invent precision.
  • If there is no number, describe the change: "the support team stopped escalating this issue", "the intern started submitting clean pull requests by week four".
  • Be ready for "How did you measure that?". If you cannot answer, do not use the number.
Do this

Write each story as four short bullet points (S, T, A, R), not a script. Practise saying it out loud with a timer. Aim for about 90 seconds, then stop and let them ask.

Avoid

Spending a minute on company background and tech stack before anything happens. Also avoid ending on the action with no result; the interviewer is left asking "so, did it work?".

3. Building Your Story Bank

In plain words

A story bank is a short list of 8–10 real experiences you have prepared in STAR form. Each one can answer several different questions. With a good bank, you never have to invent a story on the spot.

Most behavioural questions fall into about 10 themes. If you have 2–3 stories for each theme, you are covered for almost anything.

Like a cook's prepared ingredients. Chopped onions, boiled rice and a ready sauce can become many different dishes quickly. Your prepared stories become answers to many different questions.

How to find your 8–10 stories

  1. List raw moments (15 minutes). Go through your résumé line by line, your last 12–18 months of commits or tickets, performance reviews, and messages where someone thanked you. Write every moment you remember, however small.
  2. Look for tension. Good stories have a problem: a bug, a deadline, a disagreement, a mistake, something unclear. Smooth projects make weak stories.
  3. Pick for variety. Choose 8–10 that together cover the columns in the table. Include at least one failure and one conflict; these are asked almost everywhere.
  4. Prefer recent and yours. Last 2 years if possible, and stories where you made decisions. Freshers can use college, internships, clubs, part-time jobs or family responsibilities.
  5. Write STAR bullets. Four lines each. Add one number if you honestly have one.
  6. Fill the worksheet. Copy the table above with your own stories. Any empty column is a gap to fill before the interview.

The same story can answer different questions if you change the angle: which part you stretch and what lesson you end on. Story (B), the webhook bug, is a good example:

Do this

Keep the bank in one document you can read in 10 minutes before an interview. Before each interview, pick your "top 5" for that company based on its values, and know which story you will use for failure and conflict.

Avoid

Using the same story for three questions in one interview. Reuse across interviews is fine; within one interview, the interviewer wants to see range. Also avoid made-up stories: follow-up questions expose them quickly.

4. Tell Me About Yourself

In plain words

"Tell me about yourself" is usually the first question. It is not asking for your life story or your résumé read aloud. It is asking: "Who are you professionally, and why are you a fit for this role?"

Use a simple order: present (what you do now), past (how you got here, with one or two highlights), future (why this role is the next step). Aim for 60–90 seconds.

Like the first slide of a good presentation: it tells the audience who is speaking, why they should listen, and where the talk is going. It sets up the rest of the conversation.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about yourself."Can you summarise yourself clearly and confidently?
"Walk me through your résumé."Is there a sensible story behind your moves?
"Introduce yourself."Which parts of your background match this job?

Template: experienced (60–90 s)

Fill in the blanks

Present: "I'm a ___ developer with about ___ years of experience, currently at ___, where I work on ___ using ___."

Past: "I started out ___. Some work I'm proud of: ___ (result with a number), and ___ (a second highlight showing a different skill)."

Future: "I'm now looking for ___, and this role interests me because ___."

Example (Brightcart developer): "I'm a full-stack developer with about three years' experience at Brightcart, an e-commerce SaaS company. I work across React on the front end and Node and Java Spring services on the back end, with PostgreSQL. I joined as a front-end developer and moved to full-stack in my second year. Two things I'm proud of: I led the checkout performance work that brought load time from 4.2 to 1.6 seconds and lifted conversions by about 6%, and I took over a delayed reporting tool after a teammate left and shipped its core in three weeks. I also mentor our interns. I'm now looking for a role with more ownership of backend systems at larger scale, which is why this team's work on payments interests me."

I also led our college team's attendance app and, with three friends, came second in a 24-hour hackathon. Do this

Plant "hooks": mention one or two stories briefly (like the checkout speed-up) so the interviewer asks about them. You then get to tell your best, most prepared stories.

Avoid

Memorising word for word; it sounds robotic and you freeze if you lose a line. Memorise the three parts and your key numbers, and say it naturally each time. Also avoid personal details (age, marital status, family) unless they are relevant.

5. Communication, Delivery & Virtual Interviews

In plain words

A good story told badly still scores badly. Delivery is how you say it: order, pace, clarity, confidence. In behavioural rounds, your communication is part of what is being assessed, because you will explain your work to others every day on the job.

Like food in a restaurant: the dish can be excellent, but if it arrives cold and messy on the plate, the diner remembers the mess. Delivery is the plating.

Structure: signpost your answer

  • Headline first. Start with one sentence that answers the question: "Yes, the clearest example is when I disagreed with a senior developer about rewriting our orders service."
  • Signpost. "There were three things I did…", "The result was…". These words help the listener follow.
  • Close the loop. End with a result and one line of learning, then stop. Silence after your answer is fine.

Pace and filler words

  • Nervous speakers speed up. Slow down on purpose, especially on numbers and names.
  • Fillers ("um", "like", "basically", "actually", "you know") are normal in small amounts. Replace them with a short pause; a pause sounds thoughtful, a filler sounds unsure.
  • Record yourself answering two questions on your phone. Listen once. Most people notice one habit to fix, and fixing that one habit helps a lot.
  • Avoid heavy jargon with non-technical interviewers (HR, managers from other teams). Explain terms in plain words: "p95 load time, meaning the slowest 5% of page loads".

Thinking time and "I don't know"

SituationWhat to say
You need a moment to pick a story"That's a good question, let me think for a second about the best example." Then take 5–10 seconds.
You do not have an exact example"I haven't faced exactly that, but the closest was…" and tell a related story. Do not invent one.
The question is unclear"Do you mean a disagreement about technical direction, or with a person's behaviour?"
You genuinely do not know (a fact, a process)"I'm not sure. Here is how I would find out…". Honesty scores better than a guess.
You lose your thread mid-answer"Let me step back: the key point is…". Recovering calmly is itself a good signal.

Body language

  • Eye contact: on video, look at the camera when you make a key point, not at your own image.
  • Posture: sit upright, slightly forward; hands visible, calm gestures.
  • Face: a natural smile at the start and end; nod when listening.
  • In person: a firm handshake if offered, greet everyone on the panel, and do not check your phone while waiting.

Do this

Do one full mock interview on the same app, at the same desk, at the same time of day as the real one. It removes most technical surprises and a lot of nerves.

Avoid

Talking non-stop to fill silence, interrupting the interviewer, or answering a question different from the one asked. If you are unsure what they want, ask a short clarifying question first.

6. Teamwork & Collaboration

In plain words

Teamwork questions check whether people will enjoy and benefit from working with you. Can you share work, communicate, help others, and put the team's goal above your own credit?

Like a cricket team: a batter who scores a century but runs out three partners is not a good team player. Interviewers look for someone who makes the whole side stronger.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about working in a team."What role you naturally take, and whether you share credit.
"How do you work with other teams?"Can you coordinate across functions: design, QA, product, backend?
"A teammate is not pulling their weight…"Do you address it directly and kindly, or complain and do it yourself silently?
"Have you helped a colleague?"Generosity with time and knowledge, without being asked.

Common questions in this area

  • Tell me about a time you worked well in a team.
  • Describe a project where you had to collaborate with another team.
  • Tell me about a time a teammate was not contributing. What did you do?
  • How have you helped a teammate who was struggling?
  • Describe a time you had to rely on someone else to finish your work.
  • What role do you usually play in a team?
  • Tell me about a time you worked with someone very different from you.
  • Describe a time you had to share credit, or give credit to someone else.

How to answer

Use STAR, and make the other people visible in your actions: who you spoke to, how you divided work, how you kept everyone informed.

Template

S: "On ___, our team of ___ needed to ___."

T: "My part was ___, but the work depended on ___."

A: "I ___ (set up a way to coordinate), ___ (helped or unblocked someone), ___ (communicated progress or a risk)."

R: "We ___ (outcome). What I took away about working in teams: ___."

Best stories from the bank: (A) checkout speed, which needed the backend team to merge API calls; (D) taking over the reporting tool with the PM; (E) mentoring interns. Fresher: the college attendance app, where one member stopped contributing, or the hackathon.

Good vs weak answer

Weak

"In our college project one person did nothing, so I just did his part as well. We still submitted on time and got good marks. I'm the kind of person who gets things done."

Strong

"In our four-person attendance app, one teammate stopped committing code in week three. I messaged him privately and learned he was struggling with the backend and too embarrassed to say. I paired with him for two evenings on the API setup and we split his remaining work into smaller tasks on our board so progress was visible. He finished the reports module, we submitted on time, and I learned to check in early instead of assuming someone is lazy."

Do this

Name people's roles ("our QA engineer", "the PM") and show at least one moment where you helped someone else succeed. End with a team result, then your personal lesson.

Avoid

Stories where you are the hero and everyone else is weak. Also avoid blaming a teammate by name or calling them lazy; interviewers imagine you describing them the same way one day.

7. Conflict & Disagreement

In plain words

Conflict questions check whether you can disagree without damage: raise a concern, listen, use evidence, reach a decision, and still work well together afterwards. They are asked in almost every behavioural round.

A story with no conflict is a weak answer. So is a story where you "won" and the other person looks foolish. The ideal story shows respect on both sides and a decision based on facts.

Like two doctors disagreeing about a treatment. They do not shout; they look at the test results together, maybe order one more test, and agree on what is best for the patient.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about a disagreement with a colleague."Do you stay calm and use facts rather than rank or emotion?
"…with your manager?"Can you push back respectfully, and accept a decision you lost?
"…with a difficult person?"Empathy: do you try to understand why they behave that way?
"What happened afterwards?"Is the relationship still healthy? Did you commit to the final decision?

Common questions in this area

  • Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate.
  • Describe a disagreement with your manager. How did it end?
  • Tell me about a time you had to work with a difficult person.
  • Have you ever pushed back on a requirement or deadline?
  • Describe a time you were wrong in a disagreement.
  • Tell me about a time you disagreed but went along with the decision.
  • How do you handle a code review comment you disagree with?
  • Describe a conflict between two other people that you helped resolve.

How to answer

Template

S/T: "___ and I disagreed about ___. It mattered because ___."

A: "First I asked about their reasons and learned ___. I explained mine: ___. We agreed the shared goal was ___. To decide, I suggested ___ (data, a spike, asking a third person)."

R: "We chose ___. The result was ___. Our working relationship ___. I learned ___."

Best stories from the bank: (C) rewrite vs refactor with a senior developer, which is the main conflict story; (H) pushing back on the PM who wanted to skip tests before the sale weekend, for "pushback" and manager-type questions. Fresher: the college project member who stopped contributing, or a disagreement over the hackathon idea.

Good vs weak answer

WeakStrong (story C)
"A senior dev wanted to rewrite the whole orders service, which was a bad idea. I told him it was risky and eventually the manager agreed with me.""A senior developer wanted to rewrite our orders service; I felt an incremental refactor was safer. I asked what pain he was seeing, and he was right that the code was hard to change. We agreed the goal was faster changes without risking orders. I proposed a two-day spike: he sketched the rewrite, I refactored one module. The refactor showed most of the gain with far less risk, so we chose it, and he led the design of the new module boundaries. We still pair regularly."
Other person looks foolish; manager "picked a winner".Both sides respected; evidence decided; relationship improved.
Do this

Show that you considered you might be wrong. It is even stronger to have one story where the other person's idea won and you committed fully: "I disagreed, we went with her approach, and I made sure it succeeded."

Avoid

"I don't really have conflicts, I get along with everyone." It sounds either untrue or conflict-avoidant. Also avoid stories about personal dislikes, gossip, or escalating to a manager as your first move.

8. Leadership & Ownership

In plain words

Ownership means treating a problem as yours until it is solved, even when it is not strictly your job. Leadership for engineers rarely means having a title; it means influencing people and direction without authority: proposing a plan, mentoring, taking charge in a crisis.

Freshers are not expected to have managed people. They are expected to show initiative: taking a lead in a project, a club, or a hackathon.

Like a flatmate who notices the water tank is leaking. A tenant says "not my problem, tell the landlord". An owner puts a bucket under it, calls the plumber, and tells everyone which tap to avoid.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about a time you took ownership."Do you step in when something falls between the cracks?
"Tell me about a time you led without authority."Can you persuade peers with clarity and evidence?
"Describe going beyond your role."Initiative, and judgement about when it is worth it.
"Have you mentored anyone?"Patience, and whether you grow other people.
"What happened after it shipped?"Do you follow through: monitoring, docs, follow-up fixes?

Common questions in this area

  • Tell me about a time you took ownership of a problem that was not yours.
  • Describe a time you led a project or initiative.
  • Tell me about a time you influenced a decision without formal authority.
  • Describe a time you took over someone else's work mid-way.
  • Have you mentored or trained someone? How did it go?
  • Tell me about a time you had to make a decision with incomplete information.
  • Describe a time you went beyond what was asked.
  • Tell me about something you owned end to end, from idea to production.

How to answer

Template

S: "___ was at risk because ___, and nobody clearly owned it."

T: "I decided to take it on because ___ (impact on users or the team)."

A: "I ___ (got clarity: scope, owners, deadline), ___ (made a plan and shared it), ___ (did the hard part myself or brought in the right people), ___ (followed through after launch)."

R: "___ (outcome with a number). Afterwards I ___ (doc, process, handover) so it would not depend on me."

Best stories from the bank: (D) took over the delayed reporting tool, re-scoped with the PM, shipped the core in 3 weeks: the main ownership story; (B) owned the webhook bug fix, refunds and postmortem; (E) mentoring two interns: the main leadership story; (A) led the checkout performance work. Fresher: leading the college attendance app, or the internship dashboard you owned end to end.

Good vs weak answer

Weak

"When my teammate left, I was given his reporting project. I worked extra hours every day and finished it. I always give 100%."

Strong (story D)

"When a teammate left, our internal reporting tool was already four weeks late and had no clear scope. I read his code and open tickets for two days, then met the PM and listed the 12 requested features by how often finance used them. We agreed to ship the top four first. I set a weekly demo so stakeholders saw progress, and asked a colleague to review the data queries because that was the riskiest part. We shipped the core in three weeks and the finance team stopped building the reports by hand. I also wrote a short handover doc so nobody would be stuck the way I was."

Do this

Show judgement, not just effort: how you decided what mattered, who you involved, and how you made the outcome last beyond you (docs, tests, process).

Avoid

Equating ownership with long hours or doing everything alone. Also avoid taking over someone's work without telling them or your manager; that reads as poor collaboration, not leadership.

9. Failure, Mistakes & Learning

In plain words

Everyone who ships software breaks something sooner or later. When an interviewer asks about a failure, they are not hunting for a perfect person. They want to know what you do after things go wrong: do you hide, blame, or own it and fix it?

The best failure answers are honest, show a real cost, and end with a change you still follow today.

Think of a good doctor who once gave the wrong dose. You do not trust the doctor who says "that never happens to me". You trust the one who says "it happened once, I caught it, told the patient, and now I double-check every chart".

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about a time you failed."Self-awareness. Can you name a real failure without shrinking it to nothing?
"What was your role in it?"Ownership. You say "I", not "the team" or "the PM".
"What did you do next?"Calm action under stress: contain the damage first, then fix the cause.
"What would you do differently?"Learning. A specific habit or process change, not "I learned to be careful".
"Has it happened again?"Proof the lesson stuck. Ideally a later moment where the new habit helped.

Common questions in this area

  • Tell me about a time you failed.

10. Pressure, Deadlines & Prioritisation

In plain words

Software work always has more to do than time to do it. These questions check whether you stay calm, decide what matters most, and tell people early when something will not fit.

The interviewer is not impressed by "I worked all night". They are impressed by "I cut the scope in a smart way and told the PM on day two, not on the last day".

It is like packing for a flight with a 7 kg bag limit. You cannot take everything. The skill is choosing what goes in, what gets shipped later, and not arguing with the scale at the airport.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"How do you handle tight deadlines?"A method, not a mood. How you break work down and decide order.

11. Problem Solving & Initiative

In plain words

These questions look for two things. Problem solving: when something is broken or unclear, can you break it down, find the real cause, and fix it? Initiative: do you act on problems nobody assigned to you, or wait to be told?

Your answer should show your thinking steps, not just the final fix. The interviewer is judging how you would behave on their messy, real problems.

A good mechanic does not just replace parts until the noise stops. They listen, test one thing at a time, find the loose bolt, and then tell you how to stop it happening again. Initiative is the mechanic who also mentions your worn tyres without being asked.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about a hard problem you solved."A clear method: measure, guess, test, confirm. Not luck.
"How did you find the root cause?"Data and tools (logs, profiler, metrics), not guesswork.

"Did anyone ask you to do this?"Initiative. You spotted it and acted within sensible limits.
"What was the impact?"A result you measured: time saved, errors cut, money earned.

  • Tell me about a time you simplified something complex.

12. Adaptability & Learning Fast

In plain words

Tech stacks, teams, managers and priorities change all the time. These questions test whether you adjust calmly and can pick up something new quickly without waiting for a perfect course.

13. Giving & Receiving Feedback

In plain words

Teams grow through honest feedback. These questions test two sides: can you hear criticism without getting defensive, and can you give it kindly and clearly so the other person can act on it?

"How do you ask for feedback?"You seek it out; you do not wait for the yearly review.

17. Strengths & Weaknesses

In plain words

This classic HR question checks self-awareness. For strengths: can you name something real and prove it? For weaknesses: can you be honest about a genuine gap, and show you are actively working on it?

Interviewers have heard "I am a perfectionist" thousands of times. A real weakness, told with the steps you took, sounds far more confident than a disguised boast.

Like a cricket player's stats. A batter who says "I am great against spin, here are my numbers, and I am working on short-pitched bowling with extra nets every week" sounds like a professional. One who says "my weakness is that I score too many runs" sounds like he is hiding something.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"What are your strengths?"Two or three strengths that matter for this role, each backed by a story.
"What is your greatest weakness?"Honesty, self-awareness, and a clear improvement plan already under way.

"How are you working on it?"Specific steps and some evidence of progress.

18. Career Goals

In plain words

"Where do you see yourself in five years?" is not a request for a prophecy. The interviewer wants to know three things: do you have a direction, does this job fit that direction, and will you stay long enough to be worth the training they give you.

A good answer links your goal to the role in front of you. You want to grow in a way that also helps them.

It is like a cab driver asking "Where to?" before you get in. "Anywhere" is worrying. "Towards the airport, and this road goes there" is exactly what they want to hear.

19. Salary, Notice Period & Negotiation

In plain words

Salary talk makes most people nervous, but it is a normal business conversation. The company wants to hire you within its budget. You want a fair offer. Both sides do better when you know the market rate and speak calmly about it.

Two words you will hear a lot in India: CTC (cost to company — everything the company spends on you in a year, including fixed pay, variable pay, bonuses and employer PF) and in-hand (what actually reaches your bank account each month). They are very different numbers, so always ask which one is being discussed.

It is like selling a used bike. If you check the market price first, you can name a fair number with confidence. If you guess, you either scare the buyer or undersell yourself.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"What is your current CTC?"A baseline for their offer. Also honesty — this is checked later.
"What is your expected CTC?"Whether you fit their band, and whether your expectation is based on reason or on a guess.
"What is your notice period?"When you can actually join, and whether they must plan for a delay.
"Can you join earlier? Is buyout possible?"How flexible you are, and whether you understand your own company's policy.
"Do you have other offers?"How urgent it is to close you, and whether you are using them only as leverage.

Common questions in this area

  • "What is your current CTC, and what is the fixed / variable split?"
  • "What are your salary expectations?"
  • "This is the top of our band. Can you accept it?"
  • "What is your notice period? Is it negotiable?"
  • "Are you currently serving notice? What is your last working day?"
  • "If we make an offer, will you join? Do you have any other offers in hand?"
  • "Would you be open to a lower fixed with a higher joining bonus?"
  • For freshers: "Are you okay with the package and the location offered?"

Step 1 — Research the range

  • Salary sites: Glassdoor, AmbitionBox and Levels.fyi show reported pay by company, role and experience. Look at several and take the middle, not the highest entry.
  • People: ask seniors, alumni and friends at similar companies. Ask "what is the range for 3 years' experience?" rather than "what do you earn?".
  • Recruiters: a recruiter who calls you often shares the band if you ask directly: "What is the budget for this role?"
  • Know your own numbers: current fixed, variable, bonus, stock, and your in-hand. Know what your total would be if you stayed another year (appraisal, pending bonus, unvested stock).

Step 2 — When to say a number

If asked very early (first screening call), you can politely defer: "I would like to understand the role and the responsibilities first. I am sure we can agree on something fair if it is the right fit." If they insist, or the form has a mandatory field, give your researched range. Many Indian companies ask for current and expected CTC on the first call, and refusing entirely can look evasive, so do not stonewall.

Step 3 — Give a range anchored on research

Fill-in template

"Based on what I have seen for [role] with [X years] in [city], and the scope of this role, I am looking at [low] to [high] CTC, with most of it fixed. I am flexible on the structure if the overall package is right."

Make the bottom of your range a number you would genuinely be happy with, because companies often offer near the bottom. Base it on market data and the role, not only on "current CTC plus a percentage".

Step 4 — Negotiate politely

  • Thank them first and show you want to join: "Thank you, I am really excited about this role."
  • Ask one clear question: "Is there any flexibility on the fixed component? Based on my research and my experience with (A) the checkout performance work, I was expecting closer to [number]."
  • If the fixed cannot move, ask about other parts: joining bonus, notice buyout, stock, relocation support, an early salary review.
  • Only mention another offer if it is real, and state it plainly without threats.
  • Once you agree, stop negotiating. Accept graciously.

20. Tricky HR Questions: Gaps, Switches, Relocation

In plain words

Some HR questions poke at something on your CV that looks like a risk: a gap, several short jobs, a layoff, a move to a new city. The interviewer is not trying to trap you. They want to hear that you understand why it looks risky and that the risk does not apply now.

The formula is always the same: short honest fact, what you did or learned, why it is not a problem for this job. Then stop talking.

22. Amazon Leadership Principles

In plain words

Amazon publishes a list of 16 Leadership Principles (LPs). They are not decoration — interviewers use them as the scoring sheet. Almost every behavioural question at Amazon is really "show me a time you lived this principle". Even technical rounds usually include one or two LP questions.

So the preparation is simple to describe: know all 16, and have at least one story for each, with the most common ones covered twice. Your story bank from section 3 already does most of the work.

It is like an exam where the syllabus is printed on the front page. Nobody knows the exact question, but you know every topic it can come from — so there is no excuse for leaving a topic out.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about a time…" (for a specific LP)A real, specific story with your actions and a measurable result.
Follow-ups: "What exactly did you do?", "What data did you use?", "What would you do differently?"Depth. Amazon interviewers dig several layers into one story to check it is real and that you drove it.
"What was the result?"Numbers and business impact, not "it went well".
"Tell me about a time you failed / were wrong"Ownership and learning, without blaming others.

How the loop works

A typical Amazon interview loop has several interviewers. Each one is usually given a few LPs to probe, so the same principle is rarely tested twice. One of them is often a Bar Raiser: an experienced interviewer from outside the hiring team, trained to keep the hiring standard high across the company. The Bar Raiser pays close attention to LPs and has a strong voice in the final decision. You will not always know who it is, so treat every round the same.

  • Prepare for 3–4 follow-ups per story: "why that approach?", "what was the hardest part?", "what did you learn?", "what would you do differently?".
  • Use recent stories (last 2–3 years where possible). Freshers can use internship, project and hackathon stories.
  • Do not repeat the same story with every interviewer. Spread (A)–(I) across the loop.

Good vs weak answer

Weak (Dive Deep)

"We had a payments issue once. The team looked into the logs and found the bug and fixed it. It was a good learning experience."

Strong (Dive Deep)

"38 customers were charged twice during a sale. Support said it was random, but I pulled the webhook logs and saw every duplicate had the same event ID arriving twice within 2 seconds. Our handler was not idempotent. I added idempotency keys, wrote a script to find every affected order, and got all 38 refunded within a day. I also wrote the postmortem. We have had zero duplicate charges since."

Do this

Map at least one story to all 16 LPs, and two stories to Customer Obsession, Ownership, Dive Deep, Deliver Results and Have Backbone. Practise the follow-ups aloud. Include a failure story you can tell without blaming anyone.

Avoid

Hypothetical answers ("I would…"). "We" all the way through. Stories without results. Making up stories — deep follow-ups expose them quickly. Bad-mouthing a colleague in a conflict story.

23. Google, Microsoft, Meta & Startups

In plain words

Every company asks the same kinds of behavioural questions, but each one listens for slightly different things. Knowing what they value lets you pick the right story from your bank and stress the right part of it. You do not need a new set of stories for each company.

It is like cooking the same dal for different guests: a little less spice for one, a little more ghee for another. The recipe is yours; you only adjust the seasoning.

Google

Google has publicly described four broad attributes it looks for:

  • General cognitive ability — how you approach and break down problems, not just your grades.
  • Role-related knowledge — the skills and experience for this particular job.
  • Leadership — stepping up when needed, even without a formal title, and stepping back when someone else should lead.
  • "Googleyness" — broadly: comfort with ambiguity, collaboration, humility, a bias to act, and doing the right thing for users and the team.

Behavioural questions often come as hypotheticals too: "What would you do if a teammate kept missing deadlines?" Answer the hypothetical with a clear approach, then add "I faced something similar…" and give a short real story.

Adjust your answer

For story (C), stress the reasoning: how you framed rewrite vs refactor, what data the spike gave, and how you brought the senior dev along without authority.

Microsoft

Microsoft talks openly about a growth mindset: the belief that ability grows with effort and learning. Interviewers listen for curiosity, learning from failure, asking for feedback, and helping colleagues succeed.

  • Expect questions like "Tell me about a time you failed", "What did you learn from feedback?", "Tell me about something new you learned recently".
  • Use words like "I learned", "I changed how I…", "I asked for feedback", and show the change actually happened.
Adjust your answer

For story (F), spend more time on what changed: "Since then I break estimates into tasks, add a buffer for unknowns, and flag risk at the first stand-up. My last four estimates were within a few days."

Meta

Meta's behavioural interview is commonly described as focusing on a few themes:

  • Conflict — disagreements with peers or managers, and how you resolved them.
  • Growth — feedback you received, weaknesses you worked on, how you have changed.
  • Ambiguity — making progress when the goal or plan was unclear.
  • Impact — the biggest results you drove, and how you chose what mattered most.

Answers should show scope and impact that match the level you are applying for. Expect "what would you do differently?" as a follow-up.

Adjust your answer

For story (D), stress the ambiguity: no documentation, a half-built tool, unclear priorities — and how you re-scoped with the PM to ship the core in 3 weeks.

Startups

Startups have small teams and few processes. They want people who own outcomes, move fast, and are happy to do things outside their job title.

  • Expect: "Tell me about a time you did something outside your role", "How do you handle changing priorities?", "What would you build first in our product?".
  • Show ownership (you finish things), speed (you ship and learn), and many hats (a developer who also talks to customers or sets up deployment).
  • Try the product before the interview and have one thoughtful suggestion.
Adjust your answer

Stories (G) and (I) are perfect: you learned Kubernetes to unblock a deployment, and you joined a customer call and built a validation report nobody asked for.

  • "Tell me about a time you failed and what you learned." (Microsoft, all)
  • "Tell me about a time you worked on something with an unclear goal." (Meta, Google)
  • "What is the most impactful project you have worked on?" (all)
  • "Tell me about a time you led without formal authority." (Google)
  • "How do you learn a new technology quickly?" (Microsoft, startups)
  • "Why do you want to work at a startup instead of a big company?" (startups)

24. Service Companies & Campus Placements

In plain words

Large IT service companies such as TCS, Infosys, Wipro, Accenture and Cognizant hire freshers in big numbers, mostly through campus drives and national hiring tests. Their HR round is usually shorter and friendlier than a product-company behavioural round. They mainly check communication, attitude, flexibility and whether you will join and stay.

Many students lose these offers not on skill but on small things: a nervous self-introduction, saying no to relocation, or not knowing basic facts about the company.

It is like a driving test for a licence, not a racing trial. They are not looking for the fastest driver; they want someone safe, steady and willing to follow the road rules.

What they are really asking

They ask…They want to see…
"Tell me about yourself."Clear, confident English (or the language of the interview) and a structured answer.
"Are you willing to relocate / work in shifts?"Flexibility. Service companies place people where projects need them.

"Why our company?"Basic research and genuine interest, not "because it came to campus".
"Do you plan higher studies?"Whether you will leave during or soon after training.

25. Common Mistakes & Red Flags

In plain words

Interviewers often reject a candidate for one red flag — a moment that makes them worry about working with you — even if the rest was fine. Most red flags are easy to avoid once you know them.

It is like a cricket innings: a hundred good balls do not matter if you get run out by a silly mistake. Avoiding the run-out is part of batting well.

What they are really asking

They notice…They worry that…
Blaming others in every storyYou will blame the team here too.
Only "we", never "I"You did not actually do the work.
Vague answers with no resultsThe story is made up, or had no impact.
Arrogance or dismissing feedbackYou will be hard to work with.
Inconsistencies with your CVYou are not honest.

Smaller red flags people miss

  • Checking your phone or looking away for notes constantly.
  • Talking about salary or leave in the first technical round.
  • Negative body language: crossed arms, no eye contact, sighing at a question.
  • Over-rehearsed, robotic answers that do not match the question asked.
  • Not letting the interviewer finish a question.
  • Forgetting the details of your own CV.

How to recover in the moment

Mistakes happen. What matters is how you recover.

  • Went blank: "Can I take a moment to think of the best example?" — then take 10 seconds.
  • Rambling: "Let me get to the result…" and finish.
  • Said something wrong: "Actually, let me correct that — it was 3 sprints, not 2."
  • Wrong story for the question: "I realise that answered a slightly different question. A better example is…"

Good vs weak answer

Weak

"Our search feature was late because the PM kept changing things and the backend team was slow. We eventually shipped it."

Strong

I told the PM as soon as I saw the slip and re-planned. Since then I break work into tasks under two days and flag risks in the first stand-up. My estimates have been much closer since."

Do this

Record yourself answering five questions and watch it back for the mistakes above. Ask a friend to note every time you say "we" or blame someone. Keep answers to about two minutes with a clear result.

Avoid

Blame, lies, rambling and "no questions". Any one of these can undo an otherwise good interview.

  • Virtual: close other apps and notifications, phone on silent, test camera, mic and internet, plug in the charger, check lighting (face the light), plain background.
  • In person: reach 15 minutes early; find the washroom and reception first.
  • Keep water, a pen, a notepad and your one-page story list nearby.

Prepare logistics the night before so the morning is calm. Review, do not cram. Keep a one-page story list next to you.

Avoid

Learning new stories or frameworks the night before. Staying up late. Joining a video call from a noisy place or on low battery.
