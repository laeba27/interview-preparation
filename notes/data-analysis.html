Data Analysis

Simple notes for data analyst and business analyst interviews. Every term is explained the first time it appears. Each topic has an everyday example and small made-up data you can follow by hand.

Part 1 — Foundations

1. What Data Analysis Is

In plain words

Data analysis means looking at data to answer a question and help someone make a better decision. Data is simply recorded facts: sales, clicks, marks, prices, ratings.

Think of a doctor. Your temperature, BP and blood test are the data. The doctor reads them, finds the problem and suggests a medicine. An analyst does the same thing for a business.

Key points

  • The goal is a decision, not a chart. A chart is only a tool to explain the answer.
  • Raw data → information (organised data) → insight (what it means) → action (what to do).
  • Good analysis starts with a clear business question. Example: "Why did orders drop last month?"
  • Companies use it everywhere: pricing, marketing, stock planning, fraud checks, hiring.
  • Data analysis explains what happened and why. Data science goes further and builds models to predict the future.

Small example

StageExample
DataA kirana shop sold 40 packets of Maggi on Monday and 95 on Saturday.
InformationWeekend sales are more than double weekday sales.
InsightFamilies shop on weekends, so demand jumps then.
ActionOrder extra stock every Friday.
Remember: data analysis = question → data → insight → decision. If no decision changes, the analysis did not help.

2. The Data Analyst Role

In plain words

A data analyst collects, cleans and studies data, then explains the findings to people who are not technical, like managers. A business analyst focuses more on business needs and processes, and uses data to support them.

An analyst is like a translator. The data speaks in numbers. The manager speaks in business. The analyst turns one into the other.

Key points

  • Daily work: write SQL queries, clean data in Excel or Python, build dashboards, answer questions from teams.
  • Dashboard = a screen of charts and numbers that updates by itself. Built in tools like Power BI or Tableau.
  • Stakeholder = the person who asked the question or will use your answer. Example: the marketing head.
  • Hard skills: Excel, SQL, basic statistics, one BI tool, some Python.
  • Soft skills: asking good questions, clear writing, simple explanations, business sense.
  • Common fresher job titles in India: Data Analyst, Business Analyst, MIS Executive, Product Analyst, Analyst Trainee.
RoleMain questionMain tools

Business AnalystWhat does the business need?Excel, documents, process diagrams, some SQL
Data ScientistWhat will happen next?Python, statistics, machine learning

Remember: in interviews, say the analyst's job is "turning data into decisions". Then mention SQL, Excel and communication.

3. Types of Analytics

In plain words

There are four types of analytics. Each one answers a different question. They go from easy to hard.

Think about a cricket team that lost a match. "We lost by 20 runs" is descriptive. "Our middle order collapsed" is diagnostic. "We may lose to spin again" is predictive. "Pick one extra batter next match" is prescriptive.

TypeQuestion it answersBusiness example
DescriptiveWhat happened?Sales were ₹12 lakh in June.
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Sales fell because a big offer ended.
PredictiveWhat will happen?July sales will be about ₹11 lakh.
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Run a 10% offer on weekends to reach ₹13 lakh.

Key points

  • Descriptive uses totals, averages and charts. Most fresher work is here.
  • Diagnostic uses drill-downs (breaking a number into parts) and comparisons.
  • Predictive uses past patterns and models, like regression.
  • Prescriptive suggests an action, often by testing options.
  • Value and difficulty both go up as you move down the table.
Remember: What happened? Why? What next? What to do? That order is the easiest way to recall all four.

4. The Analysis Process

In plain words

Analysts follow a fixed set of steps. Google's popular version has six: Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyse, Share, Act.

It is like cooking a meal. Decide what to cook (ask), buy the vegetables (prepare), wash and chop them (process), cook (analyse), serve the plate (share), and see if people enjoy it (act).

Key points

  • Ask: understand the problem. Use SMART questions: Specific, Measurable, Action-oriented, Relevant, Time-bound.
  • Prepare: find and collect the right data. Check it is reliable.
  • Process: clean it. Fix blanks, duplicates and wrong formats.
  • Analyse: calculate, group, compare, find trends.
  • Share: present findings with clear charts and a short story.
  • Act: the business makes a decision based on your work.
  • CRISP-DM is another famous process with six steps: Business Understanding, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Modelling, Evaluation, Deployment. It is the same idea, used more in data science.
Remember: Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyse, Share, Act. If asked "how do you approach a problem?", walk through these six steps with one example.
Part 2 — Understanding Data

5. Data Types

In plain words

Data comes in different shapes. Structured data fits neatly in rows and columns. Unstructured data does not, like photos, videos and free text.

Structured data is like a train timetable: every train has the same columns. Unstructured data is like a WhatsApp group chat: useful, but messy and hard to put in a table.

Structure

  • Structured: tables in Excel or a database. Example: an orders table. Easy to query with SQL.
  • Semi-structured: has some labels but no fixed table. Example: JSON files, emails (sender, subject, then free text).
  • Unstructured: no fixed format. Example: product reviews, images, call recordings. About 80% of company data is like this.

Qualitative vs quantitative

  • Quantitative = numbers you can do maths on. Example: price, age, number of orders.
  • Qualitative (also called categorical) = labels or groups. Example: city, gender, payment mode.
  • Quantitative data is either discrete (counted, whole numbers: 3 children) or continuous (measured, can have decimals: 64.5 kg).

Remember: a number is not always quantitative. Pin codes, phone numbers and roll numbers are labels. Ask: "Does averaging this make sense?"

6. Levels of Measurement

In plain words

The level of measurement tells you what maths you are allowed to do with a column. There are four levels: Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio (remember "NOIR").

Think of a race. Runner names are nominal. Finishing positions (1st, 2nd, 3rd) are ordinal. The start time on a clock is interval. Time taken to finish is ratio.

LevelWhat it meansExampleAllowed maths
NominalNames or groups, no orderCity, blood group, payment modeCount, mode
OrdinalGroups with an order, but gaps are not equalRating: Poor / OK / Good, T-shirt size S/M/L+ median, ranking
IntervalEqual gaps, but no true zeroTemperature in °C, calendar year+ add, subtract, mean
RatioEqual gaps and a true zeroSalary, weight, age, sales+ multiply, divide, "twice as much"

Key points

  • True zero means zero = "none at all". ₹0 salary means no money. But 0 °C does not mean "no temperature".
  • That is why 40 °C is not "twice as hot" as 20 °C. But ₹40,000 is twice ₹20,000.
  • Ordinal gaps are unequal: the jump from "Poor" to "OK" may not equal "OK" to "Good".
  • Nominal and ordinal are qualitative. Interval and ratio are quantitative.
Remember: NOIR. Each level can do everything the level above it can, plus one more thing.

7. Data Sources & Collection

In plain words

A data source is where data comes from. Primary data is collected by you for your question. Secondary data was collected by someone else, for some other reason.

Primary data is cooking at home: you choose every ingredient. Secondary data is ordering from a restaurant: quick, but you don't know exactly how it was made.

Key points

  • Internal sources: the company's own databases, CRM (customer records), sales systems, website logs.
  • External sources: government data (data.gov.in, Census), industry reports, Kaggle datasets, public APIs.
  • Collection methods: surveys, forms, app tracking, sensors, interviews, web scraping (reading data from websites).
  • API (Application Programming Interface) = a doorway that lets one program ask another program for data.
  • Check data quality with ROCCC: Reliable, Original, Comprehensive, Current, Cited.
  • Be careful with personal data. Collect only what you need and keep it safe. India's DPDP Act 2023 sets rules for this.

Part 3 — Preparing Data

8. Data Cleaning: Missing Values

In plain words

Data cleaning means fixing or removing wrong, missing or messy data before analysis. A missing value is an empty cell, often shown as blank, NULL or NaN ("Not a Number").

Cleaning data is like picking stones out of rice before cooking. Skip it, and the whole meal is spoiled.

Ways to handle missing values

  • Delete the row: fine if only a few rows are missing and they are random.
  • Delete the column: if most of the column is empty (say 70% blank), it may be useless.
  • Fill with mean (average): for numbers without extreme values.
  • Fill with median (middle value): for numbers with extreme values, like salary.
  • Fill with mode (most common value): for categories, like city.
  • Fill with a label: write "Unknown" so the gap stays visible.
  • Forward fill: copy the previous value. Good for time data, like daily stock prices.

Worked example

CustomerAge (before)City (before)Age (after)City (after)
C122Pune22Pune
C2blankPune30Pune
C330blank30Pune
C441Nagpur41Nagpur
C5blankPune30Pune
  • Known ages: 22, 30, 41. Median = 30 (the middle one). Mean = (22 + 30 + 41) ÷ 3 = 93 ÷ 3 = 31.
  • We filled age with the median, 30. Mean (31) would also be fine here.
  • Known cities: Pune, Pune, Pune, Nagpur. Mode = Pune. So the blank city becomes Pune.
Remember: first ask why data is missing. If rich customers skip the "income" question, filling it with the average hides a real pattern.

9. Duplicates, Outliers and Formats

In plain words

A duplicate is the same record entered twice. An outlier is a value very far from the others. Inconsistent formats means the same thing is written in different ways.

In a class attendance sheet, one student written twice is a duplicate. A student aged 95 is an outlier. "Mumbai", "mumbai" and "Bombay" are inconsistent formats for one city.

Before cleaning

order_idcityorder_dateamount (₹)

102mumbai 2025/01/05520
102mumbai 2025/01/05520
103BombayJan 6, 202548000
104Delhi06-01-2025-300

What we fix

  • Duplicate: order 102 appears twice. Keep one. Otherwise sales are counted twice.
  • Text format: trim extra spaces, use one case, and map "Bombay" to "Mumbai".
  • Date format: convert all dates to one standard, like 2025-01-05 (YYYY-MM-DD).
  • Outlier: ₹48,000 is far above the rest. Check it. A bulk order is real. A typo (₹480) should be fixed.
  • Invalid value: a negative amount is impossible for a sale. Maybe it is a refund. Ask the data owner.

How to find outliers

  • IQR rule: anything below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR (see topic 13).
  • Z-score rule: anything more than 3 standard deviations from the mean (see topic 12).
  • Box plot or scatter plot: outliers stand out visually.
Remember: never delete an outlier just because it is big. First find out if it is an error or a real, important event.

10. Data Wrangling

In plain words

Data wrangling (also called data transformation or data munging) means reshaping clean data so it is ready for analysis. You add columns, combine tables, change types and summarise.

Cleaning is washing the vegetables. Wrangling is chopping them into the right size and shape for the recipe.

Common steps

  • Filter: keep only rows you need. Example: only 2025 orders.
  • Derive a column: make a new column from others. Example: revenue = price × quantity.
  • Change data type: text "500" → number 500, text "2025-01-05" → a real date.
  • Split / combine: "Asha Verma" → first name and last name. Or join them back.
  • Merge (join): combine orders with customer details using a shared ID.
  • Aggregate: summarise many rows into a few. Example: total sales per city.
  • Bin: put numbers into ranges. Example: age 18–25, 26–35, 36+.
  • Pivot: turn row values into columns, like a pivot table.
  • Normalise / scale: bring numbers to one range, like 0 to 1, so they are easy to compare.

Small example

productpriceqtyrevenue (new)price band (new)
Pen2010200Low
Bag90021800High
Notebook605300Low

Rule used for the band: price under ₹500 = Low, ₹500 or more = High.

Remember: cleaning fixes what is wrong. Wrangling reshapes what is right. Both happen before analysis.
Part 4 — Statistics

11. Mean, Median, Mode

In plain words

These three numbers describe the centre of your data, the "typical" value. Mean = average. Median = the middle value after sorting. Mode = the most common value.

If 10 friends share a pizza bill, the mean is what each pays if you split it equally. The median is what the "middle" friend ate. The mode is the topping most people chose.

Worked example

Ravi's tea stall sold this many cups in 8 hours: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 (already sorted).

MeasureHow to calculateAnswer
MeanSum ÷ count = (2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9) ÷ 8 = 40 ÷ 85
Median8 values, so average the 4th and 5th: (4 + 5) ÷ 24.5
Mode4 appears three times, more than any other4

Key points

  • Odd count: the median is the single middle value. Even count: average the two middle values.
  • The mean is pulled by extreme values. The median is not. This is why we say the median is robust.
  • Example: salaries ₹20k, ₹25k, ₹30k and the boss's ₹5 lakh. Mean = ₹1,43,750. Median = ₹27,500. The median is the honest "typical" salary.
  • Data can have no mode, one mode, or two modes (bimodal).
  • Use the mode for categories: "Which payment mode is most popular? UPI."
Remember: skewed data or outliers → use the median. Symmetric data → the mean is fine. Categories → use the mode.

12. Spread: Range, Variance, Standard Deviation

In plain words

Spread (or dispersion) tells you how far values are from each other. The centre alone is not enough. Standard deviation (SD) is the average distance of values from the mean.

Two batters both average 40 runs. One scores 38, 40, 42 every match. The other scores 0, 0, 120. Same mean, very different spread. You would trust the first one more.

Worked example (same tea data)

Data: 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9. Mean = 5.

  • Range = max − min = 9 − 2 = 7.
  • Population variance = 32 ÷ 8 = 4. Use this when you have all the data.
  • Population SD = √4 = 2. So values are typically about 2 cups away from the mean.
  • Sample variance = 32 ÷ (8 − 1) = 32 ÷ 7 ≈ 4.57. Use n − 1 when your data is only a sample of a bigger group.
  • Sample SD = √4.57 ≈ 2.14.

Key points

  • We square the differences so negatives don't cancel positives. (Notice the "x − mean" column adds up to 0.)
  • Variance is in squared units ("cups²"), which is hard to read. SD brings it back to normal units ("cups").
  • Small SD = values are close together (consistent). Large SD = values are spread out.
  • Z-score = (x − mean) ÷ SD. It says how many SDs a value is from the mean. For 9 cups: (9 − 5) ÷ 2 = 2.
  • Range is easy but uses only two values, so one outlier can change it a lot.
  • In Excel: =VAR.S(), =STDEV.S() for samples; =VAR.P(), =STDEV.P() for populations.
Remember: variance = average of squared distances from the mean. SD = square root of variance. Sample → divide by n − 1.

13. Percentiles, Quartiles and IQR

In plain words

A percentile tells you what percent of values are below a point. Quartiles cut sorted data into four equal parts. The IQR (interquartile range) is the spread of the middle 50%.

If you are at the 90th percentile in CAT, you scored more than 90% of test-takers. It is about your position, not your marks.

Worked example

Key points

  • 25th percentile = Q1, 50th = median = Q2, 75th = Q3.
  • IQR ignores the top 25% and bottom 25%, so outliers don't affect it. Use it for skewed data.
  • Different tools use slightly different methods. Excel's QUARTILE.INC gives Q1 = 14.5 and Q3 = 23.5 for this data. Both are acceptable; just say which you used.
  • Business use: "90th percentile delivery time is 40 minutes" means 90% of orders arrive within 40 minutes.
Remember: IQR = Q3 − Q1. Outlier if below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR.

14. Distributions and Skew

In plain words

A distribution shows how often each value appears. You see it with a histogram, a bar chart of value ranges. Its shape tells a story.

Heights of students in a class form a hill: most are medium, a few are very short or very tall. That hill shape is the normal distribution.

Key points

  • Normal distribution: bell-shaped and symmetric. Mean = median = mode.
  • 68–95–99.7 rule: in a normal distribution, about 68% of values are within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD, and 99.7% within 3 SD.
  • Example: exam marks with mean 60 and SD 10. About 68% of students score 50–70, and 95% score 40–80.
  • Right skew (positive): long tail of big values. Mean > median. Examples: income, house prices, order values.
  • Left skew (negative): long tail of small values. Mean < median. Example: marks in a very easy exam.
  • Uniform distribution: all values equally likely, like rolling a fair dice.
  • Bimodal: two peaks. Often means two groups are mixed, like weekday and weekend orders.
Remember: the mean follows the tail. Right skew → mean bigger than median. For skewed data, report the median.

15. Correlation vs Causation

In plain words

Correlation means two things move together. Causation means one thing actually makes the other happen. Correlation does not prove causation.

Ice cream sales and drowning cases both rise in summer. Ice cream does not cause drowning. Hot weather causes both. Hot weather is the hidden third factor.

Key points

  • The correlation coefficient (r, Pearson's r) goes from −1 to +1.
  • +1 = perfect positive (both go up together). −1 = perfect negative (one up, other down). 0 = no straight-line link.
  • Rough guide: |r| above 0.7 = strong, 0.3 to 0.7 = moderate, below 0.3 = weak.
  • r only measures straight-line links. A curved pattern can have r near 0.
  • A confounding variable is a hidden third factor that affects both things (like hot weather above).
  • To prove causation, run a controlled experiment, like an A/B test (topic 20).
  • In Excel: =CORREL(A2:A6, B2:B6).

Small example

Study hours12345
Marks4050556570

Here r ≈ 0.99, a very strong positive correlation. But the link is still only proven to be a pattern, not a cause.

Remember: "Correlation is not causation." Always ask: could a third factor explain both?

16. Probability Basics

In plain words

Probability is how likely something is to happen. It goes from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). 0.5 means a 50% chance.

A weather app saying "70% chance of rain" is probability. It doesn't promise rain. It says: on 10 days like this, it rains on about 7.

Key points

  • Basic formula: P(event) = favourable outcomes ÷ total outcomes.
  • Rolling a 4 on a dice: 1 ÷ 6 ≈ 0.167.
  • NOT rule: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). Not rolling a 4 = 5 ÷ 6.
  • AND rule (independent events, one does not affect the other): multiply. Two heads in two coin tosses = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25.
  • OR rule (events that can't both happen): add. Rolling a 1 or a 2 = 1/6 + 1/6 = 1/3.
  • Conditional probability P(A | B) = chance of A, given B already happened. Read "|" as "given".
  • Expected value = the long-run average result. Sum of (value × probability).

Business example

BoughtDid not buyTotal
Used a coupon60140200

  • P(buy) = 100 ÷ 1000 = 0.10 (10%).
  • P(buy | coupon) = 60 ÷ 200 = 0.30 (30%).
  • P(buy | no coupon) = 40 ÷ 800 = 0.05 (5%).
  • Expected value of a lucky draw: 10% chance to win ₹500, else ₹0. EV = 0.1 × 500 + 0.9 × 0 = ₹50.
Remember: AND → multiply, OR → add, NOT → 1 minus. Conditional probability is just "look only at the rows where B is true".

17. Sampling

In plain words

The population is the whole group you care about. A sample is a smaller part you actually study. We use samples because checking everyone is too slow or costly.

To check if dal is salty, you taste one spoon, not the whole pot. But you must stir first. Stirring is what makes the sample fair.

Sampling methods

MethodHow it worksExample
Simple randomEveryone has an equal chancePick 100 roll numbers by lottery
StratifiedSplit into groups, then sample randomly from eachPick 50 students from each year
SystematicPick every k-th itemEvery 10th customer in the bill list
ClusterPick whole groups at randomPick 5 colleges and survey everyone there
ConvenienceWhoever is easy to reach (not random)Asking only your friends

Key points

  • Sampling bias = the sample does not represent the population. Example: an online survey misses people without internet.
  • Bigger samples give more reliable answers, but only if they are not biased.
  • Margin of error = how far the sample answer may be from the true answer. "40% ± 3%" means the truth is probably 37–43%.
  • Central Limit Theorem (simple version): if you take many large samples and calculate each sample's mean, those means form a normal curve. This is why many tests work even when the data is not normal.
  • Survivorship bias = studying only the winners. Example: only studying apps that succeeded, ignoring the ones that failed.
Remember: a small, random sample beats a huge, biased one. Stir the pot before tasting.

18. Hypothesis Testing

In plain words

Hypothesis testing checks whether a difference you see is real, or just luck. A hypothesis is a claim you want to test.

It works like a court. The accused is "innocent until proven guilty". Here, we assume "nothing changed" until the evidence is strong enough to say otherwise.

Key terms

  • Null hypothesis (H0): "no effect, no difference". Example: the new button does not change sales.
  • Alternative hypothesis (H1): "there is an effect". Example: the new button changes sales.
  • Significance level (α, alpha): how much risk of a false alarm you accept. Usually 0.05 (5%).
  • p-value: if H0 were true, how likely is a result at least this extreme? Small p = the result is surprising under H0.
  • If p < 0.05 → reject H0. The result is "statistically significant".
  • If p ≥ 0.05 → fail to reject H0. Not enough evidence. (We never say "H0 is proven true".)

Steps

  1. Write H0 and H1.
  2. Choose α (usually 0.05).
  3. Pick the right test (see topic 19).
  4. Calculate the test statistic and p-value (Excel or Python does this).
  5. Compare p with α and decide.
  6. Explain the result in plain business words.

Example

A coaching centre says its new method raises average marks. Old average = 60. A sample of new students averages 64. Test gives p = 0.03.

  • H0: new method average = 60. H1: new method average ≠ 60.
  • p = 0.03 is less than 0.05 → reject H0. The improvement is unlikely to be just luck.
Remember: "If p is low, the null must go." p-value is NOT the chance that H0 is true. It is how surprising your data is if H0 were true.

19. Common Tests and Errors

In plain words

Different questions need different tests. A t-test compares averages. A chi-square test compares counts in categories.

Choosing a test is like choosing a tool. You use a ruler for length and a weighing scale for weight. Averages and category counts need different tools too.

20. A/B Testing

In plain words

An A/B test is an experiment. You show version A to one random group and version B to another, then compare results. It is hypothesis testing used in real products.

A chai seller makes two recipes and gives each to random customers for a week. Whichever recipe gets more repeat buyers wins. Random groups make it fair.

Steps

  1. Pick one goal metric. Example: conversion rate = buyers ÷ visitors.
  2. Write H0 ("no difference") and H1.
  3. Change only one thing. Example: button colour, or price shown.
  4. Split users randomly: A = control (old), B = variant (new).
  5. Decide sample size and run time in advance. Run at least one full week to cover weekdays and weekends.
  6. Check significance, then decide.

Worked example

VersionVisitorsOrdersConversion
A (old "Buy" button)5,0002004.0%
B (new "Buy Now" button)5,0002505.0%
  • Absolute lift = 5.0% − 4.0% = 1 percentage point.
  • Relative lift = 1 ÷ 4 = 25% better.
  • A two-proportion z-test gives p ≈ 0.016, which is less than 0.05. So B is a real winner, not luck.

Common mistakes

  • Peeking: stopping the test as soon as it looks good. This creates false winners.
  • Changing many things at once, so you don't know what worked.
  • Too small a sample, or too short a run.
  • Ignoring guardrail metrics: metrics that must not get worse, like refunds or app crashes.
Remember: random split, one change, fixed sample size, then check p-value. Statistical significance is not the same as business importance; also check if the lift is big enough to matter.

21. Regression Basics

In plain words

Regression finds a line (formula) that best links an input to an output, so you can predict. The input is the independent variable (x). The output is the dependent variable (y).

An auto-rickshaw fare is a base charge plus a fixed amount per km. Regression finds that "base + rate × distance" formula from past rides.

Simple linear regression

Formula: y = a + b × x

  • a = intercept: the value of y when x is 0 (the base charge).
  • b = slope: how much y changes when x goes up by 1 (the per-km rate).

Worked example (study hours data from topic 15)

Hours (x)12345
Marks (y)4050556570
  • Mean of x = 3. Mean of y = 280 ÷ 5 = 56.
  • Slope b = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ Σ(x − x̄)² = 75 ÷ 10 = 7.5.
  • Intercept a = ȳ − b × x̄ = 56 − 7.5 × 3 = 33.5.
  • Line: marks = 33.5 + 7.5 × hours. Each extra hour adds about 7.5 marks.
  • Prediction for 6 hours: 33.5 + 7.5 × 6 = 33.5 + 45 = 78.5.

Key points

  • Residual = actual − predicted. For 3 hours: predicted 56, actual 55, residual = −1.
  • The best line makes the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. This is called least squares.
  • R² (R-squared) goes from 0 to 1. It is the share of the change in y that the model explains. Here R² ≈ 0.99, which is a great fit.
  • Multiple regression uses many inputs: sales = a + b1 × ad spend + b2 × discount.
  • Logistic regression predicts a yes/no outcome, like "will this customer leave?"
  • Don't predict far outside your data. 20 hours of study won't give 183.5 marks!
  • In Excel: =SLOPE(), =INTERCEPT(), =RSQ(), or add a trendline to a scatter chart.
Remember: y = a + b·x. Slope = change in y per 1 unit of x. R² = how well the line fits. Regression shows links, not proof of cause.

22. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

In plain words

EDA means exploring a new dataset to understand it before doing any serious analysis. You look at its size, columns, gaps, ranges and patterns.

EDA is like walking around a new city on day one. You learn the main roads and landmarks before planning any trip.

A simple EDA checklist

  1. Shape: how many rows and columns?
  2. Columns and types: which are numbers, text, dates?
  3. Missing values: how many blanks in each column?
  4. Duplicates: any repeated rows?
  5. Summary stats: min, max, mean, median, SD for number columns.
  6. Counts for category columns: how many orders per city?
  7. Univariate (one column at a time): histograms, box plots.
  8. Bivariate (two columns together): scatter plots, correlation, grouped averages.
  9. Time trends: line chart by day, week, month.
  10. Write down questions that come up. They guide the next step.

23. KPIs and Metrics

In plain words

A metric is any number you track. A KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is one of the few most important metrics that show if the business is meeting its goals.

In a car, many dials exist, but the speedometer and fuel gauge are the ones you watch while driving. Those are the KPIs.

Common business metrics

RevenuePrice × units sold₹500 × 2,000 = ₹10,00,000

24. Cohort Analysis

In plain words

A cohort is a group of users who share a starting point, like "everyone who signed up in January". Cohort analysis tracks each group over time to see how they behave.

A school tracks each batch separately: the 2022 batch, the 2023 batch. Comparing batches shows if teaching got better, which a single school-wide average would hide.

Worked example: monthly retention

Signup monthUsersMonth 0Month 1Month 2Month 3
Jan200100%60% (120)45% (90)40% (80)
Feb250100%56% (140)40% (100)—
Mar300100%65% (195)——

How to read it

  • Each row is one cohort. Each column is "months since signup".
  • Read across a row: how one group drops off. Jan users fall from 100% to 40% in 3 months.
  • Read down a column: compare groups at the same age. Month 1: Jan 60%, Feb 56%, Mar 65%.
  • March's Month-1 retention is the best. Ask: what changed in March? Maybe a new onboarding flow.
  • Blank cells (—) are months that have not happened yet. This makes a triangle shape.
  • Cohorts can also be by first purchase month, acquisition channel or city.
Remember: cohort analysis compares groups fairly by "age". Overall averages mix new and old users and can hide problems.

25. Funnel Analysis

In plain words

A funnel is the series of steps a user takes toward a goal, like buying. Funnel analysis counts how many users reach each step and where they drop off.

Think of a kitchen funnel: wide at the top, narrow at the bottom. Many people visit a shop window, fewer walk in, fewer try clothes, and only some pay at the counter.

Worked numbers

StepUsersStep conversionDrop-offFrom top
Visit10,000——100%
View product6,00060%4,00060%
Add to cart1,80030%4,20018%
Checkout90050%9009%
Purchase60066.7%3006%

Key points

  • Step conversion = users at this step ÷ users at the previous step.
  • Overall conversion = users at the last step ÷ users at the first step = 6%.
  • Here, "view → add to cart" is the weakest step (30%). Ideas: better photos, clearer prices, reviews.
  • Split the funnel by device, city or traffic source. Often one segment (like Android) causes the drop.
  • Funnels show where users leave. Surveys, session recordings or A/B tests show why.
Remember: fix the biggest leak first. Improving a 30% step usually gives more than polishing a 67% step.

27. SQL Basics for Analysts

In plain words

SQL (Structured Query Language) is how you ask a database for data. A query is one question written in SQL. Analysts mostly read data with SELECT.

SQL is like giving a librarian a clear note: "Get me all books by this author, after 2015, sorted by title." You say what you want; the database finds it.

Table: orders

order_idcustomercityamountorder_date
1AshaPune5002025-01-01
2RaviDelhi3002025-01-02
3NehaPune7002025-01-03
4ArjunMumbai4002025-01-04
5KiranDelhi6002025-01-05

Key clauses

  • SELECT = which columns. FROM = which table.
  • WHERE = filter rows, before grouping.
  • GROUP BY = make groups, then use aggregate functions (functions that summarise many rows): COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX.
  • HAVING = filter groups, after grouping.
  • ORDER BY = sort (DESC for biggest first). LIMIT = keep only the first few rows.
  • DISTINCT = remove repeated values. CASE WHEN = an IF statement inside SQL.
-- Orders above ₹400, biggest first
SELECT customer, amount
FROM orders
WHERE amount > 400
ORDER BY amount DESC;
-- Result: Neha 700, Kiran 600, Asha 500

-- Total sales per city, only cities above ₹500
SELECT city, SUM(amount) AS total_sales, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
GROUP BY city
HAVING SUM(amount) > 500
ORDER BY total_sales DESC;
-- Result: Pune 1200 (2 orders), Delhi 900 (2 orders). Mumbai (400) is filtered out.

-- Label each order with CASE WHEN
SELECT customer,
       CASE WHEN amount >= 500 THEN 'High' ELSE 'Low' END AS order_size
FROM orders;

Order in which SQL runs

FROM → WHERE → GROUP BY → HAVING → SELECT → ORDER BY → LIMIT
  • This is why you cannot use a SELECT alias (like total_sales) inside WHERE in most databases: WHERE runs before SELECT.
  • COUNT(*) counts all rows. COUNT(column) skips NULLs.
  • NULL means "unknown". Check it with IS NULL, never = NULL.
Remember: WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after grouping. This is the most-asked SQL question for analysts.

28. SQL Joins and Window Functions

In plain words

A JOIN combines rows from two tables using a shared column, like a customer ID. A window function does a calculation across related rows but still keeps every row.

A join is like matching students' names on the attendance sheet with their marks sheet. A window function is like writing each student's class rank next to their marks, without merging the class into one row.

  • Self join = a table joined to itself. Example: employees and their managers in one table.

Window functions

Uses the orders table from topic 27. Format: FUNCTION() OVER (PARTITION BY ... ORDER BY ...). PARTITION BY = split into groups. ORDER BY = order inside each group.

-- Rank orders inside each city
SELECT customer, city, amount,
       RANK() OVER (PARTITION BY city ORDER BY amount DESC) AS city_rank
FROM orders;

-- Running total of sales by date
SELECT order_date, amount,
       SUM(amount) OVER (ORDER BY order_date) AS running_total
FROM orders;

-- Previous order's amount (for day-over-day change)
SELECT order_date, amount,
       LAG(amount) OVER (ORDER BY order_date) AS prev_amount
FROM orders;

29. Python and pandas for Analysts

In plain words

Python is a programming language. pandas is a Python library (ready-made code) for working with tables. A pandas table is called a DataFrame. matplotlib is a library for charts.

pandas is like Excel with a remote control. Instead of clicking, you write steps once, and you can re-run them on new data any day.

Core commands

import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

df = pd.read_csv("orders.csv")      # load a CSV file into a DataFrame

df.head()                # first 5 rows
df.shape                 # (rows, columns), e.g. (5, 5)

df.describe()            # count, mean, std, min, quartiles, max
df.isnull().sum()        # missing values per column

# Select and filter
df["amount"]                          # one column
df[df["amount"] > 400]                # rows where amount is above 400
df[(df["city"] == "Pune") & (df["amount"] > 400)]   # two conditions

# Clean
df = df.drop_duplicates()                               # remove repeated rows
df["amount"] = df["amount"].fillna(df["amount"].median())  # fill blanks with median
df["city"] = df["city"].str.strip().str.title()         # " pune" -> "Pune"
df["order_date"] = pd.to_datetime(df["order_date"])     # text -> real date

# New column
df["high_value"] = df["amount"] >= 500

city_sales = df.groupby("city")["amount"].sum().sort_values(ascending=False)
# city
# Pune      1200
# Delhi      900
# Mumbai     400

Key points

  • how="left" in merge = LEFT JOIN. Options: "inner", "left", "right", "outer".
  • df.pivot_table(index="city", values="amount", aggfunc="sum") = an Excel pivot table.

  • Use & (and), | (or) with brackets around each condition. Plain and gives an error.
  • NumPy = fast maths on arrays; pandas is built on it. seaborn = nicer statistical charts.

31. Charts and Storytelling with Data

In plain words

Data storytelling means explaining your findings as a short, clear story: what happened, why it matters, and what to do. The right chart makes the point obvious in seconds.

A good analyst is like a news reporter. The headline comes first ("Sales fell 15% in March"). Details and proof come after. Nobody reads a news story that starts with a table of numbers.

Choosing a chart

GoalBest chartExample
Compare categoriesBar chartSales by city
Show change over timeLine chartDaily orders this month
Show parts of a wholeStacked bar, or pie with 2–4 slicesShare of UPI vs card vs cash
Show a distributionHistogram, box plotSpread of order values
Show a relationshipScatter plotAd spend vs sales
Show one key numberKPI card (big number)This month's revenue: ₹12.4 L

A simple story structure

  1. Context: what question were we answering?
  2. Finding: the one main insight, in one sentence.
  3. Evidence: one or two charts that prove it.
  4. Recommendation: what the business should do next.
  5. Impact: what we expect to gain (money, users, time).

Good chart habits

  • Give charts a title that states the finding: "Android orders fell 23% after the app update", not "Orders by platform".
  • Bar charts must start at zero. Otherwise small differences look huge.
  • Remove clutter: 3D effects, heavy grid lines, too many colours.
  • Use one highlight colour for the thing that matters, and grey for the rest.
  • Avoid pie charts with many slices. People can't compare angles well.
  • Know your audience. A CEO wants the answer and the action. A fellow analyst wants the method.
Remember: answer first, then proof. One chart, one message. Always end with a clear recommendation.

32. Case Study: Why Did Orders Drop?

In plain words

Here is a full, small example that uses the six steps. Interviewers often give a case like this and ask, "How would you approach it?"

It is like detective work. You start with a crime (the drop), collect clues (data), rule out suspects (segments) and name the culprit with proof.

1. Ask

A food delivery app in Pune saw orders fall from 40,000 in February to 34,000 in March. That is a drop of 6,000, or 6,000 ÷ 40,000 = 15%. Question: "Why did March orders fall 15%, and how do we recover?"

2. Prepare

  • Pull orders, app sessions and funnel events for Feb and March using SQL.
  • Collect context: app release dates, offers, competitor news, weather, holidays.

3. Process

  • Remove 300 duplicate test orders. Standardise area names. Convert timestamps to dates.

4. Analyse

Break the drop into segments (a drill-down):

PlatformFeb ordersMarch ordersChange
iOS14,00014,0000%
Android26,00020,000−23%
Total40,00034,000−15%
  • The whole drop (6,000) is on Android. iOS is flat. So it is not weather, prices or a competitor, which would affect both.
  • Funnel check on Android: "cart → payment success" fell from 80% to 62% starting 5 March.
  • An Android app update was released on 5 March. Error logs show a UPI payment bug in that version.

5. Share

Headline: "The March drop is caused by a UPI payment bug in the 5 March Android update. Fixing it should recover about 6,000 orders a month." Support it with one line chart of Android vs iOS daily orders and one funnel chart.

6. Act

  • Engineering fixes the bug and releases a patch.
  • Send affected users a small coupon to win them back.
  • Set up an alert: if payment success falls below 75% on any platform, notify the team.
  • Check the recovery in April with the same dashboard.
Remember: for any "metric dropped" question: confirm the number is right, check for seasonality, split by segment (platform, city, channel, new vs old users), check the funnel, then look for what changed on that date.
Revision

Quick Revision

The most-asked questions, with short and simple answers. Say these in your own words.

Basics & Process

  • What is data analysis? Studying data to answer a question and support a decision.
  • What does a data analyst do? Collects, cleans and analyses data, then explains findings with charts and dashboards.
  • Four types of analytics? Descriptive (what), diagnostic (why), predictive (what next), prescriptive (what to do).
  • Steps of analysis? Ask, Prepare, Process, Analyse, Share, Act.
  • Structured vs unstructured? Rows and columns vs free form like text, images and audio.
  • Levels of measurement? Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio (NOIR).
  • Primary vs secondary data? Collected by you vs collected by someone else.

Cleaning

  • How do you handle missing values? Find out why, then delete, or fill with mean, median, mode or "Unknown".
  • Mean or median to fill blanks? Median if there are outliers or skew; mean if data is symmetric.
  • How do you find outliers? IQR rule, z-score above 3, or a box plot.
  • Should you delete outliers? Only if they are errors. Real extremes can be important.
  • Cleaning vs wrangling? Cleaning fixes errors. Wrangling reshapes data for analysis.

Statistics

  • Mean vs median? Mean is the average and is pulled by outliers. Median is the middle and is robust.
  • What is standard deviation? The typical distance of values from the mean.
  • Why n − 1 for sample variance? A sample slightly underestimates spread; n − 1 corrects it.
  • What is IQR? Q3 − Q1, the spread of the middle 50%.
  • Right skew? Long tail to the right; mean > median. Example: income.
  • 68–95–99.7 rule? In a normal curve, 68% within 1 SD, 95% within 2, 99.7% within 3.
  • Correlation vs causation? Moving together vs one causing the other. Prove cause with experiments.
  • What is a p-value? How surprising the data is if the null hypothesis were true. Below 0.05 → significant.
  • Type I vs Type II error? False alarm vs missed real effect.
  • t-test vs chi-square? t-test compares means; chi-square checks if categories are related.
  • What is an A/B test? Random split into control and variant, change one thing, compare a metric.
  • What is R²? How much of the change in y the model explains, from 0 to 1.

Business Metrics

  • Conversion rate? Buyers ÷ visitors.
  • Churn rate? Customers lost ÷ customers at the start of the period.
  • CAC and LTV? Cost to win one customer vs total value a customer brings. LTV:CAC above 3 is healthy.
  • What is cohort analysis? Tracking groups that started at the same time to compare behaviour fairly.
  • What is funnel analysis? Counting users at each step to find where they drop off.
  • KPI vs metric? Every KPI is a metric, but only the few most important metrics are KPIs.

Tools

  • VLOOKUP vs XLOOKUP? XLOOKUP can look left, defaults to exact match and has a "not found" value.
  • Why INDEX-MATCH? Works in any direction and does not break when columns are inserted.
  • SUMIF vs SUMIFS? SUMIF takes one condition; SUMIFS takes many.
  • WHERE vs HAVING? WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after.
  • INNER vs LEFT JOIN? INNER keeps only matches; LEFT keeps all left rows, with NULLs where no match.
  • RANK vs DENSE_RANK vs ROW_NUMBER? 1,2,2,4 vs 1,2,2,3 vs 1,2,3,4.
  • pandas groupby? Splits rows into groups and summarises each, like SQL GROUP BY.
  • Which chart for a trend? A line chart.
Remember: give the simple answer first, then one small example with numbers. That beats a long textbook definition.

Learning Path

Go step by step. Practise each step on a real dataset (Kaggle or data.gov.in) before moving on.

  1. Excel — formulas, XLOOKUP, SUMIFS, pivot tables, basic charts.
  2. Basic statistics — mean, median, SD, percentiles, distributions, correlation.
  3. SQL — SELECT, WHERE, GROUP BY, HAVING, JOINs. Then window functions and CTEs. Practise daily on LeetCode or HackerRank.
  4. One BI tool — Power BI or Tableau. Build one dashboard with KPIs and filters.
  5. Business metrics — conversion, churn, retention, CAC, LTV, funnels, cohorts.
  6. Python — pandas for cleaning and grouping, matplotlib or seaborn for charts.
  7. Hypothesis testing and A/B tests — p-values, t-test, chi-square, sample size basics.
  8. Projects — 2 or 3 end-to-end projects (e.g. sales dashboard, customer churn analysis, A/B test report) on GitHub with a short write-up.
  9. Communication — practise explaining one project in 2 minutes: problem, approach, finding, impact.
  10. Case practice — "Why did metric X drop?" and guesstimates. Talk through your steps out loud.
Remember: for every analysis, ask three questions. Is the data right? What does it mean for the business? What should we do now?
