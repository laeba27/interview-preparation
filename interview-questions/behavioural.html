Part 1 — How Behavioural Interviews Work

1. What Interviewers Are Really Assessing

Sample answers use an example profile. Replace the details with your own stories.

BasicWhy did you choose software engineering?

What they're testing: whether your motivation is real and lasting, or just "it pays well".

Honestly, it started with a small thing. In school I wrote a messy little script to calculate my family's monthly expenses from a spreadsheet, and the moment it ran and saved my dad an hour, I was hooked. What I liked wasn't the code itself, it was that I could build something once and it kept being useful.

That's still what keeps me here. At Brightcart I've been a full-stack developer for about three years, and the work I enjoy most is when a technical change shows up as something a real person notices. When we got the checkout page from a 4.2 second p95 load time down to 1.6 seconds, conversions went up about 6%. That's thousands of shoppers who just had a smoother day, and a business that made more money, because of work a few of us did.

The second reason is that the field never lets me stand still. In the last year alone I've had to learn Kubernetes basics for a migration and get much deeper into Postgres performance. I like that the job rewards curiosity.

So the short version: I like building things that keep being useful, and I like a job where I'm always learning. Software engineering gives me both every week.

Fresher version: I got into it through a college project. Four of us built an attendance app for our department, and seeing faculty actually use it instead of paper registers made it click for me that code can remove real friction. My internship confirmed it: I built an admin dashboard in React for a startup, and the ops team started using it daily within the two months I was there. I want a career where that feeling is the normal part of the job.

Make it yours: swap in your own first "it worked and someone used it" moment, then one recent example that proves the interest is still alive. Likely follow-up: "What part of engineering do you enjoy least?"

BasicDescribe yourself in three words.

What they're testing: self-awareness, and whether you can back adjectives with evidence instead of listing buzzwords.

I'd say reliable, curious and calm. Let me give you a quick reason for each, because words are cheap.

Reliable, because when something is mine, I see it through. When a payment-webhook bug double-charged 38 of our customers, I owned the fix, worked with support on every refund, and wrote the postmortem. We added idempotency keys so that class of bug can't happen again.

Curious, because I like understanding why something is slow or broken, not just patching it. On our checkout speed project I didn't stop at "the page is slow". I traced it down to render-blocking third-party scripts and oversized images, which is how we got p95 from 4.2 seconds to 1.6.

Calm, because I think clearly when things go wrong. During that webhook incident there was a lot of pressure, and my job was to slow down enough to stop the bleeding first, then find the root cause.

If I had to pick the one my team would say first, it's probably reliable.

Make it yours: pick three words that are true and different from each other (not "hardworking, dedicated, committed"), and attach a 1–2 sentence proof to each. Likely follow-up: "Which of those three would your teammates disagree with?"

IntermediateWhat would your manager say about you?

What they're testing: whether your self-image matches how others see you, and whether you can quote real feedback.

I can actually answer this with what my manager wrote in my last review, which is more useful than me guessing.

The first thing she highlighted was ownership. When a teammate left in the middle of our internal reporting tool project, it was already behind. I took it over, sat down with the PM to re-scope it to the features people actually needed, and we shipped the core in three weeks. Her words were roughly "Picks up messy things and makes them boring again", which I took as a compliment.

The second was that I raise risks early. Before a big sale weekend, our PM wanted to skip tests to squeeze in a feature. I pushed back, but with an alternative: a smaller scope that we could test properly. My manager liked that I came with a plan, not just a "no".

She'd also tell you what I'm working on. My estimates used to be optimistic. I once said a search feature would take one sprint and it took three. Since then I break work into smaller pieces and give ranges instead of single dates, and that's been noticeably better this year, but she'd say I'm still improving there.

So: owns things end to end, flags risk early, and is getting better at estimating.

Make it yours: quote actual review lines or Slack praise if you have them, and include one honest growth area so it sounds real. Likely follow-up: "If I called your manager right now, what would she say you should improve?"

IntermediateWhat motivates you at work?

What they're testing: whether what drives you fits the role, team and company culture.

Three things, in roughly this order.

First, visible impact. I do my best work when I can connect my code to a number or a person. The checkout speed project is the clearest example. We had a clear metric, p95 load time, and I could watch it drop from 4.2 seconds to 1.6, and then watch conversions go up about 6%. That kind of feedback loop keeps me going through the boring parts.

Second, solving problems that are a bit beyond me. When our deployment migration was blocked because nobody on the team knew Kubernetes, I spent two weeks learning the basics and got us unblocked. Being slightly out of my depth is uncomfortable, but it's where I grow fastest.

Third, helping people around me get better. I mentored two interns last year, and seeing one of them go from dreading code reviews to giving thoughtful reviews himself was as satisfying as anything I shipped.

What doesn't motivate me much is work that has no clear user or purpose. I'll still do it well, but I'll ask a lot of "why" questions first. That's partly why this role appeals to me: your team seems close to the customer.

Make it yours: choose motivators that match the job description, and tie each to a concrete moment. Avoid "money" or "learning" alone. Likely follow-up: "Tell me about a time you felt demotivated. What did you do?"

IntermediateWhat kind of environment do you do your best work in?

What they're testing: culture fit, and whether you'll be unhappy or struggle in their actual setup.

I do my best work in teams that are clear about goals but flexible about how we get there. At Brightcart, the projects where I shone were the ones with one clear outcome, like "make checkout faster" or "ship the reporting tool's core in three weeks", and then enough trust that the engineers could figure out the approach.

I also do well where disagreement is normal and safe. When a senior engineer and I disagreed about rewriting versus refactoring our orders service, nobody treated it as a fight. We ran a small spike, looked at the results, and agreed on an incremental refactor. I like teams that settle things with evidence, not seniority.

On the practical side, I like a mix of focused time and collaboration. I'm happy to block off mornings for deep work and do reviews, pairing and meetings in the afternoon. And I'm comfortable with hybrid or remote. I worked closely with a platform team in another time zone during a Kubernetes migration, and it went fine because we wrote things down.

That said, I've worked in fairly chaotic stretches too, like right before a sale weekend, and I can adapt. I just try to bring a bit of structure with me.

Make it yours: research the company's working style first and lead with the overlap, but don't claim to love something you'd hate. Likely follow-up: "What environment would you struggle in?"

AdvancedWhat would your manager say is the one thing holding you back?

What they're testing: honesty under pressure, and whether you take real action on weaknesses rather than naming a fake one.

She'd say estimation, and she'd be right. I tend to estimate the happy path. The clearest example: I told the team a search feature would take one sprint, and it took three. The indexing work was bigger than I thought, and I hadn't accounted for edge cases in how products with variants should show up in results.

What made it worse was that I didn't raise the risk early enough. I kept thinking I could catch up, so the PM found out about the delay later than she should have.

Since then I've changed three things. I break anything bigger than a few days into smaller tickets before I estimate. I give a range, like "four to seven days", and say what drives the high end. And if I'm halfway through the time and less than halfway through the work, I flag it that day, not at the end of the sprint.

It's improved. On my last few projects I've been within the range I gave. But I wouldn't call it solved, and my manager wouldn't either. It's something I keep an eye on every sprint.

Fresher version: My internship lead said I didn't ask for help early enough. On the admin dashboard, I spent almost two days stuck on a table-filtering bug before asking, and he fixed my misunderstanding of the API in ten minutes. Now I use a simple rule: if I've been stuck for an hour with no new idea, I write up what I tried and ask.

Make it yours: name a real, non-fatal weakness for the role, show the moment it hurt, and list concrete changes. Likely follow-up: "How do you know it's actually improved?"

2. The STAR Method

Sample answers use an example profile. Replace the details with your own stories.

BasicWhat's a project you're proud of?

What they're testing: whether you can tell a clear, structured story with your own actions and a measurable result.

Situation: At Brightcart, our checkout page had become slow. The p95 load time was 4.2 seconds, and analytics showed people dropping off right there.

Task: I was asked to lead the performance work and get it under two seconds before the holiday season.

Action: I started by measuring instead of guessing. I set up real-user monitoring and ran Lighthouse traces, and I found three big problems. First, four third-party scripts were render-blocking, including a reviews widget that loaded on checkout for no reason. I worked with marketing to remove two and defer the other two. Second, product images were full-size uploads. I added responsive image sizes and lazy loading below the fold. Third, our React bundle shipped the whole account area to checkout. I split the bundle by route, which cut the checkout JavaScript by about 40%. On the backend, I found an N+1 query in the cart summary endpoint in our Spring service and replaced it with a single join. I shipped each change behind a flag so we could measure them one by one.

Result: p95 dropped from 4.2 seconds to 1.6, and conversions on checkout went up about 6% over the next month. I also added a performance budget to CI so it can't quietly creep back up.

What I learned is to measure first. Two of the things I was sure were the problem turned out to barely matter.

Fresher version: At our 24-hour hackathon, my team of three built a tool that turned lecture recordings into searchable notes. I built the React front end and the search. Around hour 16 our transcription API hit rate limits, so I added caching and batched requests to keep the demo working. We finished 2nd out of about 40 teams. My lesson: get a thin version working end to end early, then polish.

Make it yours: choose a project where you drove the key decisions and have a number, even a rough one. Keep Situation and Task to two sentences. Likely follow-up: "What was the hardest trade-off you made on it?"

BasicTell me about the hardest technical problem you've solved.

What they're testing: your debugging approach and technical depth, told as a structured story rather than a ramble.

The hardest one was a payment-webhook bug that double-charged 38 customers.

We got a spike of support tickets one morning saying people were charged twice. I was on call, so it was mine to sort out.

First I stopped the damage. I found that every duplicate charge matched a webhook that our payment provider had retried, so I paused our retry-triggered order processing and switched to a manual queue for a few hours. Then I went after the root cause. The provider retries a webhook if it doesn't get a response within a few seconds. Our handler did the charge confirmation and then a slow inventory call before responding, so under load it timed out, the provider retried, and a second worker processed the same event again. Nothing in our code checked whether we'd already handled that event.

I fixed it in two layers. I added idempotency keys, storing each provider event ID in Postgres with a unique constraint, so a repeat is a no-op. And I moved the slow inventory work to an async job so we respond quickly. I wrote a test that fires the same webhook twice in parallel to prove it.

All 38 customers were refunded within a day, I wrote the postmortem, and we haven't had a duplicate charge since.

My lesson: any handler that can be retried must be idempotent. I now check for that in every code review touching external callbacks.

Make it yours: pick a problem where the cause was not obvious and you can explain it simply. Name the actual mechanism. Likely follow-up: "Why wasn't this caught in testing?"

IntermediateTell me about a time you improved a process.

What they're testing: whether you notice recurring pain and fix the system, not just the one-off symptom.

Our team's estimates were unreliable, and I was part of the problem. I'd committed to delivering a search feature in one sprint, and it took three. That slipped a marketing launch, and I wasn't the only one on the team who'd done something like that that quarter.

After the retro, I offered to propose a better way to estimate. I looked at our last ten tickets that overran and found a pattern: almost all of them had an unknown in them, a new library, an unclear requirement or an unfamiliar part of the codebase, and we'd estimated as if we knew the answer.

So I suggested three changes. Any ticket with a real unknown gets a short, time-boxed spike first, usually one or two days, and we estimate after. We estimate in ranges, not single numbers, and the PM gets the range with the reason for the high end. And we added a mid-sprint check: if a ticket has used half its time and isn't half done, the owner flags it in stand-up. I wrote it up as a one-page doc, ran it past my manager, and we tried it for two sprints before making it the default.

Over the next quarter, the share of tickets that went over their upper estimate dropped from roughly half to about one in five. The PM told me she finally trusted our dates.

My lesson: most process fixes are small. You just need data to show where the pain actually is.

Make it yours: good candidates are CI, code review, on-call, onboarding docs or release steps. Show before and after. Likely follow-up: "Did anyone resist the change? How did you handle it?"

IntermediateTell me about a time you made a decision without all the information.

What they're testing: judgement, bias for action, and whether you make decisions reversible when you're unsure.

During the checkout performance project, I had to decide what to fix first, and the data didn't give a clean answer.

We had about six weeks before the holiday season. My traces showed several problems: blocking third-party scripts, huge images, a large JavaScript bundle and a slow backend query. But I couldn't tell from lab tests how much each one would move real-user load times, because real traffic varies a lot by device and network.

Waiting for perfect data wasn't an option. So I made the decision cheap to reverse. I ranked the fixes by likely impact versus effort, based on the traces and what I knew about our users, who are mostly on mid-range phones. Then I shipped each one behind a feature flag and turned it on for 10% of traffic first, so real-user monitoring could tell me whether it helped. I also told my manager and PM my ranking and my assumptions up front, so nobody was surprised if I changed course.

It turned out I was right about the scripts and images, which gave the biggest drop. I was wrong about the backend query. It mattered much less than I expected, so I moved it to the end. Overall we got p95 from 4.2 seconds to 1.6 in time for the season.

What I took from it: when information is incomplete, make the decision small and measurable so you learn fast.

Make it yours: the key beats are what you didn't know, how you reduced the risk, and what you learned once data arrived. Likely follow-up: "What would you have done if the first change made things worse?"

AdvancedYou keep saying "we". What did you personally do?

What they're testing: your individual contribution, and whether you can separate it from the team's without either hiding or taking all the credit.

That's fair, let me be specific. On the checkout project there were three engineers plus support from design and marketing. Here's what was mine.

I did the investigation. I set up the real-user monitoring and ran the traces that found the four blocking third-party scripts and the oversized images. I wrote the one-page plan with the ranked list of fixes, and I presented it to my manager and the PM.

I personally built the route-based bundle splitting in React, which cut checkout JavaScript by about 40%, and the responsive image setup. I also found and fixed the N+1 query in the cart summary endpoint. I set up the feature flags and ran the rollout at 10%, then 50%, then 100%.

I also handled the conversations with marketing about removing two of their scripts, which was probably the hardest part, because those tools mattered to them. I showed them the cost of each script in milliseconds and we agreed to defer, not delete, the ones they needed.

What my teammates did: one handled the CDN and caching headers, and the other built the CI performance budget check after I proposed it.

So the result, 4.2 seconds to 1.6, was a team result, but the diagnosis, the plan and about half the code were mine.

Make it yours: before any interview, write down for each story which parts were yours and which were others'. Say "I" for your actions and name what others did. Likely follow-up: "If you'd been on the project alone, what would have gone differently?"

AdvancedIf you did that project again, what would you do differently?

What they're testing: reflection; a candidate who says "nothing" hasn't learned from their own work.

Two things on the checkout project, one technical and one about people.

Technically, I'd set up real-user monitoring months earlier, before we needed it. Because we only started measuring when the project began, I didn't have a long baseline. That made it harder to prove which change caused the conversion lift, because a seasonal trend was mixed in. We still saw about 6% more conversions, but I'd have liked a cleaner comparison with a proper holdout group from day one.

On the people side, I'd involve marketing at the start, not in week two. I found their third-party scripts were a big part of the problem, and I came to them with the data already done and a request to remove things. They felt ambushed, and the first meeting was tense. We sorted it out, but if I'd brought them in while I was still investigating, they'd have been part of the solution instead of feeling like the problem.

One more small one: I'd write the performance budget into CI on day one. We only added it at the end, and in between, one change quietly added back 30 KB.

The general lesson for me is to set up measurement and involve affected teams before I start fixing things.

Fresher version: On our college attendance app, I'd agree clear ownership of each part in week one. We split work loosely, and when one member stopped contributing, nobody noticed for two weeks because nobody knew exactly what he owned. Now I'd put owners and dates on a shared board from the start.

Make it yours: give one technical and one process or people improvement, and keep it concrete. Likely follow-up: "Did you apply that lesson on your next project?"

3. Building Your Story Bank

Sample answers use an example profile. Replace the details with your own stories.

BasicTell me about a recent accomplishment.

What they're testing: whether you have recent, specific work you can talk about with pride and numbers.

The most recent one I'm happy with came out of a customer escalation. One of our larger B2B clients kept having their bulk product imports fail. They'd upload a spreadsheet of a few thousand products and get a generic "import failed" error, and support had been going back and forth with them for two weeks.

The support lead asked if an engineer could join a call, and I volunteered. On the call I watched their team do an import, and I realised the real problem wasn't one bug. Their files had a mix of issues: missing SKUs, prices with currency symbols, and some rows in a different encoding. Our importer stopped at the first bad row and told them nothing useful.

So I built a validation report. Before importing, the system now checks every row and returns a downloadable list of problems with the row number and a plain-English fix, like "Row 214: price contains a currency symbol, remove it". Good rows can import while bad rows wait. I built it in about a week in our Node service and wrote a short help-centre article with support.

The client's next import went through on the first try, and bulk-import support tickets across all customers dropped by about 70% the following month.

What I liked about it was that the fix came from watching a real user, not from reading tickets.

Make it yours: pick something from the last 6–12 months with a clear before and after. Old wins make interviewers wonder what you've done lately. Likely follow-up: "Why hadn't anyone fixed that already?"

BasicTell me about something you learned recently.

What they're testing: how you learn, how fast, and whether you apply it to real work.

The biggest one this year was Kubernetes. Our team was moving our services from VMs to a Kubernetes cluster run by the platform team, and our migration was stuck because nobody on my team knew it well enough to write the deployment configs or debug problems.

I gave myself two weeks to get to "useful", not "expert". I did it in three layers. First, the concepts: I went through the official tutorials and drew my own diagram of pods, deployments, services and ingress, and how a request flows through them. Second, hands-on: I ran a local cluster and deployed one of our smaller Node services to it, deliberately breaking things, wrong ports, bad health checks, missing environment variables, to see what failures look like. Third, real work: I paired with a platform engineer for two sessions and wrote the configs for our first real service, with him reviewing.

By the end of the second week I'd migrated two services and written a short internal guide for my team with the commands and common errors. The rest of the team used it to migrate the remaining services, and the migration finished about a month ahead of the new estimate.

What I learned about learning: set a clear goal for "good enough", and get to real work quickly. The deliberate breaking was the most useful part.

Fresher version: I recently learned SQL window functions for a placement test. I practised on a public dataset until I could write running totals and rankings without looking anything up, then used them to add a "top absentees" report to our college attendance app.

Make it yours: choose something relevant to the role and show your method, not just the topic. Likely follow-up: "What's something you're learning right now?"

IntermediateWhat's something you're proud of that isn't on your resume?

What they're testing: depth of character and the things you value, beyond shipped features.

Mentoring. Last year I mentored two interns on our team, and it doesn't show up anywhere on my resume, but I think it's some of my best work.

One of them was struggling specifically with code reviews. He'd get a PR back with fifteen comments and go quiet for a day, and then make exactly the changes asked without understanding why. His PRs were getting bigger and slower because he was nervous about sending them.

I tried a few things. I started doing some of his reviews live over a call instead of in writing, so he could ask "why" in the moment and hear the tone, which is hard to read in comments. I asked him to open smaller PRs, one idea each, so feedback was easier to take in. And I had him review my PRs, which surprised him. It showed him that reviews are a normal conversation, not a grade, and he found a real bug in one of mine, which helped his confidence a lot.

By the end of his internship, his PRs were half the size, his review cycles went from about three days to under one, and he got a return offer. He now reviews other people's code with a lot of care.

The lesson for me was that most "skill" problems are partly confidence problems.

Make it yours: good options are mentoring, internal docs or talks, open-source contributions or community work. Pick one that shows a trait the role needs. Likely follow-up: "What did you do with the other intern, who wasn't struggling?"

IntermediateTell me about a project that didn't go as planned.

What they're testing: honesty, ownership, and whether you have ready stories that aren't all wins.

The search feature I worked on last year. I estimated it at one sprint and it took three.

The task was to add better product search to the storefront: typo tolerance, filters and results that handled products with variants, like a shirt in five sizes. I'd built similar things before, so I estimated two weeks with some confidence.

The problems came in the parts I hadn't looked at closely. The indexing for variants was much more complicated than I'd assumed, because some clients had products with hundreds of variants and our first approach made the index huge. And the requirements for how filters should combine weren't clear, so we went back and forth with design twice.

My biggest mistake wasn't the estimate itself, it was that I didn't flag the risk early. By the middle of sprint one I knew it was in trouble, but I thought I could catch up. The PM found out at the sprint review, which meant a marketing email had to be moved.

I apologised to the PM directly, re-planned with her into two smaller releases, and shipped basic search with filters at the end of sprint two and the variant handling in sprint three. Afterwards I proposed spikes for unknowns and range estimates, which the team adopted.

The lesson I carry is: bad news gets worse with age. I now flag risk the day I see it.

Make it yours: every story bank needs two or three "didn't go well" stories. Keep the blame on yourself, and spend most of the time on recovery and what changed. Likely follow-up: "How did the PM react, and how is that relationship now?"

IntermediateTell me about a time you had to deliver something you weren't fully happy with.

What they're testing: pragmatism, and whether you can trade perfection for delivery while managing the debt honestly.

That would be our internal reporting tool. A teammate who'd been building it left the company mid-project, and it was already about a month behind. Finance and ops were waiting on it to replace a set of manual spreadsheets.

When I took it over, the original plan had around fifteen reports, custom date filters, charts and scheduled email exports. Realistically, finishing all of it would have taken another two months. I sat down with the PM and we went through each report with the people who'd actually use it. It turned out five reports covered most of what finance needed weekly.

So I shipped those five with simple date filters and CSV export in three weeks. I wasn't happy with parts of it. The UI was plain, there were no charts, and I'd kept some of my predecessor's code that I would have structured differently. But I was deliberate about it. I wrote down every shortcut in a short tech-debt doc, with what I'd change and why, and got the PM to agree that two of the most important items would go into the next quarter's plan.

Finance stopped using the spreadsheets within a month, and the two follow-up items did get done.

What I took away: "good enough" is fine as long as you're honest about it and write it down.

Make it yours: show that the trade-off was a conscious decision made with stakeholders, and that you tracked the debt. Likely follow-up: "How did you decide which shortcuts were acceptable and which weren't?"

AdvancedGive me a different example. Not the checkout project this time.

What they're testing: whether you have a real story bank or one polished story you reuse for everything.

Sure. If the question is still about impact, let me use the bulk-import escalation instead.

A B2B client's bulk product imports kept failing with a useless error, and support had been stuck on it for two weeks. I joined a call with the client, watched them import a file, and saw that their spreadsheets had several kinds of problems, missing SKUs, currency symbols in prices and mixed encodings, while our importer stopped at the first bad row without saying why.

I built a pre-import validation report that checks every row and gives a downloadable list of problems with plain-English fixes, and let good rows import while bad ones wait. It took about a week. Their next import worked the first time, and import-related support tickets across all customers dropped by about 70% the next month.

It's a different kind of impact from checkout. That one was measured in milliseconds and conversions. This one was about removing a lot of frustration for a customer and our support team.

If you'd like, I also have an example about taking over our delayed reporting tool, which is more about scoping and delivery.

Fresher version: If they've heard your internship story, switch to the hackathon or college project. For example: "Sure, a different one: at a 24-hour hackathon, I built the search and front end for a lecture-notes tool, fixed a rate-limit problem at hour 16 with caching, and we came 2nd."

Make it yours: build a grid of 8–10 stories against common themes (impact, conflict, failure, leadership, learning, pressure) so each theme has at least two options. Likely follow-up: "Of all your projects, which one taught you the most?"

BasicTell me about yourself. (30-second version)

What they're testing: whether you can summarise who you are and why you're here, fast, for a recruiter screen or a packed panel.

Sure. I'm a full-stack developer with about three years at Brightcart, an e-commerce SaaS company. I work across React, Node.js and Java with Spring, on top of Postgres.

The work I'm best known for there is performance and reliability. I led the project that took our checkout page from a 4.2 second p95 load time down to 1.6 seconds, which lifted conversions by about 6%, and I owned the fix and postmortem for a payment bug that led us to add idempotency across our webhooks.

I'm now looking for a role where I can work on a larger product with more scale, which is why this position caught my eye.

That's the short version. Happy to go deeper into any of it.

Fresher version: I'm a final-year B.Tech Computer Science student. I did a two-month internship at a startup where I built an admin dashboard in React that their ops team uses daily. I also led the front end of our college attendance app and came 2nd at a 24-hour hackathon. I'm looking for a full-stack role where I can learn fast from a strong team.

Make it yours: the structure is present (role and stack), proof (one or two wins with numbers), future (why this role). Keep it to about 80 words. Likely follow-up: "Tell me more about the checkout project."

BasicTell me about yourself. (90-second version)

What they're testing: the standard opener; they want a clear story that gives them hooks for follow-up questions.

I'm a full-stack developer, and for the last three years I've been at Brightcart, a mid-size company that sells an e-commerce platform to online stores. My day-to-day stack is React on the front end, Node.js and Java with Spring on the back end, and Postgres.

A couple of things I've done there that I think say the most about me. I led our checkout performance project: I measured where the time was going, split our bundle, fixed images and third-party scripts, and got p95 load time from 4.2 seconds to 1.6, which lifted conversions by about 6%. I also took over an internal reporting tool that was behind after a teammate left, re-scoped it with the PM and shipped the core in three weeks.

First, I take ten seconds to structure my thoughts before speaking. If the topic is "Should AI replace human jobs?", I note two or three angles: economic, social and ethical. That gives me more to say than people who jump in with one point.

Second, I try to speak early, in the first two or three turns, but only with a real point. Opening well, by defining the topic or suggesting a structure, is valued. But if I can't open strongly, a good point in the middle is better than a weak opening.

Third, I add facts or examples. A number or a real-world example, like the adoption of AI tools in customer support, gives a point weight.

Fourth, I listen and build on others. Saying "Building on what Rahul said about jobs, I think the bigger question is retraining" shows listening and teamwork, which evaluators look for.

Fifth, if nobody has concluded, I summarise at the end, fairly, including views I didn't agree with. That's often what evaluators remember.

And I keep my body language calm: I look at the group, not the evaluators.

Fresher version: Practise with friends on current topics like digital payments or remote work, with a timer, and ask them to give you feedback on how often you interrupted.

Make it yours: prepare structures for common GD topic types (tech, economy, social, abstract). Likely follow-up: in the GD, "Can you summarise the discussion?"

AdvancedIn a group discussion, what do you do if someone dominates or the group gets chaotic?

What they're testing: (as a prep question) whether you can stay composed, bring order and get heard without aggression.

The key is not to compete by being louder. Evaluators notice who restores order, not who wins the shouting.

If someone dominates, I wait for them to pause, acknowledge their point briefly and then move forward: "That's a good point about cost. I'd like to add another angle." It gets me in without attacking them.

If the group becomes chaotic, with several people talking at once, I suggest a structure: "We've got three views here. Maybe we can take each one for a minute?" That's a leadership move that doesn't need authority.

If a quiet participant tries to speak and gets cut off, I bring them in: "I think Priya was about to say something." It shows teamwork, and evaluators remember it.

If I strongly disagree with someone, I disagree with the point, not the person, and give a reason: "I see it differently, because the data on that suggests the opposite."

And if I'm getting no chance at all, I use short, clear points rather than waiting for a long gap that never comes.

I faced this in a campus GD where one person kept talking over everyone. I suggested going around the table once for final points, and then summarised. I was shortlisted, and the feedback mentioned the summary.

Make it yours: practise one polite interruption phrase and one "bring someone in" phrase. Likely follow-up: in a personal interview after the GD, "How do you think the discussion went?"

BasicWhat are the most common mistakes in behavioural answers?

What they're testing: (as a prep question) whether you know the patterns that sink otherwise strong candidates.

There are six I see most often, and I've made a few of them myself.

First, saying "we" throughout. The interviewer can't tell what I did. When I first practised my checkout story, I said "we" everywhere, even though I did the profiling myself. Now I say "I found" and "I proposed", and use "we" only for genuine team results.

Second, long setups. Spending two minutes on the Situation leaves no time for the Action, which is what's actually scored.

Third, no result, or a vague one like "it went well". I try to include a number where it's honest, like 4.2 to 1.6 seconds, or 38 customers refunded in 24 hours.

Fourth, a hypothetical instead of a story. "I would normally…" isn't an answer to "Tell me about a time…".

Fifth, fake weaknesses and failures, like "I work too hard". Interviewers have heard them all.

Sixth, blaming others, even subtly. Every conflict story where the other person was simply wrong is a red flag.
